## November 1

# Austremonius of Clermont

*Apostle of the Auvergne, first bishop of Clermont, traditionally fl. c. 250*

He was one of the seven bishops the Roman church sent into Gaul in the middle of the third century — Saturninus to Toulouse, Trophimus to Arles, Paul to Narbonne, Martial to Limoges, Gatian to Tours, Denis to Paris, and Austremonius (in the Auvergnat speech that grew up around his shrine, *Stremoine*) to the great volcanic plateau in the heart of Roman Gaul where the Arverni had once held against Caesar. He arrived at *Augustonemetum* — the Roman name for what would become Clermont — perhaps around 250, and walked into a city of perhaps thirty thousand inhabitants where the Christian community he found was, at most, a handful.

He built a small chapel inside the city walls, the *acta* preserve, *non palam sed occulte* — not openly, but in secret. He preached. He baptized. He ordained, from the small group of converts who collected around him, the first generation of Auvergnat presbyters and deacons. The community grew. Within a few years he was running the catechumenate openly; within a generation the diocese he had founded had spread up the Allier and out across the high pasturelands of the central massif. He was perhaps the first martyr of the see — the older *acta* have him beheaded by the local pagans, having converted the children of a leading family and aroused the father to fury — though whether his life ended in martyrdom or in peaceful old age the Bollandists declined to settle.

The cult was immediate and unbroken. Gregory of Tours — who grew up in the Auvergne in the sixth century and who knew the Clermont cathedral inside and out — preserves the eucharistic miracle the *acta* report at length: a peasant who had been given consecrated bread by Austremonius after Mass, having stowed it in his satchel for the road home, was caught by a sudden flood at the crossing of the Allier and would have drowned but for the satchel, which floated him to shore as if it were a boat. Gregory adds that this is why, in the Auvergne in his day, the country people kept the practice of eating the *eulogia* — the blessed bread distributed at the end of Sunday Mass — before any other food on the Lord's day.

He was buried at first inside the city walls and later translated to the great abbey of Issoire, twenty miles south of Clermont, where his bones still lie. The Clermont he founded crowned its kings of France there and held its first crusade-council there in 1095 and rebuilt its black-stone cathedral over his crypt in the thirteenth century. The first bishop of the chair has been buried for seventeen hundred years. The chair has not been empty since.
