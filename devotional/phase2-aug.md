## August 1

# The Holy Maccabean Mother and Her Seven Sons

*With Eleazar the elder; martyrs at Antioch, c. 166 B.C.*

They are the only saints of the Old Testament whom the Western church has kept with a feast and a basilica from the earliest centuries. The AASS Augusti I opens the month with their entry — *DE SS. MARTYRIBUS ELEAZARO SENE, SEPTEM FRATRIBUS MACHABÆIS, ET HORUM MATRE* — and notes the unusual fact: of all the prophets, kings, and confessors of the Hebrew scriptures, of all who had laid down their lives for the Law from righteous Abel to Zechariah, these alone the Catholic Church drew into the cycle of the Christian martyrs, ahead of nearly every Christian saint by date of death.

They had been arrested at Antioch in 166 B.C., under Antiochus IV, who had set himself to extinguish the religion of Israel by force. The old priest Eleazar, ninety years old, the AASS calls *fratrum institutor* — the instructor of the family — was brought first. He was told to eat swine's flesh in public, simply to appear to eat it; on the table sat a piece of permitted meat his apostate colleagues had quietly substituted for the unclean. He refused even the appearance of compromise. *It is unbecoming to our age,* he said, *that we should dissemble; whereby many young persons might think the aged Eleazar to have gone over to the manners of the heathen.* He was scourged to death.

The mother, whom the Greek Synaxaria call Solomonis and whom Latin tradition leaves unnamed, was brought next with her seven sons. The boys were tortured one at a time, in the order of their birth, in their mother's sight. The eldest, having his tongue cut out, hands and feet cut off, then fried alive in a great pan, encouraged his brothers in the dialect they spoke at home, and the king did not understand. The mother spoke to each in turn in the same Hebrew her sons answered her in. *I cannot tell how thou wast formed in my womb,* she said to the youngest, *but the Creator of the world, that formed the generation of man, will restore to you again the breath and the life.* The youngest died last; the mother died with him.

The basilica at Antioch over their graves was already a pilgrimage site in the fourth century, Augustine attests in *Sermon* 300. Their relics travelled west to Rome — the church of San Pietro in Vincoli still holds a portion. The Christian church recognized in them a martyrdom by anticipation: they had died for the same single thing the apostles would die for two centuries later — for the Law of the Lord, which is also Christ Jesus, the *Logos* of every commandment.

> *"I cannot tell how thou wast formed in my womb; but the Creator of the world, that formed the generation of man, will restore to you again the breath and the life."* — the mother to the youngest son, *2 Maccabees* 7:22-23

## August 2

# Stephen I, Pope

*Roman pontiff and martyr, d. 257*

He was a Roman of old citizen stock — *antiqua Roma oriundus* — and rose through the Roman clergy under Pope Lucius I. He was elected to the chair of Peter in May of 254, in the second year of Valerian, in the breathing-space between the Decian persecution and the one that would kill him. He governed perhaps three years and three months. He died on the second of August, 257, by the consistent witness of every ancient martyrology AASS prints — Hieronymian, Adonian, Usuardine, Bedan — and of the Greek Synaxaria, in which he is *hieromartyr*.

He governed in the middle of the longest inner-Church controversy of the third century. Cyprian of Carthage and the African church, with the bishops of Asia behind him, held that converts baptized by heretics outside the visible Church must be re-baptized on entering Catholic communion. Stephen, on the inherited Roman teaching, held that the baptism of Christ done in the Name of the Trinity was the baptism of Christ wherever administered, and that the convert needed only the laying-on of hands. He refused to use the African formula; he asked, simply, that *nothing be innovated except what has been handed down* — *Nihil innovetur nisi quod traditum est*. The phrase, drawn from a letter Cyprian preserved, became one of the small permanent sentences of the Roman magisterium. The dispute did not break communion. Both bishops died in the persecution of 257 before either had moved.

The narrative of his death the AASS keeps from the *Menologium* of Basil. Driven into hiding in his own house at Rome by the renewal of the persecution under Valerian, he continued the sacraments by night: baptizing pagans, consecrating deacons and presbyters, taking the prayers of the threatened community. He had healed the daughter of a tribune named Nemesius; through her the father was baptized, and through him many of his household, several of whom went on to die in tortures of various kinds. Stephen buried their bodies with his own hands. When at last the soldiers came for him, they found him at the altar — *ante altare intrepidus et immobilis cœpta mysteria perficiens*, fearless and unmoved, finishing the mysteries he had begun. They beheaded him in his own chair on the second of August.

Pope Paul I translated his body in 761 to the church of San Silvestro in Capite, where it lies still.

> *"Nothing should be innovated, except what has been handed down."* — Stephen, in his letter against re-baptism, preserved by Cyprian

## August 3

# Lydia the Purpurarian

*Convert of Paul, first Christian of Europe, c. 50*

The AASS Augusti I opens her entry with a gentle puzzlement — *Mirum sane est* — that neither the Western nor the Eastern church kept the memory of so notable a matron in any of the ancient liturgical books, until the late-modern *Roman Martyrology* at last inserted her on the third of August: *Philippis in Macedonia, sanctæ Lydiæ purpurariæ; quæ, prædicante ibidem sancto Paulo Apostolo, prima omnium credidit Evangelio.* The first to believe the Gospel — in Europe.

The whole Vita is in the sixteenth chapter of the Acts of the Apostles. She came originally from Thyatira in the province of Lydia in Asia Minor — a Greek city whose famous trade was the dyeing of cloth in deep-red shellfish purple — and had moved west to Philippi, the great Macedonian colony eight Roman miles from the Aegean coast, where she had set up as a dealer in purple cloth. The Greek of Luke calls her by the title of her trade: *πορφυρόπωλις*. The purple-trade was a regulated imperial monopoly, *neque concessa cuilibet* — not granted to just anyone — which implies that Lydia was a woman of property and standing.

She was already a god-fearer — *colens Deum* — a Gentile sympathizer with the Jewish faith, but not a full proselyte. Philippi had no synagogue; the city lacked the ten adult Jewish men the *minyan* requires. Instead, the small community met on the Sabbath at a *προσευχή* — a prayer-house — on the riverbank outside the western gate. Paul, Silas, Timothy, and Luke had landed at Neapolis a few days before, walked the seven miles inland to Philippi, kept the Sabbath, and went down to the river to find the praying community. They sat with the women who had gathered there. *And a certain woman named Lydia, a seller of purple of the city of Thyatira, one that worshipped God, heard us; whose heart the Lord opened, that she attended unto the things which were spoken of Paul.*

She was baptized that day with her household. She compelled — the Greek word is firm: *παρεβιάσατο* — Paul and his companions to stay at her house. They stayed many days. The first house-church in Europe assembled in her front room. When Paul was at last freed from the city's prison, he returned to her house to say goodbye before he left. Her name does not appear in his letter to the Philippians, written a decade later from Roman imprisonment — the AASS infers from this that she had died before 61, perhaps not long after Paul's departure. The Philippian church the letter so warmly addresses had begun in her house. *Mirum* indeed, the AASS adds, that the church forgot her memory for so long.

> *"And a certain woman named Lydia, a seller of purple, whose heart the Lord opened — and she was baptized, and her household."* — *Acts* 16:14-15

## August 4

# Dominic Guzmán

*Founder of the Order of Preachers, c. 1170–1221*

The AASS Augusti II opens its substantial entry with the observation that Dominic is one of the best-documented saints of the high middle ages. Nine sworn witnesses of his life gave deposition before the canonization tribunal under oath; three hundred more were taken in Toulouse alone. His immediate successor as Master General, Jordan of Saxony, wrote a brief Vita while a generation of brothers who had walked with the founder were still alive to correct him.

He was born about 1170 at the hamlet of Caleruega in Old Castile, the youngest son of Felix of Guzmán and his wife Jane of Aza. He studied at Palencia, was ordained a canon regular at the cathedral chapter of Osma, and became sub-prior in his early thirties. In 1203 his bishop Diego of Acebes took him as travel-companion on a diplomatic embassy to Denmark; the road back, in 1206, passed through Languedoc, where the bishop and his canon found the Albigensian heresy entrenched and the Cistercian counter-preachers — who arrived in episcopal trains, with horses and servants — being ignored. Diego said publicly that this would not work. The two of them stopped at Prouille that summer, dismissed their retinue, and walked the rest of the year on foot, preaching village to village in poverty, sleeping on the floor of whatever house took them in.

Diego died in 1207. Dominic stayed. He founded the small women's community at Prouille that year — convert women of the Cathar gentry — and a brotherhood of preacher-priests gathered around him as the years went on. He had asked Pope Innocent III for confirmation at the Lateran Council of 1215 and been told to take an existing rule; he chose the Rule of Saint Augustine, added his own *Constitutions*, and got confirmation under Honorius III in late 1216. The new *Ordo Prædicatorum* — the first religious order whose primary work was preaching, and the first whose mendicancy was structural rather than personal — spread astonishingly fast: by 1221 it had perhaps three hundred houses across Europe.

He governed from Bologna in his last years. He had walked, by Jordan's count, perhaps fifteen thousand miles between 1207 and 1221. He died at Bologna on the sixth of August, 1221, in a borrowed bed because he refused to die in one of his own brothers'. The AASS keeps his feast on August 4 — the older Western liturgical date, fixed before the modern Roman calendar moved him to August 8. Canonized 1234.

> *"You will gain more by gentle prayer and a contrite heart than by argument."* — Dominic, to his brothers preaching in Languedoc, recorded by Jordan of Saxony

## August 5

# Oswald of Northumbria

*King and martyr, c. 604–642*

He was the second son of Aethelfrith, king of Northumbria, and Acha — sister of King Edwin who would later rule Northumbria and rival the father. His father died in battle when Oswald was about thirteen, and Edwin drove the sons of Aethelfrith into exile. Oswald and his elder brother Eanfrid and the younger Oswiu spent the next seventeen years among the *Scotti sive Picti* of what is now the western coast of Scotland. They were catechized and baptized in exile by the Iona monks who had come down from Columba's foundation a generation before. Oswald, the AASS records, passed his teenage years between the rudiments of the faith and the games of war. He acquired both.

Edwin was killed at Hatfield Chase in 633 by Cadwallon, the British king of Gwynedd, in alliance with Penda the pagan king of Mercia. The kingdom broke into its two parts. Oswald's brother Eanfrid took Bernicia, apostatized, and was killed within a year by Cadwallon. The way was clear. Oswald led the small force of Northumbrian exiles south against Cadwallon at a place the chroniclers call *Denisesburn* — modern Heavenfield, by Hadrian's Wall. The Iona Vita of Columba, written by Adamnan a generation later, records the vision: Columba came to the king the night before the battle, where he slept in his pavilion on his cushion, and told him to go forward. He set up a wooden cross by his own hand on the field at dawn, before the men were drawn up, while the soldiers heaped earth around its base. The army knelt around it. They won.

He governed Northumbria for eight years. He sent at once to Iona — the source of his own catechesis — for a bishop, and the abbot sent him Aidan, who established the see of Lindisfarne with Oswald's gift of land. The king translated for Aidan in the early years, because Aidan had not yet learned enough English; *interprete utebatur ipso rege* — the king served as interpreter. He gave food in royal feasts to the poor at the gate; Bede says he once broke a silver dish at table and ordered the pieces distributed among them.

He was killed at the Battle of Maserfield — modern Oswestry, in Shropshire — in 642, fighting again against Penda of Mercia. He was thirty-eight. Penda had his head and his arms cut off and displayed on stakes. His brother Oswiu recovered the relics: the body to Bardney, the head to Lindisfarne with Aidan, the arms to Bamburgh — and the cult began the same year.

> *"Let us all kneel, and pray together to the Almighty, the living and true God, in His mercy to defend us from the proud and fierce enemy."* — Oswald, before Heavenfield, recorded by Bede HE III.2

## August 6

# Justus and Pastor

*Boy-martyrs of Toledo, the schoolboys of the persecution, d. 304*

They were brothers — Justus was nine, Pastor seven, the older *acta* preserve — schoolboys of the small Roman frontier-town of *Complutum* on the Henares river, the place the Spanish would later call Alcalá de Henares thirty miles east of Madrid. Their parents were of the local Hispano-Roman Christian gentry; the boys attended the public *ludus litterarius* of the town, where the magister taught Latin grammar and the elements of rhetoric to the children of the better families. The persecution of Diocletian had reached Hispania in 304. The local *præses* was Dacian, a methodical persecutor whom the Spanish hagiographic tradition would associate with most of the great Iberian martyrs of the period. He came up the road from Toledo to Complutum in the late summer of 304 with the standing edict to require sacrifice from every household.

The boys were in the schoolroom when the news came that Dacian was holding tribunal in the town forum. They put down their wax tablets, walked out of the school against the master's protests, and ran the four blocks to the forum. They presented themselves before the prefect, hand in hand. *We are Christians,* they said. *We will not sacrifice to your gods. We have come to die for Christ, who is the only God and the only true Lord.* Dacian was momentarily speechless. He tried to talk them out of it: he asked their ages, he reminded them of their parents, he offered them sweets. The boys held. Dacian, finally enraged, ordered them taken out beyond the city walls, scourged, and beheaded on the spot. The execution was carried out at a place the local Christians afterward called the *Campo Laudable* — the Praiseworthy Field — half a mile north of the town.

The Christian community recovered the small bodies that night and buried them in a single grave outside the walls. The cult began at once. By the late fourth century, Paulinus of Nola preserves, the burial spot was already a major pilgrimage site of the Iberian peninsula — Paulinus himself buried his infant son Celsus near it, *propinquis conjunctum tumuli foedere Martyribus,* joined to the nearby martyrs by the bond of the tomb. The relics were rediscovered around 388 by Bishop Asturius of Toledo at the prompting of a vision; he stayed at Complutum to guard them, and the basilica that grew over the graves became the cathedral of Complutum and is the seed of the present Cathedral-Magistral of Alcalá.

The two boys are the patrons of grammar schools across Spain and Latin America — every elementary school in the old Spanish-speaking world has, somewhere on its walls, a small image of two children with a palm branch and a scroll. The schoolboys who walked out of the schoolroom to die became the patrons of the schoolroom they had walked out of.

> *"We are Christians. We have come to die for Christ."* — Justus and Pastor, before Dacian at Complutum

## August 7

# Victricius of Rouen

*Soldier-confessor, archbishop, apostle of the Morini, c. 340–417*

He had been a soldier under Julian the Apostate. The AASS Augusti II preserves the key narrative from the two letters of Paulinus of Nola — Letters 18 and 37 — that are nearly the only contemporary source for his life. He was a young Christian in the army, perhaps twenty, when at a military council on the day the standards were blessed for a campaign, he stepped forward, removed the military belt of his rank, threw the arms down at the feet of his tribune, and said he would not fight any longer under the apostate emperor. The tribune flew into a fury. They beat him with rods and shattered them across his back. They threw him into a prison cell whose floor had been spread with fragments of broken pottery so he could not lie down. The torturer's hands, the AASS Vita reports, were struck with sudden inability; he could not strike the next blow. Victricius was sentenced to death. The executioner sent to do the work was struck blind on the way. Victricius walked free.

He spent the next years in northern Gaul learning the language of the Belgic tribes — the Morini, the Nervii, the people of the long Boulogne coastal road. He became a priest, then in the early 380s archbishop of Rouen — eighth in the metropolitan see, and a successor in spirit to Martin of Tours, who at Vienne in 389 met both Victricius and the young Paulinus of Nola at the same gathering. Victricius spent the next twenty-eight years preaching across the Morini country between Rouen and the Channel coast, founding the first monasteries in those barbarian regions. He brought to the Frankish frontier the same austere monasticism Martin had established at Marmoutier.

The most consequential thing he wrote was occasioned by a gift from Ambrose of Milan. Ambrose sent him a small chest of relics — from the martyrs of Bologna, perhaps from Gervase and Protase whose bodies Ambrose had discovered in 386 — and Victricius's procession of those relics through Rouen in 396 became the occasion of his sermon *De Laude Sanctorum*, the *Praise of the Saints*. It is the earliest patristic treatise on the proper place of the cult of relics: the bodies of the martyrs, Victricius preached, are not magical objects but the same flesh that Christ took, sanctified by the same Spirit that raised him, awaiting the same resurrection that we await. The treatise was read across the West for the next millennium.

He was honored by Pope Innocent I with apostolic letters answering his canonical questions and commending his learning. He died at Rouen in peace, perhaps 417, in his late seventies. Ordericus Vitalis wrote his epitaph six centuries later: *Victricius victor, vitiorum fortis et ultor* — Victricius the victor, strong avenger of vices, who steeped the Lord's Church in his nourishing commandments.

> *"What we venerate in the relics is not the bones, but the Christ in whose Body those bones already share."* — Victricius, *De Laude Sanctorum*, on the procession at Rouen

## August 8

# Aemilianus of Cyzicus

*Bishop and iconodule confessor of the Hellespont, d. c. 815–820*

He was archbishop of Cyzicus — the great fortified peninsula-city on the southern shore of the Sea of Marmara, where the Hellespont opens into the Propontis — at the moment the Byzantine iconoclast crisis broke open for the second time. The AASS Augusti II preserves him on the eighth of August on the testimony of the Greek Synaxaria and the Menaia, with a brief Latin notice taken into the *Roman Martyrology*: *Cyzici in Hellesponto sancti Æmiliani episcopi, qui pro sacrarum imaginum cultu a Leone imperatore multa passus, demum in exsilio vitam finivit.* This is Leo V the Armenian, who reigned 813 to 820 — the second wave of iconoclasm, not the first.

The Patriarch Nicephorus of Constantinople — the orthodox iconodule who had been raised to the see in 806 — called Aemilianus and the other firm iconodule bishops of the empire to gather at the imperial capital in late 814, when the new emperor's intentions had become clear. Leo had decided to revive Constantine V's policy and to suppress the veneration of icons throughout his dominions. He summoned the bishops to a hearing in the palace, expecting either compliance or a forum he could control. Aemilianus answered him with the sentence the AASS prints in the original Greek of Theostericus's near-contemporary Acts:

*If the question be ecclesiastical, Emperor, as thou hast said, let it be inquired in the church, as the custom is. For from the beginning ecclesiastical questions have been inquired into in churches, not in royal palaces.*

He would not compromise. He stayed in the city the days of the hearing under guard, was sent into custody, beaten, and eventually exiled to a remote place whose name the AASS does not preserve. He died there sometime between 815 and 820, in privations the Greek elogium calls *millenis ærumnis* — countless tribulations. He was succeeded at Cyzicus by an iconoclast appointed from the palace, and the second wave of iconoclasm raged through the empire for another twenty-eight years until the empress Theodora, regent for the boy Michael III, restored the icons in 843 — a restoration sealed at last on the first Sunday of Orthodoxy in March of that year.

> *"Ecclesiastical questions have been inquired into in churches, not in royal palaces."* — Aemilianus, to Leo the Armenian, AASS Aug 8

## August 9

# Numidicus of Carthage

*Confessor-presbyter under Decius, c. 250*

The AASS Augusti II preserves him under the single source from which the whole entry is built — Cyprian of Carthage's letter to his clergy and people, Epistle 35, written perhaps in the spring of 251 while Cyprian was still in his year of hiding from the Decian persecution.

Numidicus had been, before the persecution, simply a layman of the African church — perhaps a deacon, perhaps in minor orders; the sources do not specify. When the Decian edict reached Africa in the summer of 250 and Christians of every rank were summoned to sacrifice, a substantial group of Carthaginian believers gathered around him in a place the *Acta* do not name and refused. The proconsul's officers herded the group together, gave them the standard last chance, then heaped stones around them and set the pile on fire. Numidicus stood among them. His own wife stood at his side. Cyprian's Latin keeps both possibilities open of what he saw next: *uxorem adhærentem lateri suo concrematam simul cum ceteris* — his wife, clinging to his side, burned with the rest. Or, as Cyprian gently adds in the same sentence, *conservatam magis dixerim* — preserved, rather, I should say. The wife, the bishop hopes, was preserved by God in the burning. The text leaves us between the two readings.

Numidicus himself was half-burned — *semiustulatus et lapidibus obrutus*, half-scorched and crushed under the stones. He was left for dead with the pile when the officers withdrew. His daughter — Cyprian does not name her — came that evening with the sorrowful piety of which the bishop writes, *sollicito pietatis obsequio*, searching for her father's body to bury it properly. She moved the stones. She found him still breathing. She brought him home and nursed him back. He had not chosen to live; he had wanted to go with the others; *remansit invitus*, Cyprian says, he remained behind against his will.

Cyprian ordained him presbyter of the Carthaginian church the following year, and gave the reason in a single sentence: *to adorn with glorious priests the priestly company of Carthage which, by the apostasy of some, had been left desolate.* The Decian persecution had broken the African clergy; the bishop was rebuilding from those whom the persecution had not broken. Numidicus served as presbyter at Carthage for the rest of his life, the only fragment of which we have is the address Cyprian gives him in Letter 40 — *gloria divinæ dignationis illustres*, glorious by the honor of divine consideration.

> *"He sent before him to martyrdom a great number, slain by stones and by flames; and he saw his own wife, clinging to his side, burned with them."* — Cyprian, *Epistle* 35, on Numidicus

## August 10

# Lawrence of Rome

*Archdeacon and martyr, d. 258*

He was a Spaniard, the AASS Augusti II preserves the tradition — from Huesca in Aragon, born to Christian parents named Orentius and Patientia. He went to Rome young, was made acolyte and then deacon under Sixtus II, and at the time of the Valerian persecution of 258 served as the *archidiaconus* — the senior of the seven deacons of the Roman church, the man with charge of the church's plate, the church's archives, and the church's relief-rolls of the Roman poor.

Sixtus was arrested on August 6 in the catacomb of Praetextatus while keeping the Eucharist with his deacons. Lawrence, the AASS records from the contemporary Acts and the *Peristephanon* of Prudentius, ran after the soldiers as Sixtus was being led away and wept at his bishop's feet. *Whither goest thou, Father, without thy son? Whither, Priest, without thy deacon? Thou hast never offered the sacrifice without thy minister.* Sixtus answered him with the line the Roman office still keeps: *I am not leaving thee, son. After three days thou shalt follow.* Sixtus and the four deacons with him were beheaded that same day.

The Prefect of the city had Lawrence brought in within hours. The empire had decided to fund the Persian war by confiscating church property; the Prefect demanded the treasury of the Roman church. Lawrence asked for three days to gather it. He went out into the city and emptied the deacon's coffers among the poor of all twenty regions — the blind men of the *titulus Pudentis*, the lame of the *titulus Praxedis*, the widows of the Trastevere, the orphans of the Vatican fields, every name on the relief-roll he kept. On the third day he gathered them on the steps of the prefect's tribunal and presented them with the line that the *Acta* preserve as their core: *These are the treasures of the Church.*

He was tortured. The Prefect ordered him roasted on an iron gridiron over slow coals — a long death, designed to be visible. The tradition preserved by Ambrose, Augustine, Leo the Great, and Prudentius records that after some hours Lawrence raised his head and said, with the levity he had kept his whole life: *Assum est; versa et manduca* — *It is roasted on this side; turn me over and eat.* Augustine, who quotes the line in two separate sermons on Lawrence's feast, comments that it was not bravado but charity: the deacon was making it easier for his executioners to finish what they had to finish.

He died on the night of August 10, 258. They buried him on the Via Tiburtina. Constantine in the next century built the great basilica of San Lorenzo fuori le Mura over the spot, where his bones still lie.

> *"These are the treasures of the Church."* — Lawrence to the Prefect of Rome, the *Acta* preserved by Ambrose and Prudentius

## August 11

# Alexander the Charcoal-burner

*Bishop of Comana, martyr, third century*

The AASS Augusti II tells the whole story from one source: Gregory of Nyssa's *Oration on the Life of Gregory Thaumaturgus*. The setting is the city of Comana in Pontic Polemoniac — a small town on the river Iris in the territory of Neocaesarea, where Gregory the Wonderworker was bishop in the middle decades of the third century. The Christian community at Comana, which had grown rapidly under his influence, sent a delegation begging him to come and consecrate a bishop for them. He came. He spent some days teaching among them. When the moment came to choose, the citizens could not agree.

They put forward many candidates. Gregory passed over each in turn, asking the citizens whether they had not yet considered a man more suitable. At last someone, in evident sarcasm, said: well, what about Alexander the coal-burner? — and pointed across the open square at the black-faced figure at his cart, sooty from the kilns, dressed in rags, looking, the witty citizen said, more fit to be ridiculed than to be ordained. The crowd laughed. Gregory did not. He asked that the man be brought forward.

Alexander came. He had, Gregory of Nyssa says with care, the body of a young man at the flower of his strength, but the body was beneath a covering of soot and coarse cloth designed precisely to draw no attention. The Wonderworker took him aside. They talked. Alexander, the *Vita* preserves, was no peasant. He was a philosopher of substantial education and noble family who had chosen extreme poverty and disguise as a discipline — both to keep his body in hard labor for the sake of virtue, and to hide the physical beauty he believed dangerous to his chastity, and to make a livelihood whose every coin could be given as alms to the poor. *Non urgente paupertate ad ejusmodi vitæ genus devenerat:* he had not been driven to this kind of life by need. He had chosen it. He had been hiding under the bushel, and Gregory had come to put him on the candlestick.

Alexander was bathed, given a fresh tunic, examined for orthodoxy, and consecrated bishop of Comana the same week. He served the see for the rest of his life — perhaps twenty years — and is remembered chiefly for two things in Gregory of Nyssa's narrative. His sermons drew crowds from neighboring cities; the rusticated philosopher who had hidden his learning gave it back, when given the office, in a Greek so clear that even illiterate hearers understood. And he was killed in the persecution that fell on Pontus in the third century — *per ignem consummato martyrio*, his course completed by fire. The man who had blackened his face with the smoke of the kilns was at last consumed by a fire he had not lit.

> *"Small in his own eyes, vile and despised in the world, but truly great before God."* — Gregory of Nyssa, on Alexander

## August 12

# Clare of Assisi

*First disciple of Francis, founder of the Poor Ladies, c. 1193–1253*

The AASS Augusti II opens with the title that the medieval Office of Clare keeps: *prima Sancti Francisci discipula* — first disciple of Saint Francis. She was born about 1193 at Assisi, of the noble Offreduccio family, the elder daughter of a household that expected her to marry well and was already negotiating the contract when she heard Francis preach during the Lenten season of 1211. She was eighteen. She slipped out of her father's house through a small side gate the family later kept blocked up — *Porta della Morte*, the death-gate, the one used for funerals — on the evening of Palm Sunday, 1212, walked the mile down the hill to the chapel of the Portiuncula, and presented herself to Francis. He cut her long hair himself, in front of the small Franciscan community gathered around the altar, and clothed her in the rough wool habit of the *Friars Minor*.

Her family tried for a week to bring her back. Her uncle Monaldo led a band of armed kinsmen to the Benedictine convent of San Paolo where Francis had hidden her, intending to drag her home; she clung to the altar and bared her shorn head to show them she had crossed over. Her younger sister Agnes followed her into religious life sixteen days later. Francis settled the two of them, with a few other women who came in the months that followed, at the small church of San Damiano just outside the walls of Assisi. Clare lived there forty-two years and never left.

She was abbess of San Damiano from 1215 until her death, against her own wish. She wrote, in her last years, the *Rule of Saint Clare* — the first religious rule ever composed by a woman in the Western Church, and the first that bound a community to absolute corporate poverty: the *Privilegium Paupertatis* she petitioned from Innocent III and got. Honorius III and Gregory IX tried to soften the rule on her, both for the safety of the community and out of pastoral solicitude. She refused them gently. *I have not come to follow Francis,* she said to Gregory, who had asked her to allow the community to hold endowments, *to be relieved of his poverty.*

The AASS keeps the famous moment of 1241, when a Saracen detachment from the army of Frederick II besieged San Damiano. Clare, then bedridden, asked to be carried to the cloister wall with the Eucharist in a silver pyx in her hands. She prayed. The soldiers, the contemporary witnesses unanimously report, fell back from the wall as if a force had pushed them. The army withdrew the next morning. The image of Clare with the pyx is the standard medieval image of the saint.

She died on the eleventh of August, 1253, two days after Pope Innocent IV had come in person to her bedside at San Damiano and laid the parchment of the approved Rule on her chest. She was sixty. The AASS commemorates her on August 12, the day of her funeral.

> *"I have not come to follow Francis to be relieved of his poverty."* — Clare, to Pope Gregory IX, AASS Augusti II

## August 13

# Maximus the Confessor

*Theologian, monk, dyarch of the human will of Christ, c. 580–662*

The AASS Augusti III titles its long entry for Maximus together with the two Anastasii, his disciple-monk and the Roman papal legate who were tortured with him. The Synaxaria love the Greek pun: *Μέγιστος Μάξιμος* — Maximus great in name, but greater still in deeds.

He was born about 580 in a noble Byzantine family and rose at the imperial court under Heraclius to the office of *protasecretis*, first of the imperial secretaries, the official whose duty was to never leave the emperor's side and whose hand drafted the imperial dispatches. He renounced the office at perhaps thirty-five, took the monastic habit at Chrysopolis across the Bosporus, became abbot there, and fled the Persian invasion of Asia Minor in 626 to Carthage. He lived in North Africa for the next decade and wrote — the *Ambigua* on Gregory Nazianzen, the *Questiones ad Thalassium* on scripture, the *Mystagogia* on the liturgy. The seventh-century church had begun to debate whether Christ, who had two natures, could be said to have two operations and two wills, or only one. Maximus argued, with relentless clarity, that the doctrine of one will collapsed the human nature of Christ into the divine, and that the gospel's *Father, not as I will, but as thou wilt* required two real wills — the eternal will of the Son in his Godhood, and the human will of the Son in his manhood, perfectly harmonized but really distinct.

He went to Rome in 645. He persuaded Pope Martin I to condemn Monothelitism formally at the Lateran Council of 649. The emperor Constans II, who had endorsed the heresy by the *Typos*, arrested Martin in 653 (Martin would die in exile within two years) and Maximus, with his Greek disciple Anastasius and the Roman papal legate also named Anastasius, in 655. The three were tried jointly at Constantinople. They refused to anathematize the Lateran decrees. They were exiled three times, brought back, retried. In their final trial in 662 the emperor had Maximus's tongue cut out and his right hand cut off so that he could neither speak the doctrine again nor write it down. They were sent to a fortress at Schemarum in Lazica, on the eastern shore of the Black Sea. Maximus died there on the 13th of August, 662, eighty-two years old. The two Anastasii died in the same exile within four years.

The Sixth Ecumenical Council, twenty years after his death, condemned Monothelitism in precisely his terms. The Greek church gave him at once the title the Latins would not give him until the seventeenth century: *Confessor*. The mutilated mouth had spoken correctly. The mutilated hand had written more durably than the imperial decree that had ordered the mutilation.

> *"He has cut off the hand that wrote the truth, and the tongue that spoke it. He has not cut off the truth."* — attributed to Maximus after the sentence, AASS Augusti III

## August 14

# Eusebius of Rome

*Presbyter and confessor under Constantius II, d. c. 357*

He was a Roman presbyter of the *titulus* on the Esquiline that still bears his name — the basilica of Sant'Eusebio just east of the Lateran, one of the old Lenten stational churches of the city. The AASS Augusti III preserves him on the testimony of the *Roman Martyrology*: *Romæ, Eusebii presbyteri & confessoris sub Constantio Ariano, sepulti in cœmeterio Callisti.* He died in his own house, in a tiny cell four feet wide, after seven months of imprisonment for refusing the imperial Arian settlement.

The Arian crisis had reached Rome by the middle of the 350s. The Augustus of the West, Constantius II — the son of Constantine — was a convinced Homoean and had bent every imperial pressure on the Latin bishops to subscribe his Arianizing creeds. Pope Liberius held out at first, was exiled to Thrace in 355, and after two years of pressure subscribed the Sirmium formulary and was sent back to Rome. The deacon-pope Felix II, who had been elected during Liberius's exile, was driven from his see when Liberius returned. Eusebius — a presbyter of the orthodox party who had stood with Felix — would not communicate with the restored Liberius.

He preached from the door of the *titulus*-house on the Esquiline he had put at the church's disposal, where the assembly gathered for the Eucharist and the catechumens were taught. *Many in Rome,* the AASS-printed *Vita* records, *by holy confession and the Catholic faith avoided the communion of Liberius through the teaching of Eusebius the presbyter*. The emperor Constantius, when next in Italy, summoned Eusebius to a hearing — with Liberius standing beside the imperial throne. The AASS preserves the dialogue:

*Constantius: Are you the only Christian in the city of Rome?*
*Eusebius: We trust in the Lord, that Christ will find us faithful.*
*Liberius: Are we not in the place of Julius, our predecessor?*
*Eusebius: If you had persevered in the faith you seemed to hold under persecution at the first.*

The emperor sent him back to his own house — and ordered the house sealed around him. The cell they shut him into, the *Acta* report, was four feet wide. He prayed in it standing, with his face to the east, for seven months. He died on the fourteenth of August, perhaps 357. Two presbyter relatives — Gregory and Orosius — collected the body at night and buried it in the Cemetery of Callixtus on the Appian Way, next to the body of Pope Sixtus II martyred a century before. They inscribed the tomb with three words: *Eusebio homini Dei* — to Eusebius, man of God. Gregory was ordered locked alive in the same crypt for the offence of the burial. Orosius dug him out half-dead by night and buried him beside Eusebius when he died.

> *"As for us sinners, we hold the integral faith. As for you, by the envy of your error — you have driven into exile a Catholic bishop praying alone on his small farm."* — Eusebius, to Constantius and Liberius, AASS Augusti III

## August 15

# Alypius of Tagaste

*Bishop, lifelong friend of Augustine, c. 360–c. 430*

He grew up two streets from Augustine in the small Numidian town of Tagaste, the younger of the two by a few years. He admired the older boy, followed him to Carthage to study rhetoric, then to Rome to study law. He had no idea where any of it would lead. He thought, like his teacher, that he was building a career. What he was building was a friendship that would carry both of them into the church.

Augustine, who could never be brief about Alypius, tells one early story against him with great affection. Alypius had sworn off the gladiatorial games as bloody and pointless. Friends in Rome dragged him to the amphitheater anyway. He shut his eyes; the crowd roared; he opened them. He was, the *Confessions* says, *no longer the man who had come, but a member of the crowd to which he had come.* The cure took years. It came in the same Milanese garden where Augustine wept under a fig tree and heard the child's voice say *take and read.* Augustine read Romans 13: *put on the Lord Jesus Christ.* He passed the book to Alypius. Alypius read the next verse: *him that is weak in the faith, receive ye.* He took it to himself. The two were baptized together by Ambrose at the next Easter vigil.

Alypius went home and became bishop of the town they had both left as boys. Augustine became bishop of Hippo, sixty miles north. They wrote each other constantly for the next forty years, fought Donatists and Pelagians shoulder to shoulder, grew old in adjacent sees. When Paulinus of Nola, hearing of Alypius from Augustine, asked for a written life of him, Augustine took on the task himself: *totum Alypium inseram præcordiis tuis* — *I shall set the whole of Alypius into your heart.* He never finished it. The *Confessions*, begun a few years later, carries Alypius only as far as the baptism. The rest of him survives in scattered letters, in the marginal anecdotes of a friend too busy keeping a province in the church to write the life he had promised.

The friendship outlasted the biography. Most do.

> *"Whoever thinks kindly of that man thinks of God's great mercy and God's wonderful gifts."* — Augustine, *Letter* 27 to Paulinus

## August 16

# Hyacinth of Poland

*Dominican missionary to the Slavs, c. 1185–1257*

The AASS Augusti III gives him as *Hyacinthus confessor, ex Ordine FF. Prædicatorum, Cracoviæ in Polonia* — Polish historians commonly call him *Iacko*, the Slavic form of his religious name. His Vita was first written by a fellow Polish Dominican, Stanislaus of Cracow, in the middle of the fourteenth century, and amplified for his canonization by Severinus of Cracow.

He was born about 1185 in Silesia, of the noble Odrowąż clan whose arms — an iron horseshoe — still mark every Dominican foundation he made. He studied at Cracow, then at Bologna and Paris, and was made canon of the cathedral chapter at Cracow on his return. His uncle Ivo Odrowąż, bishop of Cracow, took him to Rome in 1218 on a diplomatic embassy. The two arrived to find the city talking about a small Spanish friar named Dominic whose new order of preachers Honorius III had just confirmed. The uncle and the nephew both witnessed, at the Roman convent of Saint Sabina, a miracle the *Acta* preserve: Dominic raised a young man named Napoleon Orsini from the dead. The bishop asked Dominic for friars to take back to Poland. Dominic answered that he had none yet who could speak the language. The bishop offered his nephew. Hyacinth took the habit from Dominic's own hands, with two companions — Ceslaus, his fellow Pole, and a German friar called Henry — and rode home with the new Order in his saddlebags.

He founded the first Dominican houses of the Slavic lands. Cracow first, in 1222 — the church of the Holy Trinity, still the Polish mother-house of the order. Sandomierz, Płock, Wrocław in Silesia, Gdańsk on the Baltic coast in succession. He preached in Polish, Ruthenian, Lithuanian, and German as the missions extended east. He went to Kiev — then a Christian Slavic city before its destruction by the Mongols — and founded the Dominican community there in 1228. He went north to the still-pagan Prussians and Pomeranians.

The most famous moment of his life was the Mongol assault on Cracow in March of 1241. The Tartar army under Batu Khan had swept across Ruthenia and entered Poland; Cracow was burning by the time it reached the Dominican convent. Hyacinth, the *Vita* says, fled with the Blessed Sacrament from the high altar, but as he was leaving the chapel he heard the voice of the alabaster statue of the Virgin behind him: *Hyacinth, my son, do you leave me here to be profaned by the unbelievers?* He went back, took the heavy statue in his other arm, and walked out of the burning city carrying both. He crossed the Vistula, the chronicler adds, dry-shod with the Eucharist in his right hand and the Virgin in his left. The statue still stands in the Holy Trinity church at Cracow.

He died at the same Cracow priory on the fifteenth of August, 1257 — the AASS marks his commemoration on the sixteenth, the day after his death. He had founded perhaps twenty houses. He was canonized by Clement VIII in 1594.

> *"Hyacinth, my son, do you leave me here to be profaned by the unbelievers?"* — the voice from the statue at Cracow, AASS Augusti III

## August 17

# Liberatus and the Carthaginian Martyrs

*Seven monks under the Vandal Arian king Huneric, 483*

The AASS Augusti III names them in the order Ado kept: Liberatus the abbot, Bonifacius the deacon, Servus and Rusticus subdeacons, Rogatus and Septimus monks, and Maximus a boy. The witness is Victor of Vita, the African Catholic bishop who survived the Vandal persecution and wrote its history within the decade — the *Historia Persecutionis Africanae Provinciae*, which the Bollandists print as the *Passio* itself. The date is 483, the seventh year of Huneric, son of Geiseric, king of the Vandals of North Africa.

The Vandals were Arian. Geiseric had crossed from Spain into Africa in 429, had taken Carthage in 439, and had ruled the western Mediterranean from his African base for nearly fifty years. His son Huneric, who succeeded him in 477, hated the Catholics with focused intensity. Edict followed edict: the Catholic clergy were exiled in waves; the Catholic basilicas were turned over to the Arian Vandal clergy; the Catholic monasteries were watched. The territory of Capsa in southern Byzacena — modern Gafsa, in central Tunisia — held a Catholic monastic community whose name we have lost; we have only its abbot Liberatus and his six companions. They were arrested in 483 and brought up the coast under guard to Carthage.

They were given the standard last chance: rebaptism by Arian clergy, in the formula Huneric had imposed on the African church — that the Son was not consubstantial with the Father, but a lesser, made being. They refused. They were thrown in the cells of the city prison, weighted with iron chains, and kept in the dark. From the cells, Victor of Vita records, they continued to strengthen *die ac nocte* — day and night — the Catholic people of the city in their faith, calling out their teaching through the bars to whoever could hear.

Huneric ordered them executed publicly. A ship was loaded with bundles of dry wood. The seven were tied to the wood. The ship was rowed out into the bay of Carthage and the fire was lit. The flames, Victor's narrative says, were extinguished by divine command in front of the watching crowd on the shore — *statim imperio divino, videntibus cunctis, exstinctus est*. The fire was rekindled; the flames went out again. The king, furious and ashamed, sent oarsmen aboard with the long oar-paddles. The seven were beaten to death with the oars, *cerebris comminutis* — their skulls broken — and the bodies thrown into the sea. By the same hour of the same day, the bodies were washed ashore intact. The Carthaginian Catholics carried them in procession, with hymns and the clergy walking before, to a monastery beside the basilica of Saint Celerina, and buried them there together. Huneric died within months.

> *"He who lit the fire could not make it burn; he who broke the skulls could not break the witness."* — Victor of Vita, on the seven martyrs of Carthage

## August 18

# Helena of Constantinople

*Empress, mother of Constantine, discoverer of the Cross, c. 248–328*

The AASS Augusti III gives her name in the imperial form preserved on her coinage: *Flavia Julia Helena*. The Bollandists open with a long source-critical discussion of her birthplace — the British tradition (Colchester, daughter of a King Coel) they reject; the Treveran (Trier) and the Bithynian (Drepanum on the Sea of Marmara, the small port-town her son would later rename Helenopolis) remain. The commentary tilts gently to the Bithynian view, on the testimony of Procopius and Anonymus Valesianus. She was born in the East. The empire she would die in had not yet been born.

She was a *stabularia*, by the Latin sources — the daughter or perhaps the keeper of an inn — when the Roman officer Constantius Chlorus, then in the East with the legions, took her into his household about 270. He was perhaps thirty; she was twenty-two. They were not formally married. She bore him a son in 272 or 273 at the Roman colony of Naissus in Moesia — modern Niš in Serbia — and they called him Constantine. The household kept together for the next twenty years. In 293, when Diocletian raised Constantius to be Caesar of the West in the new Tetrarchy, the new Caesar was required to put Helena aside and to marry Theodora for political reasons. Helena retired into obscurity at Trier with her son. She lived perhaps thirteen years out of the public eye.

Her son became Augustus on his father's death at York in 306, and over the next eighteen years fought his way to sole rule. He recalled his mother to the court. He gave her the title Augusta, the imperial purple, the right to mint her own coinage, and assigned her the Sessorian Palace at Rome with its great gardens out toward the Lateran. She converted to Christianity, by Eusebius's testimony, under her son's instruction; she became the foremost imperial patroness of the church.

In the early 320s, now in her late seventies, she made the journey to Palestine for which the Greek tradition calls her Saint. She arrived at Jerusalem in perhaps 326. The Temple platform she found in ruins; the city of Hadrian's *Aelia Capitolina* had been built over the older holy places. She had Constantine's authorization to clear, to excavate, to rebuild. Beneath a pagan shrine on the Western Hill she had the workmen dig, and found — Ambrose, Rufinus, Sozomen, Socrates, Theodoret all report it — three crosses and a *titulus* with the name of Christ. The True Cross was identified by miracle: a sick woman healed at its touch. She built the Basilica of the Holy Sepulchre over the site, the Basilica of the Nativity at Bethlehem, the Eleona on the Mount of Olives. She returned to Constantinople carrying portions of the Cross with her. She died there, perhaps eighty, in 328. The relics now lie in the church of Santa Croce in Gerusalemme at Rome, built into her old Sessorian Palace, with earth from Calvary still under its floor.

> *"My son, build a basilica over this place; for here the Lord was lifted up for the world."* — Helena, by tradition, to Constantine, after the finding of the Cross

## August 19

# Andrew the Tribune

*Roman officer and Christian-by-victory, with companions, c. 300*

The Greek calendar calls him *megalomartyr* — Great Martyr — and the Latin tradition, the AASS Augusti III observes with some asperity, mistakenly took the title as a separate name, giving us *Saint Magnus or Saint Andrew* in the older Western martyrologies. There is one martyr, Andrew, *tribunus* — a regimental officer of the Roman army in the East under Maximian — and his soldier-companions, traditionally numbered as two thousand five hundred and ninety-three.

He was sent with other regional commanders, under the central command of a general named Antiochus, against the Persians who in the late 290s had again pressed the imperial frontier in eastern Anatolia. He was not yet a Christian. He had heard the Name of Christ from a few of his men who were Christians — Andrew, by the *Acta*, was a thoughtful man, *philosophus more militari*, the kind of officer who listened — and on the eve of a battle in which his small force was outnumbered, he called the camp into a council. *Pray to the God these men have spoken of,* he said; if the God who hears their prayers gives us the victory tomorrow, I will be his soldier afterward as I have been the emperor's today. They prayed. They won the battle in inexplicable rout: the Persian line broke without a real engagement.

He kept his word. He went down to the river the same evening and was baptized — by one of the Christian soldiers, the *Acta* hint, since they had no priest with them. He instructed the rest of his unit himself in the next weeks. The whole detachment, by the time they came down off campaign and returned to their winter quarters in Cilicia, was Christian. The report reached Antiochus the general, who reached for the punishment the imperial edicts of Diocletian required. He had Andrew brought to his headquarters and stretched on a heated iron bed. He had Andrew's officers' hands transfixed with nails to wooden beams in the courtyard. The men did not recant. He sent a fresh detachment of a thousand soldiers to compel them. The thousand, the *Acta* say, were converted by Andrew where they stood.

Antiochus turned the matter over to the provincial governor, a man named Seleucus, who decided to handle it as a desertion. He had the entire band — perhaps two thousand five hundred soldiers in total — pursued into the wild country of the Cilician Taurus. They were caught in a narrow defile of the mountains. They had stopped to pray. Seleucus had them all put to the sword on the spot. The Greek verses the *Menaia* still sing keep the image: *this troop, beheaded, gave thee rivers of blood, O Christ, to whom thou hadst given rivers of water.* A spring, the *Acta* report, rose at the place of the killing. Pilgrims drank from it for centuries.

> *"Pray to the God these men have spoken of, and if he hears, I shall be his soldier."* — Andrew, the night before the Persian battle, AASS Augusti III

## August 20

# Bernard of Clairvaux

*Cistercian abbot, doctor mellifluus, 1091–1153*

The AASS Augusti IV opens its massive entry — three biographers writing in succession during the saint's own lifetime, plus an apparatus of further sources running to half the August volume — with the observation that no medieval saint has more contemporary witnesses. He was born in 1091 at Fontaines-lès-Dijon, in his father Tescelin's small castle just north of the Burgundian capital. His mother Aleth bore seven children — six sons and a daughter — and Bernard was the third. The family was minor nobility: enough land to support a knight and his retinue, not enough wealth for any of the sons to expect more than a soldier's career. Aleth raised them strictly. She fasted herself the days she nursed them. She died when Bernard was nineteen, leaving him the most marked by her formation.

He decided for the cloister at twenty-two. He chose the strictest house in Christendom: Cîteaux, founded fifteen years before by Robert of Molesme as a return to the original Benedictine rule against the prosperity of the great Cluny houses. Under Stephen Harding the third abbot, Cîteaux was struggling. Bernard arrived at the gate in the spring of 1113 with thirty companions whom he had persuaded — four of his own brothers, several cousins, a small group of Dijon friends, all of whom he had argued out of military careers, marriages, and clerical preferments. Stephen Harding was at evening prayer in the small chapel when the porter announced that a young Burgundian noble was at the gate, with what he described as a small army.

Bernard was sent two years later to found the third daughter house of Cîteaux. Clairvaux — the *Clara Vallis*, the bright valley — was on the upper Aube, in difficult forested country, given by a relative of the Burgundian count. He was twenty-five when he was made abbot. He governed there thirty-eight years and did not leave the abbacy when the offers of episcopal sees came. He refused the see of Châlons twice. He refused Genoa, Milan, the archbishopric of Reims. He remained a monk in choir and an abbot in chapter for the rest of his life.

He preached. The eighty-six sermons on the *Song of Songs* that he composed and delivered at Clairvaux through the last twenty years of his life are the most extended single piece of monastic mystical commentary in the Western tradition. He wrote *De Diligendo Deo* — the small book *On Loving God* — that opens with the four sentences the medieval centuries learned by heart: *Causa diligendi Deum, Deus est; modus, sine modo diligere*. The cause of loving God is God himself; the manner, to love him without measure. He defended Innocent II against the antipope Anacletus II through eight years of papal schism, walking in person from city to city across Europe persuading the bishops and the kings. He preached the Second Crusade in 1146 by command of Pope Eugenius III, his own former pupil. The crusade failed; Bernard accepted the public blame for the failure with characteristic candor. He died at Clairvaux on the twentieth of August, 1153, sixty-three years old. The Cistercian order at his death numbered three hundred and forty-three houses.

> *"The cause of loving God is God; the manner, to love him without measure."* — Bernard, *De Diligendo Deo* 1

## August 21

# Bernard Tolomei

*Founder of the Olivetans, plague-nurse of Siena, 1272–1348*

He was born Giovanni Tolomei in 1272, eldest son of one of the great Sienese noble families, who counted themselves descendants of Ptolemy the Geographer. He was educated at the cathedral school, studied law, was made a knight in his early twenties, taught philosophy at the rising Sienese *Studio*, and would have inherited his father's seat on the council of the *Nove* — the nine merchant-magistrates who governed Siena. He had taken the secular path his class expected. He was forty when he went suddenly blind in the middle of a public oration in the city square, mid-sentence, before a crowd of perhaps a thousand. He was led off the stage. He spent three days in a darkened room and recovered his sight on the fourth, after a long prayer the AASS records but does not quote. He took a private vow to retire from the world.

He waited three years. In 1313 he bought from his own family a tract of empty land in the *crete* — the rolling chalk badlands twenty miles south of Siena — at a place called Accona. He took the name Bernardo and went there with two companions, both fellow Sienese nobles who had decided to follow him. They built a small hermitage on a hill the chronicler began calling *Mons Oliveti*, Mount of Olives, for the grove on its eastern slope. They lived strictly: one meal a day, no meat, the full Benedictine night office, manual work on the land. Pope John XXII, suspicious at first of unauthorized Tuscan hermitages, approved the rule in 1319 as a Benedictine variant. The Olivetans grew to perhaps twenty houses by mid-century, distinguished by a white habit and devotion to the Virgin of Mount Olivet.

Bernard governed the order until 1348. The Black Death reached Siena in May of that year. Most of the city's clergy fled or died in the first weeks. Bernard left Monte Oliveto Maggiore with eighty of his monks and walked the twenty miles to Siena, set up a plague-station in a central convent, and nursed the dying through the worst months of the epidemic. Seventy-nine of the eighty died with the sick they had come to nurse. Bernard, the AASS records, died last, on the twenty-first of August, the only one of the eighty whose grave is not in Siena — for the convent did not survive the plague, and the burial site was lost.

> *"He went into the city not to escape the plague, but to die with those whom the plague had already chosen."* — AASS Augusti IV, on Bernard Tolomei at Siena

## August 22

# Sigfrid of Wearmouth

*Northumbrian abbot of Bede's house, d. 689*

The AASS preserves him through one witness above all: Bede the Venerable, who as a young monk lived under Sigfrid's brief abbacy at the twin monastery of Saint Peter at Wearmouth and Saint Paul at Jarrow, and who would later write the life of his masters. Bede knew the man personally. He calls him *sanctus Sigfridus*. Everything else comes from him.

Sigfrid had come to Wearmouth as a young man, probably from a Northumbrian noble household, drawn by the reputation of Benedict Biscop — the great traveling abbot whose five journeys to Rome had brought back the books, the chant-masters, the glass-workers, and the canonical observances that made Wearmouth the most learned monastic foundation in seventh-century England. Biscop ruled both houses with three successive coadjutor-abbots: Easterwine for four years, Sigfrid for three, then Ceolfrid, who would send the *Codex Amiatinus* to Rome.

Sigfrid took office at Wearmouth while Biscop was away on his last journey south. Bede, in the *Lives of the Abbots*, calls him *vir litteris Scripturarum sufficienter imbutus, moribus optimis ornatum, et mira abstinentiæ virtute præditum atque etiam patientiæ* — a man well-trained in the literature of the Scriptures, adorned with the best manners, and gifted with a marvelous virtue of abstinence and patience. The abstinence was the technical monastic term: he ate even less than the rule required, and not from ostentation, but from the long disposition of a man who had learned to do without.

The patience went into his last illness. He died slowly of what Bede calls *diuturnus pulmonum morbus* — a long disease of the lungs — that wasted him for nearly three years, exactly the years he was abbot. He kept the office through all of it. The brothers, who watched him at the daily Eucharist and at the night offices, learned more of monastic patience from his bearing of the disease than they had learned from the rule. He died on the twenty-second of August, 689, and was translated by Abbot Huwetberht in the next generation into the church beside Easterwine, in a single shrine. Bede himself was perhaps fifteen.

> *"Beautifully trained in the Scriptures, gifted with abstinence and patience: he taught us most when he could no longer speak."* — Bede, *Lives of the Abbots* (AASS Augusti IV)

## August 23

# Sidonius Apollinaris

*Bishop-poet of Clermont, witness of the fall of Roman Gaul, c. 430–488*

He was born about 430 at Lyon, in the great senatorial family of the Apollinares — his grandfather had been Praetorian Prefect of Gaul, his father held the same office under Petronius Maximus. He married Papianilla, the daughter of the future Western emperor Avitus. He composed panegyrics in elegant hexameters for three successive emperors — Avitus his father-in-law, Majorian who unseated him, and Anthemius — and was made Prefect of the City of Rome in 468. He served the empire while there was an empire to serve.

He returned to Gaul in 469. The province by then was collapsing. The Visigothic king Euric had begun the systematic conquest of Aquitaine and Auvergne. The bishopric of Clermont — the great walled hill-city of central Gaul — fell vacant in 470, and the local clergy and people, in a moment of necessity, asked Sidonius to take it. He had not been ordained. He had served as a senator and a court poet, no more. He accepted on a single condition: that he be allowed to keep faith with the imperial cause. He was ordained priest, then bishop, in a single rapid succession of liturgies. He was forty.

He led the defense of Clermont against Euric's siege for four years — 471 to 475 — with his brother-in-law Ecdicius, who commanded a small private cavalry from his own estates. The bishop fasted, preached, organized supplies, set the night watches on the walls. His letters of these years to the bishops of southern Gaul are among the most remarkable in the patristic corpus — half pastoral consultation, half dispatches from the front. In 475 the emperor Julius Nepos surrendered the Auvergne to Euric by treaty without consulting Clermont. Sidonius wrote to Bishop Graecus of Marseille, who had brokered it, the line the AASS preserves: *Pugnavimus pro vobis; deserti sumus a vobis* — we fought for you, and we have been abandoned by you.

Euric had him exiled briefly, then released him after a careful Latin panegyric — the price of his return — and he resumed the bishopric. He governed twelve more years under Visigothic suzerainty, kept his Latin and his hexameters and his prayer, gave away the family treasures in the famines of the early 480s, and died at Clermont, probably on the twenty-first of August, 488. He was fifty-eight. He prayed earnestly. He studied the Scriptures with the same care he had once given Vergil. He gave away his wealth as freely as he had once given his verses.

> *"We fought for you, and we have been abandoned by you."* — Sidonius, to Bishop Graecus of Marseille, after the surrender of Auvergne, AASS Augusti IV

## August 24

# Bartholomew the Apostle

*One of the Twelve, first century*

He is named simply, always alongside Philip, in the three synoptic lists of the apostles and in the first chapter of Acts. *Bar-Tolmai* is a patronymic — *son of Tolmai* — an old Davidic-era name. The personal name of the apostle is, by an early patristic identification still defended by the Bollandists and confirmed by the indirect evidence of John's Gospel, *Nathanael*: *God has given*. The synoptics use the patronymic; John uses the proper name. Both refer to the same disciple.

He came from Cana of Galilee. He was sitting under a fig tree, the only place John records him, when his friend Philip — who had just been called by Jesus — came running to find him. *We have found him of whom Moses in the law and the prophets wrote: Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph.* Nathanael did not at first agree. *Can any good thing come out of Nazareth?* Philip did not argue. He answered the way the gospel makes the answer: *Come and see.* They walked back together. Jesus saw him approaching and said, before he could be introduced, *Behold an Israelite indeed, in whom is no guile.* Nathanael asked the question of a man who had not been expected: *Whence knowest thou me?* Jesus answered: *Before that Philip called thee, when thou wast under the fig tree, I saw thee.* Nathanael answered with the confession that no one else in the gospel makes so quickly — the confession Peter would not give for another two years at Caesarea Philippi: *Rabbi, thou art the Son of God; thou art the King of Israel.*

He is present at the Last Supper, at the appearance by the Sea of Galilee, in the upper room at Pentecost. The post-apostolic tradition, preserved by Eusebius from Pantaenus of Alexandria, places his later mission in *India* — a loose patristic term covering everything east of the Persian Gulf — where Pantaenus in the second century reported finding Christian communities who still possessed a Hebrew Gospel of Matthew that Bartholomew was said to have left them. The Armenian tradition, equally ancient, places his last mission and martyrdom in Greater Armenia, where he was flayed alive at *Albanopolis* and beheaded. The flayed-skin iconography became his standard attribute: Michelangelo gave the man holding his own skin in the *Last Judgment* his own self-portrait, painted into Bartholomew's empty face.

His relics, by the ninth century, lay on the island of Lipari off Sicily; in the tenth, Otto III translated them to Rome. They lie still in San Bartolomeo all'Isola on the Tiber Island, in the city that has kept his feast since the early Middle Ages.

> *"Rabbi, thou art the Son of God; thou art the King of Israel."* — Nathanael Bartholomew, *John* 1:49

## August 25

# Louis IX of France

*King and crusader, 1214–1270*

He was born in 1214 at Poissy and called himself, ever afterward, *Louis de Poissy* — Louis of the baptismal font — preferring the place where he had been made a Christian to the place where he had been born a king. His father Louis VIII died when he was eleven. His mother Blanche of Castile governed France as regent for nine years, fending off the great baronial coup of 1226 with the same firm hand she had used to teach her son the Pater Noster. She told him, the chronicler Joinville records, that she would rather see him dead before her than knowingly commit a mortal sin. The line shaped him.

He took up the cross in December 1244 after a near-fatal illness; recovered, he prepared the Seventh Crusade for three years. He landed at Damietta in June 1249 and took the city without resistance. The campaign in the Nile delta the next year went badly. He was defeated at Mansurah, then captured. He was ransomed for the city of Damietta and a sum the kingdom had to borrow against. He spent the next four years in the Holy Land at Acre, rebuilding the Latin fortifications and negotiating with the Mamluks, while his mother died in his absence — the news reached him in 1252.

He returned to France in 1254. The next sixteen years were the years that made him *Saint Louis*. He revised the kingdom's law-codes through the *Établissements*. He instituted royal investigators — *enquêteurs* — who could ride into any seneschalcy and hear complaints against the king's own officials. He gave justice in person, sitting under the famous oak of Vincennes, where every Frenchman with a grievance could come to him directly. He founded the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris to house the Crown of Thorns and the True Cross relics he had bought from the impoverished Latin Emperor of Constantinople. He kept the Friday fast, washed the feet of the poor weekly in Lent, and refused, the AASS records, to laugh at jokes told at the expense of clerics.

He took the cross again in 1267. He was fifty-three, his health was failing, and his confessors begged him not to. He went. The Eighth Crusade landed at Tunis in July 1270. Dysentery swept the camp within weeks. The king fell ill on the twenty-fourth of August. He received the last rites on a bed of ashes spread in the shape of a cross on the floor of his tent — the medieval royal practice of *cendres* — and died on the twenty-fifth. He was fifty-six. The army withdrew. His son Philip III carried the bones home to Saint-Denis. He was canonized in 1297.

> *"I had rather see thee dead at my feet than ever know that thou hadst committed a mortal sin."* — Blanche of Castile to her son, as Louis often retold

## August 26

# Bregwine of Canterbury

*Archbishop, eighth-century continental Saxon, d. 762*

The AASS draws his entry chiefly from the Vita written by Eadmer of Canterbury in the eleventh century — the same Eadmer who wrote the life of Anselm — about a Canterbury archbishop whom Eadmer remembered through three centuries of cathedral tradition. The historical Bregwine had been born in old continental Saxony, in the country now lying between the Weser and the Elbe, of a noble Saxon family. He crossed to England as a young man.

The crossing is itself remarkable. Bregwine moved in the opposite direction of the prevailing eighth-century missionary current. Boniface, Willibald, Lull, Wynnebald — almost every name of the missions to the continent came from the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms across the Channel into the still-pagan Saxon north. Bregwine was a young continental Saxon whose own country had not yet been Christianized, traveling the other way to be educated and trained in the English church that the earlier missionary generation had built. Eadmer's Vita preserves his arrival in unembroidered prose: *among them Bregwine, coming there, soon attaining a position, was dear to all, and made a citizen of the country.*

He took the monastic habit and served as a priest of the cathedral chapter at Canterbury under Archbishop Cuthbert through the 750s. When Cuthbert died in October of 758, the Canterbury clergy elected Bregwine to succeed him. He was consecrated on the feast of Saint Michael in 759 or 760. He governed Canterbury only three years. The chief act of his pontificate was the conclusion of a long jurisdictional dispute with the abbots of the great independent monastery of Saint Augustine, just outside the Canterbury wall, over the burial of the archbishops of Canterbury. The abbey had claimed since Augustine's foundation that every archbishop should be buried at Saint Augustine's. Bregwine ruled that the cathedral itself — Christ Church Canterbury — was the rightful resting place of the metropolitans, and arranged accordingly.

The first archbishop actually buried under that ruling was himself. He died on the 26th of August, 762, and was buried in the cathedral he had defended. He was, the chronicler adds, deeply mourned. Three years had been short for a metropolitan; the Saxon convert had been just long enough at Canterbury to make himself indispensable, and was gone before anyone could prepare for the loss.

> *"He was dear to all, and made a citizen of the country."* — Eadmer of Canterbury, on Bregwine

## August 27

# Caesarius of Arles

*Provençal bishop, preacher, drafter of the Council of Orange, 470–542*

He was born about 470 at Chalon-sur-Saône in Burgundy, in a Gallo-Roman family of senatorial rank already deeply Christian. He was tonsured at the cathedral school at eighteen, lectured for two years, then walked south to the great monastic island of Lérins off the coast of Provence — already a generation old in his day, the seedbed of half the Provençal episcopate. He spent seven years there. The abbot Porcarius made him cellarer; he distributed the abbey's stores so freely that the brothers complained, and the abbot defended him: *He has given more than the rule allows; but only to those whom the rule has failed.* He fell ill from over-fasting and was sent to Arles for medical care.

He was made priest of Arles by Archbishop Aeonius, who was a kinsman. When Aeonius died in 502, Caesarius — perhaps reluctantly — was elected to succeed him. He was thirty-two. He governed Arles forty years, the longest archiepiscopal reign of any Gaulish bishop of the late-antique west.

The pontificate is remembered for three things. First, preaching: nearly two hundred and fifty of his sermons survive — the largest body of Latin homiletic preaching to come down from the fifth or sixth century in Gaul — in a Latin Caesarius worked hard to keep simple, pastoral, accessible to working farmers. He cut the Mass in half so the catechumens would not leave before the homily. He had the choir sing the antiphons in Latin verses simple enough for the congregation to follow. He preached without notes, every Sunday and every feast. Second, the monasteries. He founded the first women's monastery in the West to follow a written rule of its own — *Regula ad Virgines* — and put his sister Caesaria over it at Arles. The rule survives. It is strict: enclosed life, manual work, the recitation of all 150 psalms each week, complete community of goods. The house grew to two hundred nuns in his lifetime. Third, the Council of Orange of 529, which settled the long Semi-Pelagian controversy by drafting twenty-five canons that affirmed Augustine's doctrine without the predestinarian extensions: that grace precedes and enables every motion of the will toward God, but that the will, once moved, freely cooperates. The Pope confirmed the canons. The council became, for the next millennium of Latin theology, the standard mediating settlement on grace.

He died at Arles on the twenty-seventh of August, 542, the morning after his last consecration of a deacon. The Vita's closing lines preserve the death-bed instruction he gave his community: *No image of me anywhere; my poor habit is to be given to the next pauper at the gate; my books are to be kept where they have been kept.*

> *"The voice that fits the country pulpit is not the voice of the schools; preach what they can carry home in their basket."* — Caesarius, to his preachers, AASS Augusti VI

## August 28

# Augustine of Hippo

*Bishop, doctor of grace, 354–430*

He was born on the 13th of November, 354, at the small Numidian town of Thagaste — modern Souk Ahras in eastern Algeria. His father Patricius was a pagan town councillor of modest means who would not convert until late in life; his mother Monica was a Christian whose persistence in prayer for husband and son the *Confessions* later recorded. He went away to school at Madaura, then to Carthage, where his father's friends scraped together enough to send him for the higher rhetorical training that was the only path open in late-Roman Africa for a boy of intellectual promise without family wealth.

He spent the next fifteen years in the wilderness Possidius and the *Confessions* describe in parallel. A common-law concubine he kept for thirteen years, who bore him his only son Adeodatus. The Manichaean dualist sect, in whose framework he tried for nine years to make sense of evil. The rhetorical career that took him from Thagaste back to Carthage, then to Rome, then in 384 to the imperial Milan court of Valentinian II, where as the official municipal rhetor he heard Ambrose preach for the first time. The slow, painful conversion through Plotinus, through Paul's letters, through the conversations with the African Pontitianus about the Egyptian monks — and at last the garden scene of August 386, when, weeping under a fig tree in his rented house, he heard a child's voice in the next house singing *tolle, lege* — take and read — and opened Paul's letter to the Romans at the chapter on the works of darkness. He was baptized by Ambrose at the Easter Vigil of 387.

He went home to Africa to found a small community of *servi Dei* on the family estate. His mother died at Ostia on the way home, after the conversation by the window the *Confessions* preserves as the one shared moment of mystical participation in his life. His son Adeodatus died at seventeen the following year. Augustine was thirty-six. He was kidnapped — almost literally — into the priesthood at Hippo in 391, when on a pastoral visit the assembled congregation surrounded him in the basilica and would not let him out until the elderly bishop Valerius had ordained him on the spot. Valerius made him co-bishop in 395, sole bishop a year later. He governed Hippo thirty-four years.

The works of his hand fill thirteen folio volumes. The *Confessions* in his early forties — the first sustained autobiographical interior narrative in the Latin tradition. The *De Trinitate* across twenty years of revision. The *City of God*, written across thirteen years in response to the sack of Rome in 410, to explain why the gospel does not promise temporal empires. The anti-Pelagian writings of the last fifteen years that set the foundation for every subsequent Western theology of grace. He died on the twenty-eighth of August, 430, in his episcopal house, with the Vandal army of Geiseric three months into the siege of Hippo. He had ordered the seven penitential psalms to be copied out in large letters and hung on the walls of his bedroom, so that he could read them on his deathbed.

> *"Late have I loved thee, O Beauty so ancient and so new — late have I loved thee."* — Augustine, *Confessions* X.27

## August 29

# The Beheading of John the Baptist

*The cost of speaking the truth to a tetrarch, c. AD 29*

The four gospels give the story in three places. Mark tells it in the most detail in chapter six, Matthew in chapter fourteen, Luke briefly in chapter nine. Josephus the historian, writing two generations later in the *Antiquities of the Jews*, confirms the central facts from a sympathetic-pagan angle: that Herod Antipas the Tetrarch of Galilee imprisoned and killed John the Baptist out of political fear, and that the Jewish people held the death afterward as the cause of Antipas's later defeat at the hands of the Nabataean king Aretas. The two accounts converge. The death was historical.

The cause was a marriage. Herod Antipas had repudiated his lawful wife — the daughter of Aretas IV — and had married Herodias, the wife of his living half-brother Herod Philip. The Mosaic law explicitly forbade marriage to a brother's wife while the brother lived. John the Baptist, preaching from the eastern shore of the Jordan, had said so publicly. Antipas, who was politically careful but personally weak, did not at first know what to do. He had a popular following of his own and could not afford to make John a public martyr. He arrested him and held him in the prison fortress of Machaerus on the high desert plateau east of the Dead Sea, and went in to converse with him from time to time. Mark says he liked to hear John talk. He was, in Mark's careful phrase, *much perplexed* — *and yet he heard him gladly.*

Herodias did not share the perplexity. She wanted John dead. The opening came at Antipas's birthday feast at Machaerus. The tetrarch had gathered his court — his commanders, his great men, the principal landowners of Galilee — for the celebration. Herodias's daughter Salome, then perhaps fifteen, was brought in to dance for the guests. The performance pleased the king. He swore in front of his court that he would give her whatever she asked, up to half his tetrarchy. She left the hall to consult her mother. She came back and asked for the head of John the Baptist on a platter. The king was sorry — Mark's word — but for his oath's sake, and the sake of those who sat at meat with him, he sent the executioner. The head was brought back to the hall on the platter and given to the girl, who gave it to her mother.

John's own disciples, Mark concludes, came in the night and took the body and laid it in a tomb. The grave is unknown. Antipas afterward thought, when he heard of Jesus's miracles, that John the Baptist had risen from the dead. The forerunner who had pointed to Christ at the beginning of his ministry was the first to die for not consenting to a marriage Christ would also condemn. The cost of preaching the moral law was already, in the *exodus* Moses and Elijah had spoken of with Jesus on Tabor, being paid in flesh.

> *"It is not lawful for thee to have thy brother's wife."* — John the Baptist to Herod Antipas, *Mark* 6:18

## August 30

# Pammachius the Senator

*Roman patrician, friend of Jerome, founder of the Porto hospice, c. 340–410*

The AASS rebuilds his life almost entirely from the letters of Jerome, who corresponded with Pammachius for thirty years. He was the most distinguished Latin Christian layman of the late fourth century — a senator of the ancient *gens Furia*, descended from the consular Furii Camilli, by Jerome's own description *consulum pronepos et Furiani germinis decus*: great-grandson of consuls and the ornament of the Furian stock.

He had been raised in the kind of Roman senatorial Christianity that was already, by the 360s, a distinct social formation in the city — wealthy, learned, ascetically inclined, building libraries of patristic Greek and Latin in the great houses of the Aventine and the Caelian. He had been a fellow student of Jerome's at Rome when both were perhaps eighteen; the friendship lasted the rest of Jerome's life. He married Paulina, the daughter of Saint Paula of Rome — and so the sister of Eustochium and Blesilla — bringing his own great Furian line into union with the equally distinguished Aemilian-Scipionic line of his mother-in-law. The marriage was, by Jerome's testimony, deeply happy. They had no surviving children. Paulina died in 396 in childbirth; Jerome wrote Pammachius the consolation letter that the AASS quotes at length.

What Pammachius did in widowhood is the substance of his sanctity. He gave away the great Furian fortune systematically over the next fifteen years. He undertook, with his sister-in-law's friend Fabiola, the founding of the first public hospital in the Latin West: not a private *valetudinarium* on a great estate, but a true *xenodochium* — the *hospitium Portuense* — at the harbor town of Porto on the right bank of the Tiber mouth, where the grain fleet from Africa unloaded, and where the sick pilgrims from across the Mediterranean disembarked into the city. He paid for the building. He worked the wards himself, washing the feet of the sick. The hospital lasted as the chief Christian poor-relief institution of Rome until the Lombard invasions of the seventh century.

He was made priest at some point in the 390s. He patronized Jerome's Vulgate translation work, paid for the cost of his copyists at Bethlehem, and distributed his books at Rome. He died, probably in early 410, just before the Visigothic sack of Rome under Alaric in August of that year. His old house on the Caelian — *titulus Pammachii*, the family seat for three centuries — became after the sack the church now known as Santi Giovanni e Paolo, whose mosaics still bear his name.

> *"Pammachius — the truest Cherub of Ezekiel: kinsman, son-in-law, husband, brother — for the things of the Spirit do not stay within the names of marriage."* — Jerome, *Epistula* 66.4, on Pammachius

## August 31

# Aidan of Lindisfarne

*Irish bishop of the Northumbrians, d. 651*

The AASS rebuilds his life almost entirely from Bede's *Ecclesiastical History*, book three — Bede was born about twenty years after Aidan died, and his witnesses at Wearmouth-Jarrow had known the bishop personally. The fountain, the AASS says, is the *Historia*.

Aidan was an Irish monk of the great monastery of Iona that Columba had founded a generation before. He was sent to Northumbria in 635 at the request of King Oswald — the same Oswald who had been baptized at Iona during his years of exile and who had won the kingdom back at Heavenfield. Oswald had first sent to Iona for a more austere bishop, one Corman, who had returned within months declaring the Northumbrians unteachably barbarous. Aidan, present at the chapter where Corman gave his report, observed quietly that the failure was perhaps the master's, not the people's — *milk before meat*, in the famous phrase Bede preserves: that one should teach the simpler doctrines first, and let the deeper teaching come gradually. The abbot consecrated him bishop on the spot and sent him south.

He chose the island of Lindisfarne off the Northumbrian coast for his see — a tidal island, accessible to the mainland only at low tide, a quarter-mile from the royal fortress at Bamburgh. He governed it sixteen years. He kept the Iona monastic rule personally: he walked everywhere, refusing the horses Oswald repeatedly offered him; the Irish saint's habit, Bede explains, was that mounted travel set the bishop above the common people, while walking kept him beside them. He gave away the gifts kings and nobles brought him to the poor on the roads he walked. He read his Latin Bible aloud while walking. He took meals at the royal table only when summoned, and then ate sparingly. He was a man whose authority over the king came from his evident refusal to want anything from him.

Aidan, when he arrived, did not yet have enough Anglo-Saxon to preach in it. King Oswald, who had learned fluent Irish during his Iona years, stood beside him in the open and translated his sermons sentence by sentence — the king of Northumbria as interpreter to the bishop he had brought. The two of them, for the first year of the mission, walked the kingdom together. Aidan learned English gradually. The conversion moved with the pair of them across the moors. Oswald was killed at Maserfield in 642; Aidan continued bishop until his own death in 651, having ordained the first generation of native English bishops and trained at Lindisfarne the boy Cuthbert — who, on the morning of the 31st of August, was watching his flock on the Lammermuir Hills when he saw a great light over the sea and angels carrying a soul aloft. He learned later that Aidan had died at Bamburgh that very night. He took the vision as his call.

> *"Milk before meat. The deeper teaching, only when they can carry it."* — Aidan, at the Iona chapter, recorded by Bede HE III.5
