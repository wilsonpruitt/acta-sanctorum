## October 25

# Gaudentius of Brescia

*Bishop, advocate of John Chrysostom, c. 360–c. 410*

He was a presbyter at Brescia in northern Italy under Bishop Philastrius, and grew up in his teacher's household, learning the patristic tradition and the spoken Latin of fourth-century Cisalpine Gaul. In the late 380s he set out on a long pilgrimage to the East. He went to Cappadocia, where the biographer of Basil of Caesarea gave him a portion of the relics of the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste; he went down to the Holy Land; he spent time at the new Latin monastic foundations beginning to grow in Bethlehem and Jerusalem. While he was on the road Philastrius died, and the Brescian church — without consulting him, against his repeated protests by letter — elected him bishop in absence. He returned, the eastern bishops having refused him communion until he agreed to come home, and was ordained by Ambrose of Milan in 388.

He governed the see for twenty years. The twenty-one sermons that survive — preached at Brescia, recorded by his hearers — are among the great pieces of late-fourth-century Italian preaching. He preached on the eucharist, on the paschal vigil, on the difficult sayings of the Lord, on the saints whose relics he had brought back from the East. He built a basilica on the south side of Brescia to house those relics, the *Concilium Sanctorum,* the first major reliquary church of northern Italy.

His most consequential act was a journey he could not refuse. In the spring of 405 the western bishops, scandalized by the deposition of John Chrysostom from Constantinople, sent a high-level legation to the emperor Arcadius to demand his restoration. Innocent I of Rome chose Gaudentius to lead it. He went, with two Roman senators, on a winter voyage from Brindisi to Athens to Constantinople. The embassy was a disaster. Arcadius would not receive them. They were detained three months, refused audiences, threatened, finally put on a leaky boat and sent home through a storm that nearly drowned them. Chrysostom, exiled in the Caucasus, was never restored. He died there two years later. Four letters survive in Greek that he wrote from the road of his exile, thanking the Brescian bishop for what he had attempted and could not complete.

> *"Quos non potui de manu hostium liberare, debui certe attentasse."* — Gaudentius, on the failed legation: *those I could not free from the hand of the enemy, I was at least bound to have attempted to*
