## April 12

# Julius I, Pope

*Bishop of Rome, d. 352*

He was elected pope in 337, the year of Constantine's death, in the middle of the Arian crisis that would consume the fourth century. His predecessor Mark had governed for only ten months; his predecessor Sylvester had presided over Nicaea. Julius inherited a Roman Church that was both confidently orthodox and politically vulnerable: orthodox because the Roman clergy had always been Nicene, vulnerable because the imperial court at Constantinople had begun to favor the Arian party.

The crisis came to him almost immediately. The bishops of the East had, at a synod at Antioch in 339, deposed Athanasius of Alexandria — the chief defender of the Nicene formula — and replaced him with an Arian. Athanasius fled to Rome. Julius received him. He examined his case carefully and accepted his appeal. He summoned the Eastern bishops to come to Rome and answer for their conduct. They refused. Julius held the council anyway, in 341, with fifty Western bishops present, and reinstated Athanasius. He then wrote to the Eastern bishops a letter — the *Synodal Letter of Julius*, preserved by Athanasius — that contains the first explicit assertion in papal history that a bishop deposed by his own province may appeal to the see of Peter, and that the see of Peter cannot be bypassed in such cases. *Why was no notice given to us*, he asked the Eastern bishops, *especially concerning Athanasius? Or do you not know that this has been the custom — that the matter be referred to us, and then the right sentence be given?*

The letter did not end the controversy. Athanasius was exiled twice more after Julius's death. But the precedent was set. The Western Church had not abandoned Nicaea, and the Roman bishop had spoken plainly.

Julius died in 352 after a fifteen-year pontificate. He had built two great basilicas in Rome and had hidden a friend in his own household for seven years.
