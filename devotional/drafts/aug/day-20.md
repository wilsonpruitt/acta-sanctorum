## August 20

# Bernard of Clairvaux

*Cistercian abbot, doctor mellifluus, 1091–1153*

The AASS Augusti IV opens its massive entry — three biographers writing in succession during the saint's own lifetime, plus an apparatus of further sources running to half the August volume — with the observation that no medieval saint has more contemporary witnesses. He was born in 1091 at Fontaines-lès-Dijon, in his father Tescelin's small castle just north of the Burgundian capital. His mother Aleth bore seven children — six sons and a daughter — and Bernard was the third. The family was minor nobility: enough land to support a knight and his retinue, not enough wealth for any of the sons to expect more than a soldier's career. Aleth raised them strictly. She fasted herself the days she nursed them. She died when Bernard was nineteen, leaving him the most marked by her formation.

He decided for the cloister at twenty-two. He chose the strictest house in Christendom: Cîteaux, founded fifteen years before by Robert of Molesme as a return to the original Benedictine rule against the prosperity of the great Cluny houses. Under Stephen Harding the third abbot, Cîteaux was struggling. Bernard arrived at the gate in the spring of 1113 with thirty companions whom he had persuaded — four of his own brothers, several cousins, a small group of Dijon friends, all of whom he had argued out of military careers, marriages, and clerical preferments. Stephen Harding was at evening prayer in the small chapel when the porter announced that a young Burgundian noble was at the gate, with what he described as a small army.

Bernard was sent two years later to found the third daughter house of Cîteaux. Clairvaux — the *Clara Vallis*, the bright valley — was on the upper Aube, in difficult forested country, given by a relative of the Burgundian count. He was twenty-five when he was made abbot. He governed there thirty-eight years and did not leave the abbacy when the offers of episcopal sees came. He refused the see of Châlons twice. He refused Genoa, Milan, the archbishopric of Reims. He remained a monk in choir and an abbot in chapter for the rest of his life.

He preached. The eighty-six sermons on the *Song of Songs* that he composed and delivered at Clairvaux through the last twenty years of his life are the most extended single piece of monastic mystical commentary in the Western tradition. He wrote *De Diligendo Deo* — the small book *On Loving God* — that opens with the four sentences the medieval centuries learned by heart: *Causa diligendi Deum, Deus est; modus, sine modo diligere*. The cause of loving God is God himself; the manner, to love him without measure. He defended Innocent II against the antipope Anacletus II through eight years of papal schism, walking in person from city to city across Europe persuading the bishops and the kings. He preached the Second Crusade in 1146 by command of Pope Eugenius III, his own former pupil. The crusade failed; Bernard accepted the public blame for the failure with characteristic candor. He died at Clairvaux on the twentieth of August, 1153, sixty-three years old. The Cistercian order at his death numbered three hundred and forty-three houses.
