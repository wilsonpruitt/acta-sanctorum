## October 1

# Remigius of Reims

*Bishop, apostle to the Franks, c. 437–c. 533*

He was the third son of a Gallo-Roman senatorial family on the Marne, born at Laon around 437, the youngest of three children whose lives all left marks: his sister Latiana, mother of Bishop Lupus of Soissons; his elder brother Principius, bishop of Soissons before Lupus; and Remigius himself, made bishop of Reims at twenty-two over his loud objections, in a city whose Roman walls were already half-empty as the imperial administration of northern Gaul collapsed around him. He held the see for seventy-two years, the longest episcopate any of the Gallic chronicles record. He outlived the Empire he had been ordained in, the kingdoms of the Visigoths and Burgundians that filled the vacuum, and finally most of the Frankish chieftains who had divided the country once Childeric was gone.

What he is remembered for is one Christmas Eve. Clovis, the young Salian Frank who had killed his way to leadership of the western Franks at fifteen, married the Burgundian Christian princess Clotilde in 493; she had been working on him for four years; her two sons by him she had baptized over his shouted protests, the elder dying as an infant. In 496 Clovis was nearly defeated at Tolbiac by the Alemanni and, the chronicler Hincmar tells the story Gregory of Tours had told before him, called on his wife's God: *if you give me victory I will be baptized.* The Alemanni broke. He marched home to Reims. Remigius, who had been waiting for this conversation for ten years, took the king into the cathedral on the eve of the Nativity of 496 — three thousand of his warriors with him by the Frankish chronicles, perhaps fewer in fact — and baptized him with the famous formula that has been preserved without serious doubt: *Worship what you have burned and burn what you have worshipped.*

The Bollandists devote a long opening section to the difficulty of dating Remigius's other miracles and translations — three separate elevations of his body in three centuries — and to the manuscript tradition of his Vita. What survives the editorial care is the man behind the formula: a bishop who held one chair through every collapse and through the conversion of the people who would build Europe out of the rubble.

> *"Mitis depone colla, Sicamber: adora quod incendisti, et incende quod adorasti."* — Remigius to Clovis, at the font, 496

## October 2

# Leodegar of Autun

*Bishop blinded in the politics of the Merovingian collapse, c. 616–679*

He was a Frankish nobleman of the Burgundian aristocracy, raised at the court of the dowager queen Bathild who had been his guardian after his father's early death, educated at Poitiers under his uncle the bishop, ordained deacon, then archdeacon, then in 663 sent up the Saône to Autun as bishop of one of the most important sees in Burgundy. He stayed there for nine years and rebuilt the city. Autun in the 660s had been gutted by the dynastic fighting around the Merovingian throne; the cathedral was unroofed, the canonical residence was a ruin, the diocese had not had a settled bishop for three years. Leodegar repaired the buildings, restored the schools, fed the poor through three bad harvests, and held the western synod of Autun in 670 that issued the canons against the Monothelite heresy and the Trinitarian formulas the Western church would adopt for a thousand years.

Then Frankish politics caught up with him. Ebroin, the mayor of the palace under the boy-king Theuderic III, had been deposed in 673 in favor of a more moderate party; in 675 he came back, and he came back hunting the men who had deposed him. Leodegar had been one of them. The bishop was arrested at Autun in 676, brought to Ebroin's villa at Fécamp, blinded with a hot iron, his lips and tongue cut out, and held for two years in a Norman monastery to make sure he died slowly. He did not die. In 678 Ebroin had him pulled from the cell, given a hasty trial in absentia, deposed from his see, and beheaded in the forest of Sarcing in Artois.

His brother Gerin, who had stood by him through the whole captivity and refused to renounce him, was stoned to death in 676 at the same king's villa, in the gory parallel the Bollandists devote a long opening section to documenting. Their joint *acta* — the older Vita by an anonymous Autun cleric who knew Leodegar personally, then the longer recension by a monk of Saint-Symphorien — survive together. The translation of Leodegar's body to the abbey of Saint-Maixent in Poitou in 681 set off a cult that ran across half of France: more than two hundred medieval parishes were dedicated to him, and the village of Saint-Léger turns up in nearly every department.

He held his see for nine years and was tortured for three, and the tortured years are the ones the church remembered.

> *"Etiam si me totum lacerare voluerit, quod meum est non perdam."* — Leodegar, to Ebroin's interrogator

## October 3

# The Two Ewalds

*English missionaries among the continental Saxons, d. c. 695*

They had the same name. Bede distinguishes them only by the color of their hair — *Ewald the Black* and *Ewald the White,* dark and fair — and by the fact that the dark Ewald was the more learned of the two, a Scripture scholar of some accomplishment, while the fair Ewald was the more prayerful, a man of long silences. They had both been trained in Ireland, both ordained priest, and both heard in 692 that Willibrord was leaving for Frisia to do for the continent what Augustine had done in England a hundred years earlier. The Ewalds did not go to Frisia. They walked east instead.

They crossed into the country of the Bructeri, between the Lippe and the Ruhr, and stayed at the house of the local headman, who agreed to take them to the chieftain of his tribe. The headman delayed. The Ewalds sang the office every morning and the local people noticed; the news reached the warriors of the village before it reached the chieftain; the warriors decided that strangers who set up an altar in a borrowed cottage and sang in a language none of them knew were planning to convert their lord, and that conversions of lords meant the end of the old gods. They came for the Ewalds in the morning. The fair Ewald they killed first, by the sword. The dark Ewald, because of his learning, they tortured first and then dismembered. The bodies were thrown into the Rhine.

The chieftain, when he heard, ordered the village burned and a hundred and fifty of the inhabitants killed in penalty. The bodies of the Ewalds floated forty miles up the Rhine to the Christian settlement at Cologne, where they were recovered and buried by Pippin of Heristal, who had heard the report and sent men down the bank looking for them.

Two priests with the same name had walked into a country that was not asking for them, sung the office in a borrowed cottage, and died on a Sunday morning before they had baptized anyone. The mission they had begun without seeing the end of it was carried on by Suidbert, by Boniface, by the long generations after them. *No man knows the day,* the dark Ewald used to say of his own death, *but a man may know what he ought to do today.*

> *"A man may know what he ought to do today."* — Ewald the Black, in the older *Passio*

## October 4

# Francis of Assisi

*Founder of the Friars Minor, the Poor One, 1181–1226*

He was the son of a cloth merchant in Assisi, a vain and charming youth who spent his teens in extravagant suppers and his early twenties at war — first against Perugia, where he was captured and held for a year, then in arms again, then a long illness, then the sudden mid-riding return from a campaign he could no longer make himself care about. He was twenty-three when he kissed the leper outside the gates of Assisi and felt, he would say afterwards, *what had been bitter become sweet.* He was twenty-four when he heard the painted crucifix at San Damiano speak: *Francis, repair my house, which as you see is falling into ruins.* He took the words at their plainest, sold his father's bolts of cloth, and started to rebuild the chapel with his own hands. His father took him to court; he stripped naked in the bishop's presence and gave back the clothes; the bishop covered him with his own cloak.

The first companions came in 1208. By 1209 there were eleven of them, and Innocent III approved their first rule. The order grew with a speed neither Francis nor anyone else had foreseen. By 1217 there were five thousand brothers; by 1219 Francis was in Egypt with the Fifth Crusade, walking unarmed across no man's land to preach to the Sultan al-Kamil, who heard him for several days, refused to convert, but gave him safe passage out. He returned to find an order he could no longer govern, resigned the leadership, withdrew into the Umbrian hills.

In September 1224 on Mount La Verna, after forty days of fasting, he received in his own hands and feet and side the wounds of the Christ he had been preaching for sixteen years — the first stigmatic in church history, attested by the brothers who washed and dressed him in his last two years. He died at the Portiuncula on the evening of the third of October, 1226, lying naked on the bare ground at his own request, singing the *Canticle of the Sun,* in his forty-fifth year.

Three biographies were written within a generation. Most of what is in them, Thomas of Celano says, *I heard from his own mouth, or from faithful and tested witnesses.* The witness is what the church has loved. A man who took a verse at its plainest, who kissed what he had recoiled from, who heard a painted Christ ask for his house and gave it.

> *"Lord, make me an instrument of your peace."* — *the prayer attributed to him, fitting if not strictly from his pen*

## October 5

# Galla of Rome

*Senatorial widow, hidden mystic, c. 489–c. 546*

She was the daughter of Symmachus the consul, the great Roman statesman whom Theodoric the Goth executed in 525 along with her cousin Boethius — Symmachus had stood by his son-in-law to the end and paid for it. Galla was perhaps thirty when her father was killed; she had been recently married, recently widowed, and was suddenly the head of a household stripped of its protector by an Arian king with a long memory. She did not remarry. She gave away the family lands, kept enough to live on, walked across the Tiber to the cluster of small monastic houses that had grown up against the walls of Saint Peter's basilica, and asked to be received as one of them.

Gregory the Great, who had grown up in Rome hearing the Vatican community talk about her, devotes a chapter of his *Dialogues* to her. The story is small and quiet. She had developed, in middle age, a painful condition the physicians of Rome warned her was fatal — they had told her that she would have to remarry to remain healthy, that the celibate life would kill her. She thanked them and stayed. She lived another sixteen years. She slept in a small cell against the wall of the basilica, took the office every night with the brothers across the way, ran a hospice in her front room for the women among the Roman poor whose husbands had died in the Gothic wars and who had nowhere to go. *She was a woman of grave habits,* Gregory writes, *who so much preferred the love of God to the love of the world that she gave her thoughts to nothing else.*

In her last illness she saw, the night before she died, the apostle Peter coming to her cell. She asked him whether her sins were forgiven. *Veni,* he answered — *come.* She asked him to wait three days for her dearest friend, the abbess Benedicta, to be ready to come with her. He nodded; he came back three days later for both of them. Galla died first; Benedicta was buried beside her thirty days later, at the same Mass.

She built no order, wrote no book, founded no monastery, and held a hospice in her front room for sixteen years. The widow of a murdered consul kept a small cell against a basilica wall and lived there until Peter came for her. *She gave her thoughts to nothing else.*

> *"Veni."* — Peter to Galla, in the vision of her last days

## October 6

# Bruno of Cologne

*Founder of the Carthusians, the silent reformer, c. 1030–1101*

He was born into a noble Cologne family, sent south for his schooling at Reims, and stayed for thirty years — first as student of the cathedral school, then as its master, then as its director. He taught grammar, dialectic, theology; the men he trained included the future Pope Urban II, who would never forget him. In 1075 he was elected chancellor of the see of Reims under Archbishop Manasses, and in that office he discovered, slowly and with horror, that his archbishop was openly simoniacal — selling ordinations, appointments, dispensations — and that the diocesan administration had become a market. He spent five years pressing for reform from inside, was driven out of the city when Manasses had him exiled, was vindicated when the council of Autun deposed the archbishop in 1080, was offered the see itself, and refused it.

He was past fifty by then. He had decided, while in exile, that his life would not run any longer in the cathedral world. With six companions he walked south through the autumn of 1084 to ask the bishop of Grenoble — Hugh, his old student — for a wild place in the mountains where they could live as hermits under a common rule. Hugh took them up to a high alpine valley above the Guiers Mort river, four thousand feet in the air, snowbound seven months a year, with a rim of cliffs all around it. *Cartusia,* the place was called: the Charterhouse. They built seven small cells around a central church, kept silence except in choir, ate one meal a day, took their work alone in their cells, met only for the night office and the chapter of faults. The form they invented — neither cenobitic nor purely eremitic but a middle thing — survived them. The Carthusians have kept the same rule for nine hundred years, with so few changes that the saying *Cartusia numquam reformata, quia numquam deformata* — *never reformed, because never deformed* — has been taken into proverbs.

Bruno would not have liked the proverb. He was modest under praise, humble in office. In 1090 his old student Urban II called him to Rome as papal advisor; he came reluctantly, served briefly, refused the archbishopric of Reggio Calabria, withdrew further south to a wild Calabrian valley where he founded a second Charterhouse at La Torre. He died there on the sixth of October, 1101.

He left no rule in writing, no formal life. What he left was the silence the order has kept since. The cathedral master who would not be archbishop went up the mountain and built seven cells, and nine hundred years later they are still being lived in.

> *"O bonitas!"* — *the only saying his disciples preserved of him, his frequent ejaculation in choir: O goodness!*

## October 7

# Justina of Padua

*Virgin-martyr, patron of the Republic of Venice, d. probably c. 304*

Her *acta,* the Bollandists frankly concede, are late and unreliable — a tenth-century *passio,* a parallel account attributed to her teacher Prosdocimus, both clearly post-Lombard fabrications written when the original sources had been destroyed in the sack of Padua. What survives the editorial knife is a daughter of the Veneto Christian senatorial class, baptized in childhood by Prosdocimus — the legendary first bishop of Padua — and put to death by the sword on the seventh of October during the persecution of Maximian. The *acta* set the death-scene at the southern gate of Padua, by the marshes of the Bacchiglione, where the basilica that bears her name now rises.

Whatever the historical core, the cult is ancient and unbroken. The Lombard king Opilio raised the first basilica over her grave in the sixth century; the Benedictines took the adjacent monastery in the ninth; the great abbey of Santa Giustina, rebuilt and rebuilt, became one of the most important houses in northern Italy and the seed of the Cassinese reform that swept the Italian Benedictine world in the fifteenth century. The Republic of Venice took her as one of its patron saints — second only to Mark — and the Venetian fleet under Don Juan of Austria sailed to its great victory over the Ottoman navy at Lepanto on the morning of the seventh of October, 1571. The Christian galleys carried her image at their mastheads. The whole maritime league had been fasting on her vigil. Pius V, watching from a window in Rome, knew the battle had been won at the moment it was won. The day became, by his bull the next year, the feast of Our Lady of Victory — later renamed Our Lady of the Rosary — and the autumn calendar gained a Marian feast on the day Justina had held for thirteen hundred years.

The basilica at Padua still keeps her bones, beneath the immense Venetian-Renaissance dome at the head of the Prato della Valle. She is older than the cathedral that holds her, older than the city walls she lies inside, almost older than the church she belonged to: a girl of perhaps fifteen who would not pour wine on a pagan altar in 304, and whose silence has shaped one corner of Italian Christian memory ever since.

> *"Justina virgo, in cuius natalitio fugavit Christianus de mari Turcam."* — Pius V's own note, 1571

## October 8

# Demetrius of Thessalonica

*Soldier-martyr, the Megalomartyr of the East, c. 270–c. 306*

He was a Roman officer in the army of Maximian Galerius, born in Thessalonica to a senatorial Christian family that had so far escaped the imperial notice. The persecution that opened in 303 caught up with him in the autumn of 306, when his father, the consularis of Thessaly and Macedonia, died and the emperor appointed Demetrius to succeed him in the office. Demetrius accepted the appointment and used it to begin openly catechizing the Thessalonican Christian community in the porticoes of the city's bath-house, where his official duties gave him an excuse to gather crowds. Galerius, passing through the city for one of his winter campaigns, heard of it. He summoned Demetrius. The young officer admitted everything. He was confined to a cell beneath the public baths to await trial.

In the cell he was visited by a young Christian named Nestor who was about to fight in the amphitheater the next day against Lyaeus, the emperor's champion gladiator. Nestor came to Demetrius for a blessing. *In the name of the God of Demetrius,* he was to say at the start of the bout, and he did, and he killed the champion. Galerius, furious, had Nestor executed at once and ordered a squad of soldiers down to the cellar to dispatch Demetrius with their spears. They came at dawn and pierced him through the side. He bled out into the floor of the cellar.

The cellar where he died, eventually paved over and built into the foundation of a fifth-century basilica, became the most visited shrine in Greek Christianity outside Constantinople. The basilica that grew over it — the great five-aisled Hagios Demetrios in the upper city of Thessalonica, twice destroyed by fire and twice rebuilt — has held its place at the head of Greek hagiography for fifteen hundred years. Demetrius is the *megalomartyr,* paired in the East with Saint George; the patron of Thessalonica, whose icon was credited with defending the city against Avar, Slav, Bulgar, Norman, and Ottoman in turn; the *myrrh-streaming* one whose tomb, in the medieval centuries, was reported to weep an oil that healed sicknesses.

A young officer who used the privileges of his rank to teach the catechism in a public place, who blessed a friend going into a fight, who waited in his cell for the spears to come, has been remembered for a thousand seven hundred years.

> *"In the name of the God of Demetrius."* — Nestor, in the amphitheater at Thessalonica, 306

## October 9

# Denis of Paris and Companions

*First bishop of Paris, with Rusticus and Eleutherius, c. 250–c. 286*

He had been sent into Gaul, as Gregory of Tours preserves it, with six other bishops in the time of Pope Fabian — around 250 — to plant the church in the chief cities of the Roman north: Saturninus to Toulouse, Trophimus to Arles, Martial to Limoges, Gatian to Tours, Austremoine to Clermont, and Dionysius to Lutetia, the small island-city on the Seine that the Romans had begun to call Paris. He took with him a presbyter named Rusticus and a deacon named Eleutherius. They built the first chapel of the city on the right bank and began to baptize.

The persecution that caught them came perhaps thirty years later, under Valerian or under Maximian — the Bollandists conclude that the early 280s is most likely. The three were arrested together, brought before the prefect, scourged, racked, and finally beheaded on the high northern hill above the city — *Mons Mercurii,* the Latin called it, *Montmartre,* the hill of the martyrs, after them. The cephalophore legend that grew up around the death-scene — Denis bending down to pick up his own severed head and walking with it three Roman miles north to the place where he wanted to be buried, preaching as he went — first turns up in the ninth-century life by Hilduin of Saint-Denis, and the Bollandists handle it with patient skepticism. What they accept as older and more secure is the burial site itself: a little Christian cemetery in the Frankish villa of *Catulliacum,* north of Paris, where the bodies of the three were laid quietly in a wooden coffin, and where the chapel that grew over them eventually became, four hundred years later under Dagobert and again under Suger, the great basilica of Saint-Denis, the mausoleum of the kings of France.

The older *Passio* treats the three as the historical figures they were: a missionary bishop, his presbyter, and his deacon, who walked into a Roman city of strangers, established a chapel, baptized, were arrested, and died together on the same morning on the same hill. Their fellow-feeling was preserved in the burial: the three bodies in one trench, *unico tumulo* — *in a single grave.*

The kings of France would crown themselves in their basilica for a thousand years. The chapel on the right bank of the Seine became Notre-Dame. The mission three men had begun — bishop, presbyter, deacon, walking into a city that was not asking for them — outlived them by every measure they would have known how to count.

> *"Unico tumulo."* — *the older* Passio, *on the burial of Denis, Rusticus, and Eleutherius*

## October 10

# Paulinus of York

*First archbishop of York, missionary to Northumbria, c. 565–644*

He had been one of the second band of Roman missionaries sent into England in 601 — Augustine had asked Gregory the Great for reinforcements after the early successes of the Kentish mission, and Gregory had sent Mellitus, Justus, Paulinus, and Rufinianus along with the books and vestments and pallium that arrived at Canterbury that year. Paulinus stayed in Kent for twenty-four years as a working priest, learning the country and the politics, until in 625 the king of Northumbria — pagan Edwin, who had asked for the hand of the Christian princess Æthelburh of Kent — agreed as a condition of the marriage that his queen could bring her own bishop with her. Justus consecrated Paulinus on the twenty-first of July, 625, and put him on the road north with the wedding party.

Bede gives him the warmest portrait of any of the Italians. Paulinus was tall and slightly stooped — *gracilis statura, paululum incurvus,* working from the recollections of an old priest named Deda who had seen him — with a dark hawk-like face, very black hair, an ascetic thinness, and a voice quiet enough that those farther back in the hall had to lean forward to hear him. He preached for two years through the cold spring courts of Northumbria without converting the king. The vote came at the great council in 627 in the king's hall at Goodmanham, after the long argument the king's chief priest Coifi famously closed with the speech about the sparrow flying through the lighted hall in winter — *brief is the warmth, soon is the wind on the other side.* The court accepted the new religion. Edwin was baptized at York that Easter.

Paulinus baptized for the next six years up and down the Northumbrian rivers — in the Glen at Yeavering, in the Swale at Catterick, in the Trent at Littleborough — and built the first stone cathedral at York around the wooden chapel where Edwin had received the sacrament. The Northumbrian conversion held until the autumn of 633, when Cadwallon of Gwynedd and Penda of Mercia together overthrew Edwin at Hatfield Chase. The king was killed; the queen and Paulinus and the children fled south by sea to Kent. He never returned. He served as bishop of Rochester for the rest of his life — eleven more years, quietly, in the Kentish coastal town — and died there on the tenth of October, 644, perhaps eighty years old.

The cathedral he had built at York burned, was rebuilt, burned, and rebuilt again. The men he had baptized in the rivers became the grandfathers of the Northumbrian renaissance Bede grew up inside.

> *"Brief is the warmth; soon is the wind on the other side."* — *the unnamed counselor's sparrow-speech in Bede,* on the day Edwin's court accepted the gospel

## October 11

# Bruno the Great

*Archbishop of Cologne, brother of an emperor, scholar-prince, 925–965*

He was the youngest son of Henry the Fowler, the Saxon duke who had been elected king of the East Franks in 919 and whose sons would build the empire of the Ottos. Otto I, who was to be crowned emperor at Rome in 962, was thirteen years older; Henry of Bavaria was nine years older; Bruno was the fourth child, the dynastic spare, and his parents — perhaps his mother Mathilda, who had her own Saxon piety — set him aside for the church when he was four years old. He went to the cathedral school at Utrecht at six. He stayed in school for the next twenty-five years.

What he became was the first great scholar-prince of the Ottonian renaissance. He read Greek — not just letters but the philosophers and the Cappadocians; he assembled the largest private library north of the Alps in his own day; he kept up a correspondence with the literary luminaries of the time. When Otto, having beaten the Hungarians at Lechfeld in 955, decided he needed a brother in the see of Cologne to anchor the Rhineland, Bruno was appointed archbishop in 953, at twenty-eight, and was simultaneously named duke of Lotharingia, the long border province from the North Sea down to the Vosges. He held both offices for twelve years and held them well.

He kept his cathedral school open seven days a week to anyone who could read; he reformed the Rhenish abbeys with the new Cluniac discipline coming up from Burgundy; he patronized the manuscript ateliers that produced the great Ottonian gospel-books of Reichenau and Echternach; he settled three civil wars in his own duchy without resort to arms. He wrote, in the long winter evenings, lives of his predecessors in the Cologne see, and at his death his secretary Ruotger — who had served him for eleven years — wrote his life back. The *Vita Brunonis* by Ruotger is one of the great documents of the tenth century, gracious and unsparing.

He died at Reims on the way back from putting down a Lotharingian rebellion, on the eleventh of October, 965, at forty years old. The fourth son the family had given to the church returned to the family the order it had needed: a Latin prince who had been first a scholar, who held both crozier and sword for twelve years and never let either eclipse the other, and whose library was the quiet thing the empire had not known it required.

> *"Sapientia ædificavit sibi domum."* — *the verse Ruotger took for the epitaph: Wisdom has built herself a house*

## October 12

# Edwin of Northumbria

*King and martyr, the convert Paulinus baptized, c. 586–633*

He was the son of Ælle, king of Deira, the southern half of Northumbria, and grew up in exile after his father's defeat by Æthelfrith of Bernicia in the late 590s — first in the kingdom of Gwynedd among the British, then at the court of Rædwald of East Anglia, where the young exile spent his late twenties waiting for the chance to claim his inheritance. The chance came in 616 when Æthelfrith, alarmed by Rædwald's protection of his rival, marched south to demand Edwin be handed over for execution. Rædwald refused, met Æthelfrith at the river Idle in Nottinghamshire, killed him in battle, and put Edwin on the throne of a reunited Northumbria — Bernicia and Deira together, the largest English kingdom, stretching from the Humber to the Forth.

He governed for seventeen years and brought the kingdom to a peace it had not known in a generation. Bede preserves the famous detail: Edwin set up brass drinking-cups at the wayside springs along the Roman roads of Northumbria, *and no one,* the historian writes, *would dare touch them but for the use intended,* such was the order of the kingdom. In 625 he asked for the hand of Æthelburh, the Christian princess of Kent. Her brother sent her north on condition that she could keep her religion and bring her own bishop, and the bishop they sent with her was Paulinus.

It took Paulinus two years. The famous council of 627 — the great hall at Goodmanham, the speech of the unnamed counselor about the sparrow flying through the lighted hall in winter, the chief priest Coifi's own dramatic ride out to throw a spear into the pagan temple — closes the conversation. Edwin was baptized at York the next Easter, with his daughter Eanflæd and most of his court. The decade that followed was the high point of early-medieval English Christianity: a baptized king, a missionary bishop, six years of travelling the Northumbrian rivers baptizing crowds.

It ended at Hatfield Chase in October 633. Cadwallon of Gwynedd, the British king, allied with the pagan Penda of Mercia and marched against Northumbria. They met Edwin and his sons at the marsh-edge of Hatfield south of the Don. Edwin was killed in the line; his elder son Osfrid was killed beside him; the younger son was captured and murdered later by Penda. The body was decapitated, the head taken back as a trophy. A monk of Whitby — perhaps a man Paulinus had baptized — recovered the head some time later and brought it to York, where it was buried in the porch of the cathedral Edwin had begun. The relics survive nowhere now; the cult of the king who had been baptized and martyred for his baptism survived him by a thousand years.

> *"And no one dared touch them but for the use intended, such was the order of his kingdom."* — Bede, on the brass drinking-cups Edwin set at the wayside springs

## October 13

# Theophilus of Antioch

*Sixth bishop of Antioch, apologist of the second century, c. 115–c. 183*

He was born in the country east of the Euphrates, in the small Syriac-speaking villages around Edessa, and came to faith as an adult — *I too,* he writes near the end of the third book *Ad Autolycum,* *was once an unbeliever, until I read the prophets and the gospels and was persuaded by the Holy Spirit.* He was elected sixth bishop of Antioch around 169, succeeding Eros — sixth, that is, in the line that began with Peter — and held the see for fourteen years through the long persecution of Marcus Aurelius and the brief reprieve under Commodus. The Latin tradition remembers him on October 13.

He wrote three books *Ad Autolycum* — *To Autolycus,* a pagan friend who had pressed him for a defense of Christianity against the standard charges of atheism, immorality, and historical novelty — and the books survive intact, perhaps the only complete second-century apology that does. They are slow, patient, often surprising. The first book defends the Christian doctrine of God by the via negativa: *if you say to me, show me your God, I will say to you, show me your man — show me the man behind your eyes, behind the speech that is spoken, behind the desires you cannot see — show him to me, and I will show you my God.* The second runs through the Old Testament chronology against the Greek myths and concludes with the line that gave Christian theology one of its most permanent words. *In a similar way the three days before the lights of heaven were created are figures of the Trinity — God, his Word, and his Wisdom.* It is the first time the word *triad* — *trinitas* — appears in surviving Christian writing.

He wrote also a treatise against Marcion, a refutation of Hermogenes, several catechetical works, and the earliest commentary on the gospels in Greek of which we have notice. None of those survive. What survives is the three books to a friend who had asked an honest question and got, slowly and with great courtesy, an honest answer.

He died at his post around 183, perhaps in his late sixties, and was buried at Antioch in the cathedral he had administered. He was the bishop who first set down what every catechumen since has been taught to confess: that the God who made the world is one God in three. The word came to him not from a council but from a friend's question, and from the patience to answer it three books long.

> *"Show me the man behind your eyes, and I will show you my God."* — Theophilus, *Ad Autolycum* 1.2

## October 14

# Callistus I

*Pope, freed slave, contested architect of Roman discipline, c. 160–222*

He had been born a slave in the household of a wealthy Christian named Carpophorus, sometime around 160, and entrusted in his early manhood with the management of a small Roman bank his master had set up to receive the deposits of poor Christians. The bank failed. The deposits were lost. Callistus, blamed and probably innocent, was caught fleeing on a ship to Portus, beaten, sentenced to the treadmill, and eventually shipped to the lead mines of Sardinia for what was meant to be the rest of his short life. The empress Marcia, the Christian concubine of Commodus, secured a general pardon for the Christian prisoners around 190; Callistus came home a marked man, settled on the small church-stipend Pope Victor offered him, and rose through the deaconate under Pope Zephyrinus, who eventually trusted him enough to make him the administrator of the great Christian cemetery on the Appian Way that has borne his name ever since. He was elected pope on Zephyrinus's death in 217.

His five-year pontificate became the most controversial of any third-century Roman bishop. Hippolytus, the most learned theologian of the Roman church, refused to recognize Callistus's election and set himself up as anti-pope — the first in the history of the Roman see — and his *Refutation of All Heresies* attacks Callistus by name as a man who was readmitting penitent adulterers to communion, ordaining men who had been married more than once, and tolerating clandestine marriages between Roman matrons and Christian slaves whom they could not lawfully marry under Roman law. Callistus replied that the church was not a club of the perfect but a hospital, and that the keys had been given to bind and to loose. He stood by the policy. The third-century Latin church, slowly and against Hippolytus's bitter opposition, ratified him.

He died, the *acta* preserve, in a popular riot rather than an imperial persecution — beaten in his own house in Trastevere by a mob who had been told the Christians were responsible for a recent flood of the Tiber, thrown out a high window with a stone tied around his neck, dragged into a well, and covered over with rubble. The presbyter Asterius recovered the body that night and buried him in the cemetery on the Aurelian Way, on the fourteenth of October, 222. Santa Maria in Trastevere — built where his house had stood — keeps his name on the dedication still.

The slave who had administered the bank for poor Christians governed the Roman church for five years and was killed by a Roman mob, and the policy he insisted on — that mercy is not weakness — has not been revoked.

> *"Misericordia exaltat se super judicium."* — Callistus, in Hippolytus's hostile paraphrase: *mercy exalts itself over judgment*

## October 15

# Teresa of Ávila

*Discalced Carmelite reformer, Doctor of the Church, 1515–1582*

She was born at Ávila to a family of *converso* descent — her grandfather had been reconciled to the Inquisition in 1485 — and grew up the most charming of nine children in a strict Castilian merchant household. Her mother died when she was twelve. She read romances of chivalry through her teens — *I have come,* she would later tell her sisters, *from many vanities* — and ran away to a Carmelite convent at twenty, partly out of vocation and partly to escape her father's plans for her marriage. The convent at the Encarnación held a hundred and eighty nuns under a relaxed observance; she stayed there for twenty-six years; she was bored for most of them. Her conversion came at thirty-nine, in front of an *Ecce Homo* in a chapel corner — *the very God of my heart,* she writes in the *Vida.* The mystical experiences began in her early forties.

She was forty-seven when she founded the first reformed Carmelite convent — San José of Ávila, four nuns in absolute enclosure under the original Carmelite rule — against the resistance of the diocese, the city council, and her own provincial. She would found sixteen more in the next twenty years. The reform was hard and meant to be hard: discalced, strict poverty, perpetual fasting, no large dowries, no relaxations. She walked or rode mule-back across Castile, La Mancha, Andalusia, Catalonia, and Aragon to plant houses in town after town, in summer heat and winter snow, sometimes sleeping in stables, often arriving at midnight to a building her advance party had not yet been able to make habitable. *Lord,* she said once on the road in the rain, *if this is how you treat your friends, no wonder you have so few.* She wrote, in the same years, the *Vida,* the *Way of Perfection,* the *Foundations,* and the *Interior Castle* — four of the great works of Spanish prose.

The *Interior Castle* — a vision of the soul as a great crystal globe with seven mansions inside it, the king dwelling in the innermost — is the work the church has loved most. She died on the road at Alba de Tormes on the fourteenth of October, 1582 (the calendar reform took effect at midnight; her death-day is dated the fifteenth in the new calendar), at sixty-seven, from exhaustion. *I am a daughter of the church,* she said when she was dying. The seventeen houses she had founded were five hundred within a century. Paul VI made her, in 1970, the first woman Doctor of the Church in fifteen hundred years.

A nun bored for twenty-six years, converted at thirty-nine in front of a painting, founded an order, wrote four masterpieces, and died on a road. The patience that came before was the soil the rest grew from.

> *"I am a daughter of the church."* — Teresa, repeated several times in her last hours

## October 16

# Gall of St. Gallen

*Irish hermit-monk, apostle of the Allemanni, c. 550–c. 645*

He was the youngest of the twelve Irish monks who left Bangor in Ulster around the year 590 with their abbot Columbanus to evangelize the continent. They went first to Brittany, then up the Rhone to Burgundy, then over the Alps to Lake Constance — a journey of thirteen years, through three kingdoms and four languages, planting monasteries as they went. By 612 they had reached Bregenz on the eastern shore of the lake, where Columbanus's abrasive insistence on the Irish style of penance had finally put him beyond the patience of the Frankish-Burgundian queen Brunhilde. The little community broke up. Columbanus crossed the Alps south into Lombardy and founded Bobbio. Gall, who had fallen ill with a fever and could not travel, was left behind with two Allemanic disciples and his abbot's parting rebuke — *as long as I live, you shall not say Mass* — for his refusal to follow.

He recovered slowly through the winter of 612 in a small wooden cell near Arbon. In the spring he walked east into the heavily-forested upper Steinach valley with the deacon Hiltibod, found a clear stream and a high spring above a cliff, set the foundation-stones of a chapel, and stayed for the rest of his life. The legend preserves the small details: the bear who tried to take his fire and whom he rebuked into bringing him firewood instead and who was venerated for centuries afterward in the abbey arms; the local Allemanic chieftain who gave him the surrounding woodlands when he saw the cell; the Frankish bishop of Constance who tried to make him take the see and whom he refused with a smile. He kept his abbatial silence after Columbanus's death in 615, when a Bobbio messenger arrived with the abbot's pastoral staff and the news that the rebuke had been lifted. He said his first Mass at sixty-five.

He died perhaps in 645, an old man among his Allemanic disciples, and was buried in the wooden chapel he had built. A century later the abbey of Saint Gall would rise on the spot — one of the great monastic foundations of Carolingian Europe, the model house of the Benedictine reform north of the Alps, the producer of the *Plan of St. Gall,* of the *Antiphonary,* of historians and hymn-writers, of a school whose graduates staffed the cathedrals of Cologne and Mainz and Liège for three hundred years. The valley that had been an empty forest when Gall set down his stones has been a center of Christian Europe for a thousand four hundred years.

The disobedient disciple who would not cross the Alps, who stayed where he was sick and built a hut, planted the most fruitful Irish foundation on the continent.

> *"Maneas in Christo, frater, ego in his terris peregrinabor."* — Gall to Columbanus, on parting at Bregenz: *Stay you in Christ, brother; I will pilgrim in these lands*

## October 17

# John Colobus

*John the Dwarf, abba of Scetis, c. 339–c. 409*

He was a peasant boy from the Egyptian Thebaid, four feet tall by the most generous estimate — *colobos* in the Greek, *parvus* in the Latin, the *dwarf,* the *little one,* a name that stuck for the rest of his life and a thousand years afterwards. He went out into the desert of Scetis as a teenager, around 357, and put himself under the discipline of the great old Pambo, who took him as a novice in the same little circle that included Macarius the Egyptian and Moses the Ethiopian — the founders of the form of monasticism that would shape the desert tradition for the next sixteen centuries.

The story is the famous one — repeated in the *Apophthegmata,* repeated in Cassian, repeated by every spiritual master from Benedict to Dorotheus of Gaza. Pambo took the new novice out into the dry valley below the cell, planted a dead walnut staff in the sand, and said *water this every day until it fruits.* The nearest well was three miles away. John walked with the bucket every morning before the office and every evening before sleep. He kept walking for three years. The branch budded, leafed, and fruited. The old men of Scetis, when they heard, came out and ate the walnuts and called the tree *the tree of obedience.*

He stayed in Scetis for fifty years. He was famous in the desert for the smallness of his body and for the precision of his answers in the *Apophthegmata patrum.* *Abba,* a young brother once asked him, *what should I do with this temptation?* John told him: *sit in your cell, and your cell will teach you everything.* Another brother: *what is the most necessary virtue?* He answered: *humility, because where humility is, there is no temptation that can stand against it.* Another: *I have many evil thoughts.* John told him: *the wind blows where it will; you cannot stop it; but you can keep your sails reefed.* When the desert-raiding Mazikes broke up the Scetis communities in 408, John fled to Suez with the surviving brothers and died there, perhaps in his seventy-first year, longing to return to the cell he had not been allowed to keep.

He had founded no monastery, written no book, governed no community larger than the brothers in his immediate cell. The dead branch he had watered for three years outlived him by twelve hundred years.

> *"Sit in your cell, and your cell will teach you everything."* — John Colobus, in the *Apophthegmata patrum*

## October 18

# Luke the Evangelist

*Physician, gospel-writer, companion of Paul, fl. c. 50–80*

He was a Greek of Antioch — born at the great cosmopolitan city on the Orontes that had become, by his lifetime, the third city of the Roman Empire and the cradle of Gentile Christianity. He was a physician, trained at the medical schools that had grown up around the temple of Asclepius in his hometown. He was probably a freed slave, perhaps the household physician of one of the Antiochene Christian patrons whose names Acts preserves — perhaps even of the household of Theophilus to whom he eventually addressed both his books. The Christian community in Antioch met him as an adult convert sometime in the early fifties of the first century, and the apostle Paul met him there shortly afterward.

He travelled with Paul. The famous "we-passages" of Acts — *we sailed away from Philippi after the days of unleavened bread, and came unto them to Troas in five days* — are the moments where Luke, who has been writing in the third person about the apostle's journeys, slips back into the first plural and signals that he had been there himself. He was on the second missionary journey from Troas to Philippi; he stayed at Philippi for the next seven years; he rejoined Paul on the third journey and travelled with him to Jerusalem; he went with him on the long sea-voyage to Rome that fills the last chapters of Acts; he stayed at Rome through the apostle's first imprisonment and was apparently with him for the second one too. *Luke alone is with me,* Paul writes from his death-cell in 2 Timothy 4:11. The two had been together, by then, for seventeen or eighteen years.

He wrote his gospel and the Acts of the Apostles in those years — both of them dedicated to a Theophilus who may be patron, fellow-physician, or simply an honored Greek reader. The gospel is the most literary of the four and the most attentive to women, to the poor, to the Samaritans, to the *anawim* of late-Second-Temple piety; it preserves the *Magnificat,* the *Benedictus,* the *Nunc Dimittis,* the prodigal son, the good Samaritan, the Emmaus road. The Acts that follows is the only narrative source we have for the first thirty years of the church, and the apostolic missions outside Judea would be largely opaque to us without him.

After Paul's death he travelled, the older traditions preserve, in Greece, in Achaia, in Bithynia, where he died at seventy-three years old, *plenus Spiritu Sancto,* full of the Holy Spirit. His body was eventually translated to Padua, where it has lain in the basilica of Santa Giustina (next to the city's other patron, eleven days back in this calendar) for fifteen hundred years. The physician who stayed when others left wrote the two books on which the church's memory of its own beginning depends.

> *"Luke alone is with me."* — Paul to Timothy, 2 *Timothy* 4:11

## October 19

# Peter of Alcántara

*Discalced Franciscan reformer, mentor of Teresa of Ávila, 1499–1562*

He was born at Alcántara on the Tajo, the small Extremaduran town whose Roman bridge still spans the river, the son of a noble family in straitened circumstances — the father was a lawyer, the mother had brought a small dowry, the boy was educated by the Augustinians at Salamanca and entered the Observant Franciscans at sixteen. He was already known in the convent for the fact that he ate one meal every three days, slept ninety minutes a night, and never lifted his eyes when he walked — *I never knew the color of the wall of my cell,* he would say in old age, *for I never raised my head.* He was ordained priest at twenty-five. He preached for twenty-five years through the Extremadura and Andalusia, gathering a small group of followers around him who wanted to live the original Franciscan rule before the relaxations had set in.

In 1554 he founded the first house of his reform at Pedroso, in the Sierra Morena, with seven friars: barefoot, eating only what was begged door to door, sleeping on bare boards in cells four feet by six, keeping nightly vigil. The houses spread; by 1561 there were sixteen of them; the *Alcantarine* reform of the Franciscans became the most rigorous branch of the order in the Spanish-speaking world for the next four hundred years.

His most consequential single work, however, was a friendship. In 1559 the Carmelite nun Teresa of Cepeda y Ahumada, then in her early forties at the convent of the Encarnación at Ávila, was being investigated by the Inquisition for the unsettling visions she had begun to have and for the equally unsettling proposal she was making to break away from her own community and found a stricter house of Carmel. She was alone, terrified, and on the edge of being silenced. Peter of Alcántara, then sixty and the most respected ascetic in Spain, came to Ávila at the request of Bishop Mendoza to interview her. He spent three days with her in the convent parlor. He came out persuaded that the visions were of God, the proposal was of the Holy Spirit, and the Inquisition needed to leave her alone. *He understood me by experience,* Teresa would write in the *Vida,* *which is the only thing here below that altogether suffices.* He stayed her champion for the next three years until his death.

He died at the friary of Arenas de San Pedro on the eighteenth of October, 1562, kneeling in his cell, standing and praying — though he was sixty-three he looked, his brothers said, like a man of ninety. Teresa had a vision of him at the moment of his death three hundred miles away in Ávila and recorded it. He had told her once, near the end, *daughter, all of this and the world besides will pass; only love endures.* The reform he had begun outlasted him by four hundred years; the friend he had defended outlasted him in another way altogether.

> *"Daughter, all of this and the world besides will pass; only love endures."* — Peter of Alcántara, to Teresa, in the parlor at Ávila

## October 20

# Acca of Hexham

*Northumbrian bishop, scholar of Bede's circle, c. 660–740*

He had been raised in the household of Bishop Bosa of York, taken into the cathedral school as a child, brought along when Wilfrid — the great, contentious bishop of York whose long career filled the second half of the seventh century in Northumbria — moved his episcopal household first to Ripon, then back to York, then to Hexham on the south Tyne. Acca was Wilfrid's secretary, then his deacon, then his chief presbyter, then in 709 his successor as bishop of Hexham. He held the see for twenty-four years.

What he gave the Northumbrian church in those years, by Bede's grateful accounting, was a school and a library. The cathedral library at Hexham under Acca was the best in northern England — the great Roman manuscripts Wilfrid had brought back from his pilgrimage journeys, the Greek codices Theodore of Tarsus had passed to him through Canterbury, the patristic and historical works Acca had himself copied or imported from the continental abbeys. Bede, who lived twelve miles down the road at Jarrow, regarded Acca as a personal friend and dedicated five of his works to him: the commentaries on Mark, on Luke, on the *Hexaemeron,* on the *Song of Songs,* on the *Acts of the Apostles.* The dedicatory letters survive. They are warm, technical, full of the precise gratitude one scholar owes another who has lent him books. *Te animarum medicum nominari mihi licet,* Bede writes in the prologue to *In Marcum* — *I may be permitted to call you the physician of souls.*

Acca was also the last bishop of Hexham to govern it as a free see. In 732 the Northumbrian succession-fight that had brought King Ceolwulf to the throne caught up with him; he was deposed, perhaps for political reasons. He spent the last eight years of his life in some kind of internal exile — Bede says only that he left Hexham. He died on the twentieth of October, 740, and was brought back home for burial. The cathedral he had loved kept his bones in two carved stone crosses set up at the head and foot of his grave outside the south wall — the *crosses of Acca,* the antiquarians called them; one survives in the Hexham museum, half its eight feet of carved interlace and acanthus still legible after thirteen centuries.

The library he assembled was scattered when the Vikings burned Hexham in 875, and his books were lost; the cathedral he had governed survived the burning and was rebuilt. The man who lent Bede the books has not had to lend the church any more — what Bede made of them is what survives.

> *"Te animarum medicum nominari mihi licet."* — Bede to Acca, prologue *In Marcum:* *I may call you the physician of souls*

## October 21

# Hilarion of Gaza

*Father of Palestinian monasticism, the Western pupil of Anthony, c. 291–371*

He was born to pagan parents in the village of Thabatha, five miles south of Gaza in the Roman province of Palestine, and was sent as a teenager to the Christian schools of Alexandria. He was fifteen when he made his way out into the desert beyond Memphis to find Anthony — already, by 306, the most famous monk in the world — and stayed with him for two months. He came home to find his parents dead. He sold the small inheritance, distributed it to the poor and to his married brother, and walked seven miles down to the wilderness behind Maiuma, the port of Gaza, on the Mediterranean shore. He was sixteen. He stayed in that wilderness, in increasing isolation, for the next sixty-five years.

The *Vita Hilarionis* by Jerome — written around 390, less than twenty years after Hilarion's death, drawn directly from the recollections of his companion Hesychius — is one of the most loved of all the early monastic biographies. It is also one of the funniest. Jerome paints a young man who lived for thirty years on twelve dried figs a day, who slept on a reed mat in a hut made of mud and straw four feet high, who broke a vase of incense over a band of bandits who had come to steal his hut and persuaded them to repent and become monks themselves, who refused every visitor who came for healing and healed them anyway, who once raced a chariot in the games at Gaza on the prayer of a Christian charioteer and beat the pagan champion with the *signum crucis* drawn over the wheels.

He was driven out of Palestine in his sixties by the crowds — he could not get any solitude — and spent the last fifteen years of his life on the run from his own fame. He went to Egypt; he was found out. He went to Sicily; he was found out. He went to Dalmatia; the demoniacs queued at the harbor with their relatives. He went finally to Cyprus. The brothers at Paphos hid him in a small cave above a stream three days' ride into the mountains. He stayed for five years, surrounded only by his old companion Hesychius, and died there at eighty years old, on the twenty-first of October, 371.

The boy who had walked seven miles down the Mediterranean coast at sixteen had founded, by living a particular sort of small life there, the entire Palestinian and Syrian monastic tradition. Sabas, Euthymius, Theodosius — the great fathers of the next century — all looked back to Hilarion as their *abba.* The fame he ran from for fifteen years has been, for sixteen hundred years, the only thing about him most readers know.

> *"O Anima mea! Egredere; quid times? Egredere, quid dubitas?"* — Hilarion's death-prayer in Jerome: *Out, my soul; why do you fear? Out, why do you hesitate?*

## October 22

# Abercius of Hierapolis

*Bishop, traveller, author of his own epitaph, c. 120–c. 200*

He was bishop of Hierapolis in Phrygia in the second half of the second century — successor of Papias, who had been a disciple of John the Apostle, and writing before the persecution of Septimius Severus had begun. The acts the Bollandists print are full of the standard hagiographic apparatus — the demoniacs cured, the well that sprang up at his prayer, the trip to Rome to exorcise the daughter of Marcus Aurelius — and the editors handle the legendary material with patience. What survives the editorial knife, what no one disputes, is the epitaph he wrote for himself.

The *Inscription of Abercius* — first quoted in the late-fourth-century Greek life, then partly recovered in stone in 1883 by the Scottish archaeologist William Ramsay in the wall of a public bath at Hieropolis — is the oldest substantial Christian inscription that survives. It is twenty-two hexameter lines, in the *koine* Greek of the eastern Roman world. It was carved on the marble sarcophagus Abercius prepared for himself in his late seventies, on the eve of one of his pastoral journeys, and the slab now hangs in the Vatican Museum.

*A citizen of an elected city,* the epitaph begins, *I made this in my lifetime that there might be in plain view a place to receive my body. My name is Abercius, the disciple of a holy shepherd who feeds his flocks on the mountains and on the plains, who has great eyes that look down everywhere, who taught me the trustworthy writings.* The shepherd is Christ. The writings are the gospels. *He sent me to royal Rome to behold a queen with golden robe and golden sandals; and a people I saw there having a shining seal. I saw the plain of Syria and all the cities — Nisibis after I crossed the Euphrates. Everywhere I had brothers. Paul I had with me; faith led me everywhere; everywhere food was placed before me — the fish from the spring, mighty and pure, which the chaste virgin caught and gave to her friends to eat continually, having sweet wine and giving the cup with the bread.*

The fish from the spring is Christ. The chaste virgin is the Church. The cup with the bread is the Eucharist. The epitaph is, in a single block of second-century marble, a complete profession of Christian faith — Trinitarian, sacramental, ecclesial, eucharistic — set into the gravestone of a Phrygian bishop a hundred years before the persecutions ended and three hundred years before any of the great creeds were written. He died around the year 200, at perhaps seventy-five. The marble slab he had cut survived. What he had wanted plainly visible — *in plain view,* the epitaph says — has been visible ever since.

> *"My name is Abercius, the disciple of a holy shepherd who feeds his flocks on the mountains and on the plains."* — *Inscription of Abercius,* lines 3–4

## October 23

# Severinus of Cologne

*Gallo-Roman bishop on the Rhenish frontier, c. 340–c. 403*

He was born in Aquitaine and sent north as a young presbyter to the Rhine frontier in the 360s, when Valentinian I was rebuilding the limes and the Roman administration of the Rhineland was still standing, just barely. He succeeded Euphrates as bishop of Cologne sometime around 370, perhaps in his early thirties, and held the see for the next thirty-three years.

What he inherited was a frontier church under siege. The Cologne diocese ran from the small Roman colony at *Colonia Agrippina* north along the lower Rhine to the Frisian marshes, and east into the German forests where the Franks and the Allemanni were beginning the slow westward pressure that would, within his lifetime, push across the Rhine for good. The Christian community Severinus inherited had been founded perhaps two generations earlier, was perhaps two thousand strong in a city of forty thousand, and was hemmed in by an Arian Gothic minority on one side and a still-pagan Frankish military presence on the other. He governed it for thirty years with a steady, unspectacular, practical capacity. He built the basilica of SS. Cornelius and Cyprian on the south side of the city. He resettled the small church on the north side that the Frankish raids of 388 had burned. He buried, when no other bishop would come, the Christian dead of the great Frankish raid of 392. He kept the cathedral school open through years when the supply of papyrus from the south was unreliable.

The *Vita* preserves the small detail Gregory of Tours preserves first: that Severinus was at the office in his cathedral one Sunday morning in November of 397 when, at the moment of the *agnus Dei,* he stopped, looked up, and said audibly to his archdeacon, *Listen — do you hear the angels singing? My brother Martin of Tours is dead.* The archdeacon, who wrote the report down for the cathedral chronicle, marked the day. The news that Martin had died at Candes that morning reached Cologne by post-courier two weeks later. The dating matched.

He died perhaps in 403 and was buried in the basilica he had built. The basilica that grew out of the cult of his tomb, *Sankt Severin* in the south of the old city, is still there, a fourteenth-century Gothic church on Roman foundations on Carolingian foundations on the original tomb-shrine. The Cologne diocese he had governed survived the Roman collapse, survived Charlemagne, survived the Reformation, and is one of the oldest continuously functioning dioceses in northern Europe. The Aquitanian who had been sent up to a frontier see for thirty years had, simply, kept it.

> *"Audi: angeli canunt. Frater meus Martinus migravit ad Dominum."* — Severinus, in the cathedral at Cologne, on the morning of Martin's death

## October 24

# Felix of Thibiuca

*North African bishop, refused to surrender the books, d. 303*

The first edict of Diocletian, posted at Nicomedia in February of 303, reached Roman North Africa in the early summer. It commanded the Christian clergy to surrender their *libri sacri* — the codices of the gospels and the epistles, the working bishopric's most valuable physical possession — for public burning. Those who complied became known, in the bitter Latin that followed, as the *traditores,* the *handers-over.* Those who refused became the proto-martyrs of the persecution. Felix, bishop of the small *municipium* of Thibiuca in Africa Proconsularis — about thirty-five Roman miles southwest of Carthage — was the first North African bishop publicly to refuse.

The trial survives in detail. Felix was sixty-six years old, bishop for some years; the local *curator,* Magnelianus, was an old neighbor, and the conversation between them in the city forum was constrained by familiarity as much as by procedure. *I have the books,* Felix said. *Bring them out and burn them,* Magnelianus answered. *I will not.* *Then surrender them to me.* *I will not.* *Why?* *Because it is better that I should be burned than they.*

He was sent in chains to Carthage, then held a fortnight while the proconsul Anulinus tried persuasion, then put on a ship to Italy when the proconsul declined to execute him in his own province. The execution was carried out at Venusia in southern Apulia — the small inland town where Horace had been born three centuries earlier — by the local magistrate on instructions from Rome. Felix was beheaded on the thirtieth of August, 303. The day his community at Thibiuca received the news from Italy is what the African calendar afterwards observed.

Five of his deacons and lectors died with him: Audactus, Januarius, Fortunatianus, Septiminus, and a sixth whose name varies in the manuscripts. They too had refused. *Better that I should be burned than they* became one of the small permanent sayings of the African church. Within a generation the *traditores* who had handed over their codices for the fire would become the central case of the Donatist controversy that tore the African church apart — and the men of Thibiuca who had not handed them over would be the heroes both sides could agree on.

> *"Better that I should be burned than they."* — Felix, in the forum at Thibiuca

## October 25

# Gaudentius of Brescia

*Bishop, advocate of John Chrysostom, c. 360–c. 410*

He was a presbyter at Brescia in northern Italy under Bishop Philastrius, and grew up in his teacher's household, learning the patristic tradition and the spoken Latin of fourth-century Cisalpine Gaul. In the late 380s he set out on a long pilgrimage to the East. He went to Cappadocia, where the biographer of Basil of Caesarea gave him a portion of the relics of the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste; he went down to the Holy Land; he spent time at the new Latin monastic foundations beginning to grow in Bethlehem and Jerusalem. While he was on the road Philastrius died, and the Brescian church — without consulting him, against his repeated protests by letter — elected him bishop in absence. He returned, the eastern bishops having refused him communion until he agreed to come home, and was ordained by Ambrose of Milan in 388.

He governed the see for twenty years. The twenty-one sermons that survive — preached at Brescia, recorded by his hearers — are among the great pieces of late-fourth-century Italian preaching. He preached on the eucharist, on the paschal vigil, on the difficult sayings of the Lord, on the saints whose relics he had brought back from the East. He built a basilica on the south side of Brescia to house those relics, the *Concilium Sanctorum,* the first major reliquary church of northern Italy.

His most consequential act was a journey he could not refuse. In the spring of 405 the western bishops, scandalized by the deposition of John Chrysostom from Constantinople, sent a high-level legation to the emperor Arcadius to demand his restoration. Innocent I of Rome chose Gaudentius to lead it. He went, with two Roman senators, on a winter voyage from Brindisi to Athens to Constantinople. The embassy was a disaster. Arcadius would not receive them. They were detained three months, refused audiences, threatened, finally put on a leaky boat and sent home through a storm that nearly drowned them. Chrysostom, exiled in the Caucasus, was never restored. He died there two years later. Four letters survive in Greek that he wrote from the road of his exile, thanking the Brescian bishop for what he had attempted and could not complete.

> *"Quos non potui de manu hostium liberare, debui certe attentasse."* — Gaudentius, on the failed legation: *those I could not free from the hand of the enemy, I was at least bound to have attempted to*

## October 26

# Cedd of Lastingham

*Northumbrian missionary bishop, brother of Chad, d. 664*

He was the eldest of four brothers raised at the monastic school on Lindisfarne under Aidan in the 640s — Cedd, Cynebill, Caelin, Chad — all four of them eventually ordained, all four of them missionary priests, all four in the front line of the Bernician evangelization of the next generation. Bede, who had grown up in the monastery Cedd's brother Chad would later govern at Lastingham, gives the family the warm portrait of book three of the *Historia Ecclesiastica.*

He was sent south in 653 at the request of King Peada of the Middle Angles, who had married a Northumbrian Christian princess and wanted missionaries for his country. He worked the South Yorkshire and Derbyshire moorlands for two years; Penda's death at Winwaed in 655 ended the period of pagan Mercian dominance and opened the south for the gospel; Cedd was reassigned to the East Saxons in 656 at the request of King Sigeberht the Good. He spent the next eight years founding the church of Essex from scratch.

He worked from a small wooden chapel built into the ruined Roman fortress of *Othona* on the Blackwater estuary — the *Saxon shore* fort the Romans had built in the third century to guard against North Sea raiders, abandoned for two hundred years, the long sea-walls still standing. The chapel he raised on the foundations of the fortress chapel — the chapel of *Saint-Peter-on-the-Wall,* perhaps the oldest still-standing church in England — is still there, the Roman tile and stone of its walls visible in the seventh-century masonry. From Bradwell-on-Sea he ordained priests, built churches up the rivers, organized a small Essex diocese, and went home each summer to Lindisfarne for the long northern monastic chapter.

In 664, returning from the Synod of Whitby — at which he had been one of the conciliators between the Roman and the Celtic Easter parties — he caught the plague that was sweeping the country and died at his own foundation at Lastingham on the North York Moors. His brother Chad came down to bury him and then stayed on as the new abbot. Bede preserves the small detail that Cedd had founded Lastingham on what had been a pagan burial ground; the brother had cleansed it with forty days of prayer and fasting before laying the foundation. The cleansing held. The abbey lasted four centuries. The chapel at Bradwell-on-Sea, looking out over the cold North Sea where it has stood for thirteen hundred years, has not had to be rebuilt.

> *"Locus iste sanctus est, in nomine Domini factus."* — *the Lastingham foundation prayer Bede preserves of Cedd*

## October 27

# Frumentius of Ethiopia

*Apostle of the Aksumites, first bishop of Ethiopia, c. 305–c. 380*

He was a young Christian Tyrian boy travelling on a Red Sea trading-ship with his uncle Meropius, an Alexandrian philosopher hired to map the unsurveyed coastlines of the African and South Arabian shores for the imperial geographers. The boy was perhaps twelve years old; his older brother Edesius, perhaps fourteen, was also on board. Sometime around 316 the ship put in for water at a small port on the African coast of the Red Sea. The local tribe, hearing that the Romans had broken their treaty with the Aksumite kingdom, attacked the ship. The crew was killed. The two boys, found sitting under a tree on the shore reading their books, were spared and brought up to the king at Aksum.

The king of Aksum was Ella Amida. He took to the boys. He kept Edesius as his cup-bearer and Frumentius as his secretary and treasurer. They lived in the palace for the next twenty years, learning Ge'ez, the court administration, the trade routes south to the Nile and east to Yemen. When Ella Amida died around 330, leaving an infant son under the regency of the queen mother Sophia, the queen begged the two foreigners to stay on until the boy Ezana came of age. They agreed. For the next decade Frumentius effectively ran the kingdom.

He used the office to plant the gospel. The Roman traders coming through Aksum had been Christian for two generations; Frumentius gave them land for chapels, exempted them from taxes for liturgical purposes, encouraged them to gather Sunday assemblies in the towns where they did business. By the time Ezana came of age around 340 there was a substantial Christian community in Aksum and along the coast at Adulis. The two brothers asked permission to go home. Edesius went back to Tyre; Frumentius went down to Alexandria.

He went to see the new patriarch — Athanasius, just elected at thirty-three — and laid the situation in front of him. He did not ask for himself; he asked for a bishop. Athanasius listened and answered, in the line Rufinus preserves: *And what other man shall we find as fit, in whom is the Spirit of God as in you, who can finish what you have begun?* He consecrated Frumentius bishop of Aksum on the spot. Frumentius spent the next forty years building the Ethiopian church, baptizing King Ezana in the late 340s, training the first generation of Ethiopian-born clergy, translating the gospels into Ge'ez. Ezana set the cross on his coinage. The Ethiopian Orthodox Church, the second-oldest national Christian community after Armenia, traces its episcopate to him. They call him *Abuna Salama,* the Father of Peace.

> *"Et quem alium inveniemus virum talem, in quo sit Spiritus Dei sicut in te?"* — Athanasius to Frumentius, in council, c. 340

## October 28

# Simon and Jude

*Apostles, the Zealot and the brother of James, fl. c. 30–c. 70*

The synoptic gospels list them together at the end of the Twelve, the last two before Judas Iscariot. The Simon of October 28 is *Simon the Cananaean,* called *the Zealot* in Luke and Acts — the surname probably reflecting an earlier political affiliation with the militant Galilean nationalist movement that would, three decades after the Lord's death, lead the great Jewish revolt against Rome. The Jude is *Judas of James* in Luke's list, *Thaddaeus* in Mark and Matthew — a younger brother of the apostle James the Less, perhaps a cousin of the Lord through the family of Mary of Clopas, perhaps the author of the short canonical letter that bears his name.

The gospels say almost nothing of either of them apart from the name in the list. John preserves one line of Jude in the long discourse at the Last Supper: *Lord, how is it that thou wilt manifest thyself unto us, and not unto the world?* — a question that draws from the Lord one of the most searching answers of the upper room. *If a man love me, he will keep my words: and my Father will love him, and we will come unto him, and make our abode with him.* Of Simon the Cananaean John records nothing. They are, in the gospel narrative, members of a quiet majority — apostles who walked with the Lord for three years, ate with him at the supper, ran from the garden, gathered in the upper room, received the Spirit at Pentecost, scattered into the world to preach.

The early traditions have them work first separately and then together — Simon in Egypt and the Maghreb, Jude in Edessa and Mesopotamia, both of them eventually in Persia, where they joined forces and converted the eastern Persian *magi* before being killed together at the city of Suanir, perhaps in the early 70s. Jude was clubbed; Simon was sawn in two. The legend gives them their iconographic attributes — the saw and the club, on every Western altarpiece since the Middle Ages — but their joint feast on the twenty-eighth of October goes back to the seventh century in Rome and the fourth in the East, and rests on the older and more durable tradition that they died together for the gospel they had together believed.

The two apostles whose names are barely on the page held to that gospel through a generation longer than most. The Lord's quiet promise to Jude in the upper room — *we will come unto him and make our abode with him* — was the promise he is now remembered to have lived inside.

> *"Lord, how is it that thou wilt manifest thyself unto us, and not unto the world?"* — Jude, in the upper room, *John* 14:22

## October 29

# Narcissus of Jerusalem

*Bishop of the Holy City, accused and vindicated, c. 99–c. 215*

He was elected the thirtieth bishop of Jerusalem around the year 180 — twenty-sixth in the line, by Eusebius's count, after the twelve original Hebrew Christian bishops who had governed the church before the Bar Kokhba revolt of 135 destroyed the original community. The see he inherited had been quietly rebuilt in the long second century by the small Greek-speaking community that had moved back into Aelia Capitolina, the new Roman city Hadrian had built on the ruins of Jerusalem. Narcissus governed it through the Quartodeciman controversy and signed the decisive Palestinian council under Theophilus of Caesarea around 195 that aligned the eastern churches on the Sunday-after-Passover reckoning Rome had insisted on.

Eusebius preserves the most famous story about him. One Easter eve the deacons discovered, late on Holy Saturday, that there was no oil for the lamps for the great paschal vigil — the supply had run out, the markets were closed, the congregation was already gathering. They came to Narcissus in distress. He told them to fill the lamps with water from the cathedral well; he prayed over it, blessed it, and instructed them to light the lamps. The water burned as oil. The vigil proceeded. Eusebius adds that several of the brothers preserved samples of the water-turned-oil for many years afterwards.

The other famous story is darker. Three local enemies of Narcissus concocted a charge against him so grave and obscene that the *Historia Ecclesiastica* declines to specify it. They each swore to it under the oldest form of imprecatory oath: the first that he might be burned alive if he were lying, the second that he might be eaten with the wasting disease, the third that he might lose his eyes. The Jerusalem church, knowing Narcissus's life, did not believe them. Narcissus, however, weary of the controversy and longing for the contemplative life he had wanted as a young man, slipped away into the desert and was not seen for some years. The community, eventually concluding he had died, elected three successive bishops in his absence. Then Narcissus reappeared. The three accusers had, in the meantime, all suffered the curses they had sworn. The first had been burned to death in a house-fire; the second had wasted away; the third, seeing the fates of the first two, had publicly confessed the perjury and wept himself blind.

Narcissus resumed the see, governed it some years more, and finally — by then, Eusebius says, in his middle hundreds — accepted Alexander of Cappadocian Caesarea as his coadjutor. He died perhaps in his hundred-and-seventeenth year, certainly past one hundred. The successor he had asked for would die in Decius's persecution two generations later, the chair of James still rooted where Narcissus had set it.

> *"That sinners may not say in their hearts: there is no judgment."* — *Eusebius's gloss on the fates of Narcissus's accusers*

## October 30

# Marcellus the Centurion

*Roman officer, refused the imperial sacrifice, c. 260–298*

He was a centurion of the Legio VII Gemina, the Trajanic legion that had been stationed at León in Roman Spain for two centuries and was, by the late third century, the principal garrison of the Iberian peninsula. He had been a Christian since at least his early manhood — perhaps from childhood; the family was Christian — and had served in the legion for at least fifteen years without conflict between his religion and his rank, since the Roman army of the period required no specifically pagan act of its soldiers as a condition of service. Maximian's persecution edicts of 297 changed that. The standing orders required every soldier to participate in the public sacrifice on the emperor's birthday — to eat the meat from the altar, to pour the libation, to acknowledge the imperial divinity. The birthday came around on the twenty-first of July, 298.

Marcellus's legion, then on garrison duty at Tingis (Tangier) in Roman Mauritania, was assembled in the parade-ground for the imperial sacrifice. The wine was poured; the altar was lit; the troops formed up. Marcellus stepped out of the line, unbuckled his *cingulum militare* — the broad bronze-fitted leather belt that was the soldier's badge of rank — and laid it on the ground in front of the altar with his sword and his vine-staff on top of it. *I am a Christian,* he said. *I cannot serve the emperors except by serving Christ. From this day on I cease to follow your gods of wood and stone, who are deaf and dumb idols.* The standard-bearers stared. The other centurions shouted at him to pick up the belt. He left it where it lay.

He was arrested on the spot, held a week, and brought before the deputy prefect of the African provinces, Astasius Fortunatus, who tried for four hours to talk him into recanting. The court protocol is unusually full and unusually patient — Astasius was a humane administrator and clearly did not want to execute a fifteen-year veteran with a clean record. He kept offering alternatives: leave of absence, transfer, a quiet retirement. Marcellus answered each with the same line. *It is not lawful for a Christian to bear arms for the things of this world.* Astasius, finally exhausted, transferred the case to the higher court and let Marcellus be charged formally with desertion. He was beheaded outside the city walls of Tingis on the thirtieth of October, 298, by the local centurion's *vitis* — the same vine-staff he had himself once carried.

A court-recorder named Cassian, present for the sentencing, threw down his wax tablets and refused to write the death-warrant. He was killed with Marcellus the next month. Their two *acta* travel together in every Carthaginian recension. The fifteen-year centurion who would not pour the wine has been remembered in the Roman martyrology for seventeen hundred years.

> *"Non licet Christiano homini molestiis sæcularibus militare, qui Christo Domino militat."* — Marcellus, in the courtyard at Tingis, the line his judge could not get him to retract

## October 31

# Quintinus of Vermand

*Roman senator, missionary to the Belgae, martyr of Picardy, c. 260–c. 287*

He was a Roman of the senatorial class, son of one of the consular families of the second century, well-educated in the household, baptized as a young man under Pope Caius around 282. He went up into Gaul with the great Roman missionary band that the Gallic chronicles call *the seven bishops* — Denis to Paris, Saturninus to Toulouse, Trophimus to Arles, Paul to Narbonne, Martial to Limoges, Gatian to Tours, Lucian to Beauvais — and Quintinus, the youngest of them, walked north with Lucian into the *Augusta Veromanduorum,* the Roman frontier-town that the medieval French would call Saint-Quentin after him. He was perhaps twenty-five.

He preached for two or three years up and down the Somme valley with what success the figures put at six hundred conversions in a single afternoon at the central forum — the standard hagiographic round number, but the Roman senator's Latin and manners would have given him a hearing among the half-Romanized Belgic gentry of the region, and the Christian community of Vermand was real and substantial by the time the persecution caught up with it. The persecution was the small private hunt of a single Roman officer named Rictiovarus, *præfectus prætorio Galliarum* under Maximian, who carried his charge with a personal viciousness none of the historians of the period could quite explain. He hunted down the missionary clergy of northern Gaul one at a time. He took Quintinus in 287.

The trial-acta are circumstantial in a way that suggests they descend from a real court protocol, much elaborated. Quintinus was beaten with leaded scourges, then racked, then stretched between two stakes; boiling oil was poured down his back; nails were driven through his fingers; quicklime, mustard, and vinegar were forced down his throat; eventually two iron bars were driven through his body from neck to thigh. Through all of it, the interrogator could get only the same answer: *Christianus sum.* When the prefect finally ordered the beheading, on the thirty-first of October, the *acta* preserve the small detail the Bollandists love most: a white dove rose from the body and disappeared into the morning sky. Rictiovarus, perhaps superstitiously, perhaps simply tired, ordered the body weighted with lead and dropped into the Somme just downstream of the bridge.

Fifty-five years later the body was found by a blind Roman noblewoman named Eusebia who had been instructed in a vision to dredge the river at the bend below the bridge. She found it uncorrupt, lifted it out, was healed of her blindness when she touched it, and gave it to the local Christian community for burial. The basilica that grew over the burial became the cathedral of the diocese the Carolingians would rebuild around it in the ninth century, and the medieval town that grew around it took the saint's name. The Roman senator who had walked north to evangelize a foreign country has been the patron of that country for seventeen hundred years.

> *"Christianus sum."* — Quintinus, in the long interrogation at Augusta Veromanduorum
