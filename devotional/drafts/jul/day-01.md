## July 1

# Julius of Caerleon

*British martyr, c. 304*

The AASS Julii I lists him at the head of its day-one calendar: *S. Julius, Martyr in Britannia.* The Bollandists accepted Julius as a substantive entry but deferred the often-paired Aaron of Caerleon to other treatment. This entry follows their judgment and gives Julius alone, with the testimony of Gildas and Bede — the two oldest extant British sources — on which the AASS itself relies.

He was a Roman citizen of *Isca Silurum*, the walled administrative center of Roman Britain west of the Severn, the place now called Caerleon on the river Usk in south Wales. He was a Christian of standing in the town — not a soldier of the legion (which had been moved out a generation before), but a citizen of the *colonia*, with the property and legal protections that name implied. He had been baptized for some years by the time the Diocletianic edict of 303 reached the western provinces.

The edict ordered the Christians of the empire to offer the imperial sacrifice. The western Caesar Constantius Chlorus enforced it with notable mildness — he confiscated church buildings; he is not known to have demanded a single death — but any local magistrate who chose to enforce it could. The magistrate at *Isca* did. Gildas, writing in the sixth century, preserves the single sentence: Alban at Verulamium two hundred miles east, Aaron and Julius at Caerleon. The three names are the entire memory British history kept of the persecution. All three were beheaded outside the town walls, on the slope above the river, in the year 304 or thereabouts.

Bede copies Gildas in the first book of his *Ecclesiastical History*, and adds nothing the British chronicler could not see. The British church kept Julius's memory through the Saxon conquest, the Welsh withdrawal into the western country, and the centuries of broken communion with Rome. When Augustine of Canterbury arrived in 597, the British bishops he met at *Augustine's Oak* on the Welsh marches were Julius's far heirs. A small parish church on the slope above the Usk at Caerleon still bears his name. The AASS, having so little to print, gave him the chair-place in the calendar of July.

> *"Aaron and Julius, citizens of the City of the Legions, who in this same persecution endured most noble martyrdom."* — Bede, *Ecclesiastical History* I.7, AASS Julii I
