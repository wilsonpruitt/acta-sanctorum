## February 1

# Brigid of Ireland

*Abbess, patroness of Ireland, c. 451–525*

She was born somewhere in Leinster to a pagan Irish chieftain named Dubthach and a Christian slave woman named Brocca. The pairing was not voluntary on her mother's side. When Dubthach's wife learned of the pregnancy she demanded the slave be sold off, and Brocca was sent away to give birth in another man's house; Brigid, when she was old enough, was brought back to her father's holding as a serving girl. She gave away his food, his clothes, and eventually a jeweled sword to a beggar. He decided to sell her too. He drove her in a chariot to the court of the king of Leinster; while he negotiated her price, she gave the king's chariot ornaments away. The king refused the sale. *No one*, he said, *can purchase her as a servant; she is the bride of Christ.*

She took the veil from the elderly bishop Mac Caille — the tradition holds that Mac Caille, distracted, recited the words for the consecration of a bishop over her by accident, and Brigid alone of Irish women is therefore reckoned as bearing episcopal grace. Around the year 480 she founded a community at the oak of Kildare. It became the largest and longest-lasting double monastery in the Celtic Church: men and women living separately under one abbess, in two chapels and one Rule.

The miracles in her legend are domestic. She turned a vat of bathwater into beer for visiting clerics. She hung her wet cloak on a sunbeam to dry. She fed every poor person who came to the gate, and the supplies in the storehouse never ran low. The stories are old enough that some of them have probably blurred with the older Celtic goddess of the same name; what is certainly hers is the kindness so consistent that the Irish kept telling stories about it for fifteen hundred years.

She died at Kildare, well past seventy. The Irish put her on the same shelf as Patrick.
