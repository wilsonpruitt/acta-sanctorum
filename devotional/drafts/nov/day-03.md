## November 3

# Hubert of Liège

*Bishop of the Ardennes, the stag in the forest, c. 656–727*

He was a Frankish nobleman of the Aquitanian royal house — perhaps the eldest son of Bertrand of Toulouse, the last duke of Aquitaine before the Carolingian absorption — born around 656 to the world of court intrigue and forest hunting that the Frankish *grandes* lived in the long century of decline before Pepin and Charles Martel. He was raised at the court of King Theuderic III. He married a Bavarian princess named Floribana, by whom he had a son. He served, in his early thirties, as a count of the palace under Pepin of Herstal. He was a famous hunter — Bishop Jonas of Orléans's ninth-century *Vita* preserves the family memory of him in this period: red-haired, light-eyed, expert with hawk and hound, fond of his own horses, occasionally given to anger.

The conversion came on a Good Friday in his late thirties. The court had assembled for the great spring stag-hunt in the forest of the Ardennes; Hubert had ridden out alone before dawn, against his confessor's advice, to course a great white stag he had been tracking for weeks. The chase carried him deep into the forest. The stag, when at last he closed on it in a clearing high above the Lesse river, turned and faced him; between its antlers, the *Vita* says, was a luminous cross. The stag spoke: *Hubert, why are you hunting me, when you should be hunting your own salvation?* Hubert dismounted. The cross was still there. He went home that evening, gave away his hounds and his horses, told his wife and his son, and walked east into the wilderness with no possessions and no plan.

He took shelter at the small monastic foundation of Lambert at Maastricht. Lambert was the bishop of the Tongres-Liège diocese — a missionary see that ran from the upper Meuse north into Frisia and east into the German forest country — and Lambert took the new convert in, taught him the office, ordained him priest, and over the course of a dozen years made him the indispensable second-in-command of the Maastricht church. When Lambert was assassinated in 705, Hubert was elected his successor against his strenuous protest. He governed the see for twenty-two years.

What he did with the office was missionary work. The Ardennes forest he had once hunted in was still half-pagan in the early eighth century — small Frankish villages with sacred groves and standing stones, a thin overlay of Christian baptism on a mostly older religion — and Hubert spent twenty years on horseback going from clearing to clearing, baptizing, building chapels, ordaining local priests, settling disputes, healing the dying. The cathedral he built at Liège on the banks of the Meuse, where he had translated Lambert's body and where the city of Liège grew around the basilica he had raised, became the seat of a diocese that has not moved since. He died of a hunting-injury infection at Tervueren on the thirtieth of May, 727; the cult-day was moved to the third of November, the day his body was translated to Liège.
