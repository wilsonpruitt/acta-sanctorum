## October 7

# Justina of Padua

*Virgin-martyr, patron of the Republic of Venice, d. probably c. 304*

Her *acta,* the Bollandists frankly concede, are late and unreliable — a tenth-century *passio,* a parallel account attributed to her teacher Prosdocimus, both clearly post-Lombard fabrications written when the original sources had been destroyed in the sack of Padua. What survives the editorial knife is a daughter of the Veneto Christian senatorial class, baptized in childhood by Prosdocimus — the legendary first bishop of Padua — and put to death by the sword on the seventh of October during the persecution of Maximian. The *acta* set the death-scene at the southern gate of Padua, by the marshes of the Bacchiglione, where the basilica that bears her name now rises.

Whatever the historical core, the cult is ancient and unbroken. The Lombard king Opilio raised the first basilica over her grave in the sixth century; the Benedictines took the adjacent monastery in the ninth; the great abbey of Santa Giustina, rebuilt and rebuilt, became one of the most important houses in northern Italy and the seed of the Cassinese reform that swept the Italian Benedictine world in the fifteenth century. The Republic of Venice took her as one of its patron saints — second only to Mark — and the Venetian fleet under Don Juan of Austria sailed to its great victory over the Ottoman navy at Lepanto on the morning of the seventh of October, 1571. The Christian galleys carried her image at their mastheads. The whole maritime league had been fasting on her vigil. Pius V, watching from a window in Rome, knew the battle had been won at the moment it was won. The day became, by his bull the next year, the feast of Our Lady of Victory — later renamed Our Lady of the Rosary — and the autumn calendar gained a Marian feast on the day Justina had held for thirteen hundred years.

The basilica at Padua still keeps her bones, beneath the immense Venetian-Renaissance dome at the head of the Prato della Valle. She is older than the cathedral that holds her, older than the city walls she lies inside, almost older than the church she belonged to: a girl of perhaps fifteen who would not pour wine on a pagan altar in 304, and whose silence has shaped one corner of Italian Christian memory ever since.

> *"Justina virgo, in cuius natalitio fugavit Christianus de mari Turcam."* — Pius V's own note, 1571
