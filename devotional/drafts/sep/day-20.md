## September 20

# John of Egypt

*Blind confessor, martyr at Caesarea, d. 310*

He was an Egyptian Christian sent into the copper mines of Phaeno around 308, in the same group that took Paphnutius — the same disfiguring sentence had been applied to him: the right eye gouged with a sharpened iron, the left leg crippled with a hot blade, then the desert mines for what was meant to be the rest of his short life. John was already blind in both eyes by the time the executioners came; the older injury seems to have been from an earlier round of torture, and the cauterizing of the second eye was, the Bollandists note from Eusebius, simply the standard sentence applied to a man on whom it could no longer change anything. He was sent to the mines all the same.

What Eusebius preserves of him in book thirteen of the *Martyrs of Palestine* is one of the strangest and most intimate portraits of an early-fourth-century saint. John could not work the rock — by then he could not see, could not stand straight — and was kept apart from the labor gangs with a few other infirm confessors, in a cell where they prayed and fasted together. Eusebius, who knew him personally, says that John had memorized the entire Bible. The whole of it. Not on stone tablets, the historian writes, *not on parchment that worms and time consume, but in the tablets of the heart, in the soul itself, white and clear, in the pure light of the mind.* When the small assembly of confessors gathered for prayer John would stand in the middle of them and recite — sometimes from Moses, sometimes from the prophets, sometimes a stretch of gospel or epistle — and Eusebius, the first time he heard him, walked forward thinking the blind man must be reading from a book. He was not. He was reciting from inside himself.

In the autumn of 310 the prefect Maximinus, frustrated that the disfigured Christians were not dying as fast as he had wished, ordered them all executed together. Thirty-nine of them, including the bishop Silvanus of Gaza and the blind John, were beheaded outside Caesarea on a single morning. Eusebius watched. *I saw,* he wrote afterwards, *what truly is a man — not what appears in the outward shape of the body, but what dwells in the soul and in the mind.*

> *"Quippe corpore licet mutilato, nihilominus majorem facultatis suæ vim ac virtutem exerebat."* — Eusebius, *de Martyribus Palæstinæ* 13: *though his body was disfigured, the more strength of soul he showed*
