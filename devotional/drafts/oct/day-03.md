## October 3

# The Two Ewalds

*English missionaries among the continental Saxons, d. c. 695*

They had the same name. Bede distinguishes them only by the color of their hair — *Ewald the Black* and *Ewald the White,* dark and fair — and by the fact that the dark Ewald was the more learned of the two, a Scripture scholar of some accomplishment, while the fair Ewald was the more prayerful, a man of long silences. They had both been trained in Ireland, both ordained priest, and both heard in 692 that Willibrord was leaving for Frisia to do for the continent what Augustine had done in England a hundred years earlier. The Ewalds did not go to Frisia. They walked east instead.

They crossed into the country of the Bructeri, between the Lippe and the Ruhr, and stayed at the house of the local headman, who agreed to take them to the chieftain of his tribe. The headman delayed. The Ewalds sang the office every morning and the local people noticed; the news reached the warriors of the village before it reached the chieftain; the warriors decided that strangers who set up an altar in a borrowed cottage and sang in a language none of them knew were planning to convert their lord, and that conversions of lords meant the end of the old gods. They came for the Ewalds in the morning. The fair Ewald they killed first, by the sword. The dark Ewald, because of his learning, they tortured first and then dismembered. The bodies were thrown into the Rhine.

The chieftain, when he heard, ordered the village burned and a hundred and fifty of the inhabitants killed in penalty. The bodies of the Ewalds floated forty miles up the Rhine to the Christian settlement at Cologne, where they were recovered and buried by Pippin of Heristal, who had heard the report and sent men down the bank looking for them.

Two priests with the same name had walked into a country that was not asking for them, sung the office in a borrowed cottage, and died on a Sunday morning before they had baptized anyone. The mission they had begun without seeing the end of it was carried on by Suidbert, by Boniface, by the long generations after them. *No man knows the day,* the dark Ewald used to say of his own death, *but a man may know what he ought to do today.*
