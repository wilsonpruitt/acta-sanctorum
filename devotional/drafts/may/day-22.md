## May 22

# Rita of Cascia

*Augustinian widow, c. 1381–1457*

She was born to elderly peasants in the Umbrian hill village of Roccaporena and married at twelve, against her wish, to a quarrelsome local man. He was, by every account, harsh — sharp-tongued in the streets and at home. She bore him twin sons. She kept the house. She prayed at dawn at the rough wooden cross on the hill behind the village. After eighteen years the husband was ambushed and killed in one of the local feuds that fed on each other for generations in those mountains. Rita's two sons, by then in their late teens, swore the vendetta in the customary form, naming the killers.

She did the thing for which Cascia remembers her. She prayed that her sons would die before they could fulfill the oath. They both fell sick within months, both of natural illness, and both — by the testimony she gave the chronicler — confessed and were reconciled before they died. She was widowed in her thirties, childless, alone.

She tried three times to enter the Augustinian convent of Santa Maria Maddalena in Cascia and was turned away — the house did not take widows, and the family feud still hung in the air. The biographer says her three patrons (John the Baptist, Augustine, and Nicholas of Tolentino) brought her by night through the locked gate; the nuns, finding her in the chapel at Matins, took her in. She lived there forty years. Toward the end of her life, kneeling before a crucifix and asking to share even a little of Christ's suffering, she received a thorn wound in her forehead — a single point of pain that did not heal, and that she carried until she died, setting it aside only during her journey to Rome.

She died on May 22, 1457. The wound closed. The white bees that had hovered at her cradle when she was a baby did not need to come again.

> *Asked what the worms falling from her wound might be, she answered, with a laughing face, that they were her little Angels.* — from her Life
