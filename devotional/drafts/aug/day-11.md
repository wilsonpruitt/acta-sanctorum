## August 11

# Alexander the Charcoal-burner

*Bishop of Comana, martyr, third century*

The AASS Augusti II tells the whole story from one source: Gregory of Nyssa's *Oration on the Life of Gregory Thaumaturgus*. The setting is the city of Comana in Pontic Polemoniac — a small town on the river Iris in the territory of Neocaesarea, where Gregory the Wonderworker was bishop in the middle decades of the third century. The Christian community at Comana, which had grown rapidly under his influence, sent a delegation begging him to come and consecrate a bishop for them. He came. He spent some days teaching among them. When the moment came to choose, the citizens could not agree.

They put forward many candidates. Gregory passed over each in turn, asking the citizens whether they had not yet considered a man more suitable. At last someone, in evident sarcasm, said: well, what about Alexander the coal-burner? — and pointed across the open square at the black-faced figure at his cart, sooty from the kilns, dressed in rags, looking, the witty citizen said, more fit to be ridiculed than to be ordained. The crowd laughed. Gregory did not. He asked that the man be brought forward.

Alexander came. He had, Gregory of Nyssa says with care, the body of a young man at the flower of his strength, but the body was beneath a covering of soot and coarse cloth designed precisely to draw no attention. The Wonderworker took him aside. They talked. Alexander, the *Vita* preserves, was no peasant. He was a philosopher of substantial education and noble family who had chosen extreme poverty and disguise as a discipline — both to keep his body in hard labor for the sake of virtue, and to hide the physical beauty he believed dangerous to his chastity, and to make a livelihood whose every coin could be given as alms to the poor. *Non urgente paupertate ad ejusmodi vitæ genus devenerat:* he had not been driven to this kind of life by need. He had chosen it. He had been hiding under the bushel, and Gregory had come to put him on the candlestick.

Alexander was bathed, given a fresh tunic, examined for orthodoxy, and consecrated bishop of Comana the same week. He served the see for the rest of his life — perhaps twenty years — and is remembered chiefly for two things in Gregory of Nyssa's narrative. His sermons drew crowds from neighboring cities; the rusticated philosopher who had hidden his learning gave it back, when given the office, in a Greek so clear that even illiterate hearers understood. And he was killed in the persecution that fell on Pontus in the third century — *per ignem consummato martyrio*, his course completed by fire. The man who had blackened his face with the smoke of the kilns was at last consumed by a fire he had not lit.

> *"Small in his own eyes, vile and despised in the world, but truly great before God."* — Gregory of Nyssa, on Alexander
