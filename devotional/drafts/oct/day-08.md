## October 8

# Demetrius of Thessalonica

*Soldier-martyr, the Megalomartyr of the East, c. 270–c. 306*

He was a Roman officer in the army of Maximian Galerius, born in Thessalonica to a senatorial Christian family that had so far escaped the imperial notice. The persecution that opened in 303 caught up with him in the autumn of 306, when his father, the consularis of Thessaly and Macedonia, died and the emperor appointed Demetrius to succeed him in the office. Demetrius accepted the appointment and used it to begin openly catechizing the Thessalonican Christian community in the porticoes of the city's bath-house, where his official duties gave him an excuse to gather crowds. Galerius, passing through the city for one of his winter campaigns, heard of it. He summoned Demetrius. The young officer admitted everything. He was confined to a cell beneath the public baths to await trial.

In the cell he was visited by a young Christian named Nestor who was about to fight in the amphitheater the next day against Lyaeus, the emperor's champion gladiator. Nestor came to Demetrius for a blessing. *In the name of the God of Demetrius,* he was to say at the start of the bout, and he did, and he killed the champion. Galerius, furious, had Nestor executed at once and ordered a squad of soldiers down to the cellar to dispatch Demetrius with their spears. They came at dawn and pierced him through the side. He bled out into the floor of the cellar.

The cellar where he died, eventually paved over and built into the foundation of a fifth-century basilica, became the most visited shrine in Greek Christianity outside Constantinople. The basilica that grew over it — the great five-aisled Hagios Demetrios in the upper city of Thessalonica, twice destroyed by fire and twice rebuilt — has held its place at the head of Greek hagiography for fifteen hundred years. Demetrius is the *megalomartyr,* paired in the East with Saint George; the patron of Thessalonica, whose icon was credited with defending the city against Avar, Slav, Bulgar, Norman, and Ottoman in turn; the *myrrh-streaming* one whose tomb, in the medieval centuries, was reported to weep an oil that healed sicknesses.

A young officer who used the privileges of his rank to teach the catechism in a public place, who blessed a friend going into a fight, who waited in his cell for the spears to come, has been remembered for a thousand seven hundred years.

> *"In the name of the God of Demetrius."* — Nestor, in the amphitheater at Thessalonica, 306
