## October 1

# Remigius of Reims

*Bishop, apostle to the Franks, c. 437–c. 533*

He was the third son of a Gallo-Roman senatorial family on the Marne, born at Laon around 437, the youngest of three children whose lives all left marks: his sister Latiana, mother of Bishop Lupus of Soissons; his elder brother Principius, bishop of Soissons before Lupus; and Remigius himself, made bishop of Reims at twenty-two over his loud objections, in a city whose Roman walls were already half-empty as the imperial administration of northern Gaul collapsed around him. He held the see for seventy-two years, the longest episcopate any of the Gallic chronicles record. He outlived the Empire he had been ordained in, the kingdoms of the Visigoths and Burgundians that filled the vacuum, and finally most of the Frankish chieftains who had divided the country once Childeric was gone.

What he is remembered for is one Christmas Eve. Clovis, the young Salian Frank who had killed his way to leadership of the western Franks at fifteen, married the Burgundian Christian princess Clotilde in 493; she had been working on him for four years; her two sons by him she had baptized over his shouted protests, the elder dying as an infant. In 496 Clovis was nearly defeated at Tolbiac by the Alemanni and, the chronicler Hincmar tells the story Gregory of Tours had told before him, called on his wife's God: *if you give me victory I will be baptized.* The Alemanni broke. He marched home to Reims. Remigius, who had been waiting for this conversation for ten years, took the king into the cathedral on the eve of the Nativity of 496 — three thousand of his warriors with him by the Frankish chronicles, perhaps fewer in fact — and baptized him with the famous formula that has been preserved without serious doubt: *Worship what you have burned and burn what you have worshipped.*

The Bollandists devote a long opening section to the difficulty of dating Remigius's other miracles and translations — three separate elevations of his body in three centuries — and to the manuscript tradition of his Vita. What survives the editorial care is the man behind the formula: a bishop who held one chair through every collapse and through the conversion of the people who would build Europe out of the rubble.
