## October 14

# Callistus I

*Pope, freed slave, contested architect of Roman discipline, c. 160–222*

He had been born a slave in the household of a wealthy Christian named Carpophorus, sometime around 160, and entrusted in his early manhood with the management of a small Roman bank his master had set up to receive the deposits of poor Christians. The bank failed. The deposits were lost. Callistus, blamed and probably innocent, was caught fleeing on a ship to Portus, beaten, sentenced to the treadmill, and eventually shipped to the lead mines of Sardinia for what was meant to be the rest of his short life. The empress Marcia, the Christian concubine of Commodus, secured a general pardon for the Christian prisoners around 190; Callistus came home a marked man, settled on the small church-stipend Pope Victor offered him, and rose through the deaconate under Pope Zephyrinus, who eventually trusted him enough to make him the administrator of the great Christian cemetery on the Appian Way that has borne his name ever since. He was elected pope on Zephyrinus's death in 217.

His five-year pontificate became the most controversial of any third-century Roman bishop. Hippolytus, the most learned theologian of the Roman church, refused to recognize Callistus's election and set himself up as anti-pope — the first in the history of the Roman see — and his *Refutation of All Heresies* attacks Callistus by name as a man who was readmitting penitent adulterers to communion, ordaining men who had been married more than once, and tolerating clandestine marriages between Roman matrons and Christian slaves whom they could not lawfully marry under Roman law. Callistus replied that the church was not a club of the perfect but a hospital, and that the keys had been given to bind and to loose. He stood by the policy. The third-century Latin church, slowly and against Hippolytus's bitter opposition, ratified him.

He died, the *acta* preserve, in a popular riot rather than an imperial persecution — beaten in his own house in Trastevere by a mob who had been told the Christians were responsible for a recent flood of the Tiber, thrown out a high window with a stone tied around his neck, dragged into a well, and covered over with rubble. The presbyter Asterius recovered the body that night and buried him in the cemetery on the Aurelian Way, on the fourteenth of October, 222. Santa Maria in Trastevere — built where his house had stood — keeps his name on the dedication still.

The slave who had administered the bank for poor Christians governed the Roman church for five years and was killed by a Roman mob, and the policy he insisted on — that mercy is not weakness — has not been revoked.

> *"Misericordia exaltat se super judicium."* — Callistus, in Hippolytus's hostile paraphrase: *mercy exalts itself over judgment*
