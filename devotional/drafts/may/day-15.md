## May 15

# Isidore the Farmer

*Madrid farmhand, c. 1070–1130*

He was a poor man of Madrid, a frontier town only a generation back from the Moors, and he spent his whole life as a hired laborer on the lands of a Madrid knight. He had a wife and a son, and worked one man's fields from dawn to dark for a yearly wage. The thing his neighbors remembered about him was that he could not be got to the field before he had been to church: every morning, the Acts say, he put off the plowing to go the round of the city's thresholds and pray, and only then came out to his work — and still, with God helping him, did more than the men who had started at first light.

His fellow laborers saw only the praying, and complained to the knight that Isidore was paid to plow and spent the mornings on his knees. The knight went out before dawn and hid above the field to catch him at it. He caught, instead, a thing he had not bargained for: beside Isidore, in the grey light, two further yokes of oxen, white as nothing of this world, plowing the field alongside him — and when he looked again, only Isidore, alone, at his plow. Asked afterward who his helpers were, Isidore said only that he had called none and seen none, but God. The knight never raised the matter of the prayers again.

He was buried in the churchyard of Saint Andrew about 1130, and lay there forty years before anyone thought to move him. When at last they opened the grave the body was whole and unspoiled, with an odor like incense, and the bells of the city, they said, rang of their own accord as he was carried in. In the droughts that came after, the people of Madrid would bear him out before the altar to beg for rain, and the rain came. The farmhands' town he had left became the imperial capital that canonized him in 1622 — a city that would forget where its food came from, given a saint who never did.

> *"I neither called nor saw any helpers, but only God; whom I invoke and implore, and always have as my helper."* — Isidore, of the unseen hands at his plow
