## January 1

# Odilo of Cluny

*Abbot, founder of All Souls' Day, c. 962–1049*

A pilgrim from Auvergne, shipwrecked on a small island near Sicily, was told by a local hermit that the rocks beyond their cove opened at certain hours into fissures of flame, and that voices could be heard from them — demons complaining that souls were being torn from their grip by the prayers and Masses of Odilo and the monks of Cluny. The pilgrim made his way home, and then to Souvigny, and told the abbot the story.

Odilo did not boast. He commanded. The next year, in every monastery of his order, on the day after the feast of All Saints, the whole community gathered to celebrate the Eucharist, sing the psalms, and distribute bread to the poor on behalf of all the faithful departed. He did not invent the prayers; the Church had prayed for the dead since the catacombs. He gave them a fixed day. The practice spread out of Cluny across Europe, and in time the universal Church received what Odilo had begun.

The chroniclers remembered him for other things. Riding near Paris, he once came upon two children dead of cold in the road, unburied and unmourned. He dismounted, stripped the wool from his back to cover them, dug their graves, and rode on. During the famine years he broke up the silver vessels of his abbey for bread — even melted the imperial crown that Henry had given him — judging it unworthy to deny the poor what Christ had been killed for. And when a leprous priest from Le Puy withdrew to a hut by the Loire and asked through an intermediary to see him, Odilo came at once: the brothers watched in alarm as the abbot took the man into his arms and held him there, in the long conversation of friends. He loved, the chronicler wrote, the prerogative of nature in a man more than he dreaded the approach of disease.

What Odilo gave the Church was a calendar that confessed what every grieving parishioner already half-believed: that the dead are not lost to us, and the prayers of the living still carry. Today, wherever a son or daughter kneels for the parents they have buried, may the words still rise: *Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them.*

> *"I prefer to be mercifully judged for mercy than cruelly condemned for cruelty."* — Odilo

## January 2

# Adelard of Corbie

*Abbot, cousin of Charlemagne, c. 752–826*

He was a grandson of Charles Martel and a cousin of Charlemagne, raised among the tutors of the imperial palace and groomed for service in the kingdom. When Charlemagne, having sworn before God to marry the Lombard princess Desiderata, repudiated her in favor of a second wife while she still lived, Adelard — a cadet of the court, perhaps eighteen — refused to render any of the customary honors to the new queen. He could not stop the marriage. He could leave it. At twenty he walked out of the palace and presented himself at the gate of the monastery of Corbie.

His abbot, knowing him to be the emperor's cousin, sent him first to the kitchen garden. Adelard hoed the rows with relief — the only place in the kingdom, his biographer wrote, where he could be both a king's grandson and a nobody. After some years his family began visiting too often. He slipped out one night and walked to Monte Cassino. The emperor's messengers caught him there within months and brought him back; the brothers elected him abbot. He governed for forty years and, with his namesake, founded a daughter house at Corvey in Saxony, which would become a school of missionaries to the still-pagan north.

He wept easily. The chronicler reports he lamented his own sins and the sins of his community at least twice a day, every day. He spoke of God in such a way that some called him another Anthony. He died at seventy-five, having outlived the emperor he had broken with, and was mourned by his disciple Paschasius Radbertus in a Latin elegy from which the medieval Church learned how to grieve.

What Adelard left was not a treatise or a battle. It was the quiet record of a man who, when the kingdom asked him to bless what God had forbidden, said no, and lived another fifty years to prove the answer was the right one.

> *"Day of your death — would that it had never dawned for me, for none more evil could have come, nor any more cruel have shone for all your people."* — Paschasius Radbertus, at Adelard's tomb

## January 3

# Genevieve of Paris

*Virgin, protectress of the city, c. 422–509*

When Germanus of Auxerre passed through the village of Nanterre on his way to Britain, a crowd poured out to meet him — mothers with children, old men seeking a blessing, the curious and the devout. In the press of the crowd the bishop's eye fell on a girl perhaps eight or nine years old. He asked to be brought to her. Kissing her head, he said to her parents, "Happy are you. At this child's birth the angels rejoiced. She will be great before the Lord."

He then asked the girl whether she would consecrate her body to Christ as his bride. She answered, "Blessed are you, Father, for asking what I already desire." Germanus gave her a bronze coin, found on the ground, stamped with a cross. "Wear this from your neck," he told her, "and do not burden yourself with gold or pearl. For if the smallest charm of this world overcomes your heart, you will lack the ornaments that do not perish."

Genevieve kept the promise for eighty years. When the Huns swept toward Paris in 451, the city prepared to flee; she told them to stay, to fast, to pray, and Attila turned aside. When Clovis later besieged the same walls, she slipped past his lines and returned with grain for the starving. She was neither general nor queen, only a consecrated girl grown into an old woman whose word the city had learned to trust.

What Germanus saw in the child at Nanterre was not greatness but willingness. The holiness came later, slowly, in the keeping of small promises. Today, wherever Christ asks the question he asked through Germanus, may the answer still be: *Your handmaid hears; command what you will.*

> *"Act manfully, and what you believe in your heart and profess with your mouth, strive to fulfill in your works."* — Germanus to Genevieve

## January 4

# Pharaildis of Ghent

*Virgin, widow, royal Frankish, c. 650–740*

She was born to King Theuderic of Lotharingia and his queen Amelberga, raised from the font by her kinswoman Gertrude, and instructed in letters at a court that expected princesses to marry where the king pointed. Pharaildis had pointed elsewhere. From her mother's arms, the chronicler insists, she had given herself entire to Christ. When her father pressed her toward a marriage of his own arranging — to a nobleman named Guido — she could not refuse him in the open, but on the wedding night she knelt and prayed.

The prayer she made, the biographer preserves in four lines of awkward verse: that the same God who made the fabric of the age would grant his handmaid to keep her promise. The grace held. For thirty years she continued to share Guido's house, slipping out at midnight or cockcrow to the monastery for vigils and returning before dawn. He thought it an affair, and beat her for years. She did not leave; she did not strike back; she asked God to put an end to him. Not long after, riding hard to a hunt, he was thrown from his horse, broke himself badly, and died.

Pharaildis took the widow's veil and lived almost ninety years. The most-remembered story from her widowhood is small. In a winter field she gathered some wild geese with her staff as if they were sheep, drove them home, and forbade her servants to harm them. One she found missing — its bones and feathers in a cooking-pot — and she put them back together and sent it on its way. Her sisters Gudula and Reineldis and her brother Emebertus would all become saints in the same Belgian church.

She is painted, where her cult survived the Calvinist destruction of her shrine at Ghent, with a goose at her feet. The small miracle is what the peasants remembered. The long endurance, less easy to paint, was what carried her there.

> *"Supreme Father, King, Light, and Source of days, by whose command the fabric of this age stands: grant to your grateful handmaid to enjoy virginity."* — Pharaildis, on her wedding night

## January 5

# Edward the Confessor

*King of England, c. 1003–1066*

He was sixty when the kingdom finally came to him. His father Ethelred the Unready had died fleeing the Danes; his half-brother Alfred had been blinded and killed in the same flight; his mother Emma had spent his childhood remarrying his father's enemies. Edward had grown up in Normandy, in exile, and had said more than once that he would rather live a private life forever than recover the kingdom through the slaughter of men.

When the Danish line at last failed, the slaughter was not required. He was crowned, took to the throne with mild reluctance, and for twenty-four years ruled an England that William of Malmesbury could not stop calling — to his own faint astonishment — *quiet*. The civil wars stopped. The Danes stopped. The laws he restored were his grandfather Edgar's, recalled from sixty-eight years of disuse and reissued under his own name, with one provision freshly added: any man who lent at usury was to forfeit his goods and live as an exile. The English remembered the Laws of St. Edward longer than they remembered Edward himself.

He kept his marriage to Editha chaste, by mutual agreement; he founded the monastery at Westminster, where he would be buried and where his bones became the abbey's most-visited relic; he forgave easily; he gave widely. The chronicler delights in one small story: hunting near Windsor, a peasant once accidentally scared the deer off, and the king, roused, cried — *By God and His Mother, I will do you as much harm if I can!* — and that, says the biographer, was the worst threat Edward could imagine. *A noble spirit*, he writes, *which did not remember that it was king.*

The peace of his reign was a benediction the next century would not see. It rested, his eulogists thought, on the simple fact that the man who held the sword had never quite wanted to use it.

> *"I would rather live a private life forever than recover the kingdom through the slaughter of men."* — Edward, in exile

## January 6

# The Epiphany

*The manifestation of Christ to the Gentiles*

They came from beyond the Roman world, scholars of stars whose names the gospel does not bother to record. Tradition would later call them Caspar and Melchior and Balthasar and number them at three; the gospel only says *wise men from the East*, and *they brought gifts*. They had read a sign in the night sky and walked, by the only road they knew how to take, in the direction it pointed. The road led them to Jerusalem. They assumed, reasonably, that a new king would be born in a palace, and so they went to a palace and asked.

Herod heard them out and called for his priests, who knew the prophets. *Bethlehem*, the priests said — almost without thinking. Herod sent the foreigners south with a courteous lie about wanting to honor the child himself. The wise men went on. The star, which they had not seen since Jerusalem, returned to them on the road, and led them to a house that was no palace at all. They had walked for months. They knelt before a toddler.

They opened their treasures and laid out gold for a king, frankincense for a priest, and myrrh for the burial of a man — not yet knowing why. A warning came that night in a dream: do not return to Herod. They left Bethlehem by a different road and disappeared from the gospel forever.

The Epiphany is the showing-forth of Christ. The early Church understood it just as much as the showing-forth of *who he came for*. The first to find him after the shepherds were neither rabbis nor soldiers nor priests, but foreigners following a sign no Jew had been given. May our roads, however oddly they turn, still bring us to the same house.

> *"When they saw the star, they rejoiced with exceeding great joy."* — Matthew 2:10

## January 7

# Lucian of Antioch

*Priest, biblical scholar, martyr, c. 240–312*

He lived between two empires. He was born when Decius's persecution was already a memory and Diocletian's was a generation away, in Antioch, where Christianity had names for itself in three languages. Trained in rhetoric and Scripture both, he settled into the patient work of a textual editor: collating, comparing, correcting the manuscripts of the Septuagint and the Greek New Testament that the Church of the East was already using. The recension he produced, the so-called Lucianic text, was read aloud in the liturgies of Antioch and Constantinople for a thousand years. Jerome would later draw on it for the Vulgate.

He taught as well as edited. Among his students were Arius — whose career he did not live to witness — and Eusebius of Nicomedia. The school he led was called *Antiochene*, in distinction from the allegorical school at Alexandria: where the Alexandrians read every line of Scripture as figure, the Antiochenes read it first as history, and meaning came up from the ground. The instinct survives in modern biblical scholarship.

When Diocletian's persecution reached Antioch, Lucian fled but was arrested at Nicomedia. The emperor's officers tried first to convert him by flattery, then by threats, then by hunger — they offered him food that had been sacrificed to idols, and he refused it. He had been a priest before he was a prisoner. According to one account, on the feast of Epiphany itself, lying chained on his back in the cell, he celebrated the Eucharist on his own chest with the bread his fellow prisoners had smuggled in. The next day he was killed. The interrogator's final question received the only answer he ever gave: *I am a Christian.*

The work he had done with his pen and the death he died with his body were, in him, the same testimony — that the word made flesh could be kept in a manuscript and in a martyr alike.

> *"I am a Christian."* — Lucian, in answer to every charge

## January 8

# Severinus of Noricum

*Apostle of Noricum, c. 410–482*

He appeared on the Danube frontier without papers, in the years when the Western Empire was visibly dying. The Roman towns along the river — Favianae, Boiotro, Quintanis — were being abandoned a few at a time as Germanic raiders crossed the ice. No one knew where the strange ascetic had come from; when asked his country, he would say only, *I have come for the sake of the Lord*. His biographer, Eugippius, who would write his Life from the testimony of those who knew him, never learned the answer.

He gathered the panicked Roman remnants into ordered communities. He founded monasteries — at Favianae and at Mauternbach — where the displaced could pray, work, and be fed. He negotiated with the barbarian kings: Odoacer himself, before he sacked Italy, came to ask the holy man's blessing and was given a prophecy that he would rule sixteen years. He warned the towns in advance of every raid; the chronicles record that he was never wrong. He set up grain distributions during famine. When the Romans of Quintanis would not flee, though he had told them they must, he gathered them at his cell, said the office, lifted his eyes to the sky, and wept; that night the Rugii came over the wall.

He had stripped himself of estate, rank, and even origin, the chronicler says, in order to belong only to the kingdom whose ruler does not die. When the empire he had come to serve finally fell, four years after his own death, the monks carried his body to Italy, knowing that nothing could be safe on the Danube anymore.

He spent thirty years comforting the dying world. What he founded, on the frontier where everything else was failing, was a way of being among the displaced without despairing.

> *"I have come for the sake of the Lord."* — Severinus, when asked his country

## January 9

# Adrian of Canterbury

*Abbot, African scholar in England, c. 635–710*

He was African by birth — Libyan, the Bollandists guess — abbot of a small monastery near Naples and, in 667, the man Pope Vitalian first chose to send to Britain. The archbishopric of Canterbury had been vacant for three years; the new English Church needed a head. Adrian declined the office. He recommended instead a Greek monk named Theodore, then sixty-six years old, who was living in Rome. The pope agreed, on condition that Adrian travel with Theodore as his counselor.

They sailed in 668. Theodore was made archbishop; Adrian became abbot of the monastery of SS Peter and Paul at Canterbury, where Augustine of Canterbury was buried. The two foreigners — one Greek, one African, neither of whom had a word of English between them when they landed — set about the slow rebuilding of an Anglo-Saxon Church that had lost its bearings.

They opened a school. Students walked to Canterbury from every kingdom of the heptarchy. They were taught Latin, Greek, the Scriptures, computation, music, and — Bede notes with quiet pride — verse. Aldhelm of Sherborne was Adrian's pupil; through Aldhelm came Bede's generation, and through them the literacy of the entire Western Middle Ages. Bede, writing forty years later, says that some of Adrian's pupils were still alive in his own day and could converse in Greek and Latin as easily as in their mother tongue.

He outlived Theodore by twenty years and continued to teach until he could no longer rise from his bed. He had not wanted the journey; the kingdom of God had asked it of him anyway, and the kingdom had been the better for it.

> *"Never had there been such happy times in Britain since the English came to Britain."* — Bede, on the school of Theodore and Adrian

## January 10

# William of Bourges

*Archbishop, Cistercian, c. 1155–1209*

He was a younger son of the noble house of Nevers, given an honorary canonry while still a boy and a real one in his early twenties; he might have lived out the unremarkable life of a regular canon at Soissons and never been heard of. The death of his elder brother brought the inheritance toward him; he refused it. He left Soissons, walked across France, and presented himself at the gates of the Cistercian abbey of Pontigny. Twenty years later, when the see of Bourges fell vacant, he was abbot of Châalis. The chapter of Bourges elected him archbishop. He went into hiding. The pope wrote ordering him to accept. He accepted.

He kept the Cistercian habit under his pallium and ate at the monastic table when he could. He preached against the Albigensian errors spreading south of him without rancor, by patient instruction; he was reluctant to bless the armed crusade that Innocent III was beginning to prepare against them, preferring conversion to conquest. He arbitrated quarrels between Philip II of France and the pope, and was respected by both for the visible absence of his own interest in the outcome.

He died nine years after his consecration, worn out, on the night of January 10. The chronicle says he had spent the day hearing confessions and refused to break his fast until vespers. The community at Bourges buried him in the cathedral; pilgrims began coming almost at once, and Honorius III canonized him within a decade — a speed of recognition that had no parallel in his century.

What William offered his diocese was the witness of an office held lightly, in the constant awareness that it was on loan.

> *"Lord, you have given me this charge against my will; let me at least bear it as you would have me bear it."* — William, after his consecration

## January 11

# Theodosius the Cenobiarch

*Abbot, father of Palestinian cenobitism, 423–529*

He was Cappadocian by birth, a near-contemporary of Sabbas the Sanctified, and he came to the Judean desert when he was about thirty, planning to live as a hermit. The hermits' caves along the road from Jerusalem to Bethlehem were filling up; men came to him for counsel and would not leave. He moved deeper into the wilderness, and they followed. At last, persuaded that solitude alone was not his calling, he built a great communal monastery on a hill east of Bethlehem and made the gathering official.

What he founded was unlike any monastery the East had yet seen. He laid out four churches in the same compound — one for each of the languages his monks spoke: Greek, Armenian, Bessian, and Arabic — and the offices were prayed in all four. He built a hospital for the sick. He built a hospice for the old. He set apart a separate quarter for the mentally ill and another for the homeless poor. By the end of his life four hundred men lived under his rule, and the patriarchs of Jerusalem confirmed him as *cenobiarch*, the supervisor of all communal monasteries in Palestine.

In his old age the emperor Anastasius sent him a substantial bribe to support the Monophysite party against Chalcedon. Theodosius gave the money to the poor and wrote back that he and his monks would not betray the faith of the apostles for any throne. He was briefly exiled. He returned. He lived to one hundred and five.

He had taken a desert that had been the refuge of solitaries and turned it into a country of hospitable houses. What he taught his sons was that the silence of God is best kept not alone but in company.

> *"We will defend the faith of the apostles."* — Theodosius, in answer to the emperor

## January 12

# Benedict Biscop

*Founder of Wearmouth-Jarrow, c. 628–690*

He was twenty-five and a thegn at the court of King Oswiu of Northumbria when he set down his sword and walked south. He had no plans yet; he wanted Rome. It would be the first of six journeys he made over the next thirty-five years to Italy and back, on foot and by sea, in an age when most Anglo-Saxons died within walking distance of where they were baptized.

On the second trip he stopped at Lérins, off the Provençal coast, and stayed for two years as a monk. On the third, Pope Vitalian sent him to England with Theodore of Tarsus and Adrian of Canterbury. After two years at Canterbury he asked permission to go home north; King Ecgfrith of Northumbria granted him land at the mouth of the Wear, and in 674 he built a monastery there. Eight years later he built another at Jarrow. The two houses — Wearmouth and Jarrow — operated as one community on two sites, and would remain so for the rest of the Anglo-Saxon age.

He could not stop traveling. He went back to Rome four more times, each time returning with what no one else thought to bring: books for the library, paintings to teach the unlettered, John the Archcantor of Rome to teach plainsong, masons from Gaul to build in stone (the first stone church north of the Tees), glaziers to make windows. The library he assembled was the finest north of the Alps; without it Bede, a boy he had received into the community at seven, would not have become Bede.

His last illness ran three years. The brothers chanted the psalter around his bed in shifts so that the office would never stop. He died at the Gloria of one of the Psalms.

> *"I have brought you books, that you may forever have the means of learning."* — Biscop to his community, before his final journey to Rome

## January 13

# Hilary of Poitiers

*Bishop, Doctor of the Church, c. 315–368*

He was raised pagan in Aquitaine, married, fathered a daughter, and converted in his mid-thirties through the study of Scripture. The Church in Gaul made him a priest at once, and then — by acclamation, against his preference — bishop of Poitiers in 350. He had been a Christian fewer than five years.

What he found in his diocese was a Church drifting toward Arianism by way of imperial pressure. The Emperor Constantius II, an Arian sympathizer, was assembling councils across the West to depose the bishops who refused to repudiate Athanasius. Hilary refused. He drafted an open letter to the emperor protesting the persecution of orthodoxy. The emperor exiled him to Phrygia, a region he could not have located on a map. He went.

Exile became his great work. He learned Greek in Phrygia and read the Greek Fathers; he wrote the twelve books *On the Trinity*, the first sustained Latin treatment of the doctrine; he wrote commentaries on the Psalms and on Matthew. He attended an Arian synod at Seleucia and made himself such a nuisance to the eastern bishops by his persistent orthodoxy that the emperor sent him home four years early. He returned to Gaul to ovations, and his second decade as bishop saw the Arian collapse in the West that the next generation would credit largely to him. They would call him the Athanasius of the West.

He took in a young soldier named Martin who had asked to be released from his military oath; the discipling of Martin of Tours, and through Martin of half of Gaul, began in Hilary's house. He died at home in Poitiers, fifty-three years old, having spent half his Christian life in defense of a sentence: *the Son is what the Father is.*

> *"The best service we can render is to give back to him our small understanding of his greatness."* — Hilary, *De Trinitate* I.2

## January 14

# Felix of Nola

*Priest, confessor, d. c. 260*

Almost everything we know about him comes from a man who lived three hundred years after he died. Paulinus of Nola, bishop, poet, friend of Augustine, settled in Felix's town in his mid-forties and made it his life's work to tend the tomb of the obscure priest whose feast still drew pilgrims to that hillside in Campania. Every January 14 for fifteen years Paulinus composed a *natalicium*, a birthday poem, and read it aloud at the grave. The poems are what we have.

What Paulinus found in the local memory was this. Felix was the son of a Syrian-born minor official; he gave his patrimony to the poor on receiving it. He served as a priest in Nola under the elderly bishop Maximus. When the Decian persecution began the officers came for the bishop first; Maximus, frail, fled to the hills and was found by Felix half-dead from exposure. Felix carried him home. The officers came for Felix next. They scourged him, loaded him with chains, and threw him into a prison floored with broken pottery. He was freed — the chroniclers say by an angel, Paulinus more modestly says by an unlocked door — and went on hiding. After Decius died and the persecution lifted, Felix could have been bishop in his turn. He refused. He told the diocese that an older priest deserved the chair, and he himself lived another forty years tilling a small plot of land outside town, distributing whatever crops he raised to the poor.

The pagans of Nola, who had known him, never forgot. The Christians who knelt at his tomb in Paulinus's day were sometimes children of grandchildren of the men who had imprisoned him.

> *"He was crowned in life, since he had so often been ready to die for Christ."* — Paulinus of Nola, *Natalicium I*

## January 15

# Maurus

*Disciple of Benedict, sixth century*

He was twelve years old when his father, a Roman senator named Equitius, brought him up the road to Subiaco and placed him in the care of Benedict. Another boy, slightly younger, named Placid, arrived a few weeks later. The three names — Benedict, Maurus, Placid — would mark the beginning of the Benedictine movement in the West.

Gregory the Great, writing his *Dialogues* fifty years afterward, preserved one story about Maurus. The boys were drawing water from the lake below the monastery. Placid lost his footing, fell in, and was swept toward the deep water. Benedict, at his cell some distance off, saw it in spirit and called: *Brother Maurus, run, run — the boy is drowning.* Maurus, who had been about to scoop a bucket himself, took Benedict's blessing as a command and ran. He reached the shore, stepped onto the water, and walked across it to where Placid was sinking. He caught the boy by the hair and walked back, the chronicler says, as if on a paved road, and only when he had set Placid down did he notice what had happened.

Benedict and Maurus afterward argued — gently — about whose merit had carried the miracle: Benedict insisting it was Maurus's obedience, Maurus insisting it was Benedict's command. Placid, whom no one consulted, settled the matter by saying he had seen Benedict's cloak above his head in the water and had thought it was Benedict pulling him out.

It is the only Maurus story that the early tradition vouches for. Whatever else he did with his life he did under his abbot's shadow, content there. The story keeps because of what it shows: obedience moves across water by not stopping to ask whether water can be moved across.

> *"Whose merit did this?"* — Benedict to Maurus, the disagreement they could not resolve

## January 16

# Marcellus I, Pope

*Bishop of Rome, c. 255–309*

He was elected to the see of Rome in the year 308, five years after the end of the Diocletian persecution. The Roman Church he inherited was not so much divided as winded. Christians who had handed over the Scriptures under torture wished to be received back; Christians who had not been broken did not wish to receive them. The previous pope, Marcellinus, had himself faltered under interrogation and died under a cloud; the see had been vacant for several years. Marcellus took up the office knowing he would be hated by half the city before his first year was out.

He set up a disciplinary system for the *lapsi* — the lapsed. The penitents would do public penance for a fixed period, take their place at the back of the assembly, and only after years be readmitted to communion. The unbroken Christians thought it too lenient; the lapsed thought it cruel. Both factions rioted. Marcellus, in the same months, was also organizing Rome's first twenty-five *tituli*, the parish churches that would still be the bones of the city's Christianity in our day.

When the riots threatened public order, the emperor Maxentius — no friend of Christianity, but a friend of order — exiled Marcellus from the city. Tradition says he was forced to work the imperial stables on the road south, and died there. Damasus, half a century later, would write his epitaph: *true shepherd, because he proclaimed to the lapsed that they must weep for their crimes, he was reckoned a bitter enemy by all the unhappy ones.*

His was a short pontificate. What he gave the Church was not a great gift but a difficult one: the conviction that mercy can be ordered, and that ordering it costs.

> *"True shepherd, because he proclaimed to the lapsed that they must weep for their crimes, he was reckoned a bitter enemy by all the unhappy ones."* — Damasus, on Marcellus

## January 17

# Anthony the Great

*Abbot, father of monks, c. 251–356*

He was eighteen, newly orphaned, when he walked into church and heard the Gospel read: *If you would be perfect, go, sell what you have and give to the poor.* He walked out and did it. The land his parents had left him — three hundred fertile acres along the Nile — he gave to his neighbors. The household goods he sold. The proceeds he distributed, keeping only a small sum for his younger sister. A week later he heard another verse: *Take no thought for tomorrow.* He gave that sum away too, placed his sister in the care of consecrated virgins, and went out to the edge of his village to pray.

The desert, for Anthony, was not yet a place. It was an intention. He learned prayer from one old hermit, fasting from another, vigilance from a third, gathering virtues like a bee, as his biographer says, from every flower. Only when he had learned them did he walk beyond the inhabited world, cross the Nile, and shut himself in an abandoned fortress. For twenty years he spoke to no one. Bread and water were passed through a small window twice a year.

When at last he came out, people expected a wasted man. Instead, the biographer writes, "his body kept its former state, neither grown fat from idleness nor wasted from fasting and wrestling with demons; but such as they had known him before his retirement." He had been fighting, and he had been strengthened. To the friends who crowded around him he said only this: *I saw the snares of the enemy spread over the whole earth, and I groaned and said, Who can escape them? And I heard a voice answering: Humility.*

> *"I was here; but I was waiting to see your contest."* — the voice Anthony heard in the dark

## January 18

# Deicolus of Lure

*Irish abbot in Burgundy, c. 530–625*

He was one of the twelve monks who left Bangor with Columbanus around the year 590 to bring monastic life into the Frankish kingdoms. Columbanus was about fifty then; Deicolus was somewhere between thirty and forty. For twenty years the band walked, preached, founded houses, and confronted kings. They settled longest at Luxeuil in the Vosges. When Columbanus rebuked the Burgundian king Theuderic II for keeping a series of concubines and refusing to take a wife, Theuderic's grandmother Brunhilda — who liked the arrangement — had the Irish driven out of the kingdom.

Deicolus could not keep up. He was old; the march to the coast was a hundred miles and more; he collapsed in the forest near Lure in eastern Burgundy. The story preserved by the locals is that he fell asleep against a tree and a wild boar lay down at his feet to warm him. A swineherd found him, took him in, and a wealthy Frankish lord named Werfhar gave him a small clearing in the forest. He built a hut. Other monks who had been scattered came back to him; he built a chapel; the chapel became a monastery; the monastery, Lutra — modern Lure — would last twelve hundred years.

The chroniclers remember him for his unfailing cheerfulness. Columbanus had asked him once, in the Vosges, why he was always smiling. Deicolus said: *because no one can take Christ from me*. It was the only formal sermon of his that has come down.

He lived to nearly a hundred. The Burgundian peasants who watched him grow old took the example, and stopped being surprised that an Irish exile abandoned in a forest had built them a house of God.

> *"No one can take Christ from me."* — Deicolus, asked why he smiled

## January 19

# Wulfstan of Worcester

*Bishop, Benedictine, 1008–1095*

He was the only Anglo-Saxon bishop who kept his see after the Norman Conquest, and he kept it by being so transparently saintly that Lanfranc gave up trying to depose him. The Normans had a procedure for old English bishops: gentle pressure, a language test, an accusation of insufficient learning, replacement. Wulfstan, when Lanfranc summoned him to surrender his pastoral staff at Edward the Confessor's tomb, set the staff down on the tomb and asked Edward to keep it for him. The story — most likely embellished, certainly believed — was that no one but Wulfstan could lift it again.

What was true beneath the legend is that no one else wanted his job once they had seen him at it. He preached in English when the Latin was lost on his hearers. He visited every parish in his diocese on horseback every year, sleeping in the parsonages and eating with the parish priests. He fasted hard, prayed long, slept little, and rarely sat down. With the help of his old friend Lanfranc — who came around — he led the campaign that ended the slave trade out of the port of Bristol, where English boys and girls had been sold to Ireland for two generations. He preached against it in Bristol itself, on the docks, for weeks, until the merchants relented.

He served as bishop of Worcester for thirty-two years. He rebuilt the cathedral. He buried his Anglo-Saxon people one by one and learned to bury his Norman ones too. He died in his eighties on January 19, 1095, having outlived nearly everyone who had ever known him as anything but the bishop.

> *"Why are you afraid? Christ is still on his throne."* — Wulfstan to a Norman noble, on hearing of a rebellion

## January 20

# Fabian, Pope

*Bishop of Rome, martyr, c. 200–250*

He had come into the city from a country estate on the day the clergy of Rome were gathered to elect a new pope. He was a layman. He had nothing to do with the proceedings; he stood in the crowd outside the chamber. Eusebius records what happened next: a dove descended from above the gathering and settled on his head. Everyone present took it as a sign. Fabian was carried inside, ordained, and consecrated bishop of Rome on the same day.

He governed the Roman Church for fourteen years, the longest peaceful pontificate of the third century. He divided the city into seven ecclesiastical regions, one for each deacon, in a structure that has lasted. He sent seven missionary bishops into Gaul — Saturninus to Toulouse, Denis to Paris, Trophimus to Arles, Paul to Narbonne — and the Church there owes its origin to him. He recovered the bodies of his persecuted predecessors Pontian and Hippolytus, brought them back from Sardinia, and buried them with honor in Rome.

When the emperor Decius issued his edict in 250 requiring every citizen of the empire to sacrifice publicly to the Roman gods, Fabian was the first major bishop to be arrested. He refused the sacrifice. He was killed by imprisonment and torture rather than by a single execution, and Cyprian of Carthage, who had heard the news while himself in hiding, wrote: *the Roman Church has been adorned by the glorious death of an incomparable shepherd.*

The dove was a story. The reign was real. What it shows is that the Church can be governed for fourteen quiet years by a man whom no one had been looking for, and that he can die in a single afternoon when the time comes.

> *"The Roman Church has been adorned by the glorious death of an incomparable shepherd."* — Cyprian of Carthage, on the martyrdom of Fabian

## January 21

# Agnes

*Roman virgin and martyr, c. 291–304*

She was twelve, perhaps thirteen. Her family was Roman, Christian, well-placed enough that the son of the urban prefect had taken notice of her. The young man pressed her father for marriage; Agnes refused. She had given herself, she said, to Christ. The father appealed first to Roman law (a girl of twelve could be made to marry) and then, when she still refused, to the Diocletian edict against Christians. Agnes was brought before the tribunal.

The prefect tried persuasion. He tried his son's tears. He tried the public humiliation of having her stripped and led to a brothel — and the chronicler insists, with the kind of detail that suggests the story was told for a generation by women who had seen it, that her hair grew to cover her. He tried torture. The girl, says Ambrose seventy years later, was so unshaken that the men charged with hurting her could not work; their hands trembled and they wept. At last she was led to the place of execution. The pagan crowd, the Christian crowd, the soldiers, the prefect, his son, were all there.

She was killed by a single stroke of the sword. The Christian community of Rome buried her on the Via Nomentana the same day. By the time Constantine's daughter Constantina was building the basilica over her grave, twenty-five years later, her cult was already the most beloved in the city. Ambrose preached about her; Damasus inscribed her epitaph; Prudentius wrote her hymn.

The fact that she was twelve has never quite stopped being scandalous. It was not meant to. The Roman Church kept her at twelve because what she did at twelve we are not always brave enough to do at fifty.

> *"At an age when little girls cannot bear their parents' frowns, this child triumphed over the cruellest tyrants."* — Ambrose, *De Virginibus* I

## January 22

# Vincent of Saragossa

*Deacon and martyr, d. 304*

He was the deacon of an old man. Valerius, bishop of Saragossa, had grown deaf, his speech slow, his memory unreliable; for the last years of his episcopate Vincent had been doing the work of the diocese while the old bishop signed the documents. When the governor Dacian, on Diocletian's orders, summoned both of them to Valencia, Valerius could not have answered the charges if his life had depended on it. Vincent answered for him.

The deacon's confession at the tribunal made the prosecutor furious. Dacian, the Acts say, had Valerius sent into exile — he was too old to make a useful martyr — and turned the full apparatus on Vincent. The torments were the standard catalogue of late Roman judicial savagery: rack, iron hooks, fire, the gridiron, salt rubbed into open wounds, broken potsherds for a prison floor. Vincent endured them in silence until the moment of release; he is reported to have said, at the very end, that he now exalted in the wounds he bore for Christ. He died in his cell, exhausted, before another hearing.

His Acts were already being read aloud in Spanish basilicas by the time Augustine was a young priest, three generations later. Augustine preached at least three sermons on him; Prudentius wrote his great Spanish hymn for him; the Roman Canon names him along with Lawrence and Stephen. Within a hundred years his name had reached Gaul, Italy, North Africa, and Egypt — a deacon of an old man, executed in a corner of the empire on a day no one had wanted to remember.

He had been asked to speak for someone too tired to defend himself. He did. The rest followed.

> *"I now exalt in the wounds I bear for Christ."* — Vincent, after his last torture

## January 23

# John the Almsgiver

*Patriarch of Alexandria, c. 552–616*

He was a Cypriot, a widower, a layman, and over sixty when the emperor Heraclius asked him to take Alexandria. The Persians had just begun their march through Syria; Antioch had fallen; Jerusalem would fall within a few years; the Christian refugees pouring south into Egypt had no one to receive them. John accepted on one condition: he would call the poor his masters.

He kept books. He kept them very precisely. He had drawn up a list of all the destitute in Alexandria at the start of his patriarchate — *seven and a half thousand of my masters and brothers*, he said — and he marked it daily as families received bread and clothes from the patriarchal treasury. When the bishops of his synod objected that a patriarch should not be a clerk to beggars, John told them that the books in question were the books God would consult.

When Jerusalem fell to the Persians in 614, John sent shiploads of food and money to the besieged Christians and welcomed the refugees who arrived in his city by the thousands. When the church treasury ran dry, he melted down the plate. When a beggar tricked him three days running by appearing in different disguises and receiving alms each time, his stewards refused to give a fourth time. John called them back. *Give to him*, he said, *until we have tested whether he is Christ.*

He died at home in Cyprus, having returned there during a Persian raid on Alexandria, and was buried with the inscription he had asked for in advance: that he had given everything that was given him, and that this had been the only joy of his life.

> *"I will not be reconciled to God until I have made up to my brethren what I have failed to give them."* — John the Almsgiver

## January 24

# Babylas of Antioch

*Bishop and martyr, d. 253*

He was bishop of Antioch in the years when the empire briefly had a Christian — or near-Christian — emperor. Philip the Arab, who reigned from 244 to 249, was at minimum sympathetic; some early sources call him outright Christian. In either case Philip arrived at the door of Babylas's church one Easter night, with his retinue, expecting to enter for the vigil.

Babylas met him at the threshold and would not let him in. The emperor, it was widely known, had had his predecessor Gordian III killed to take the throne. Babylas explained, courteously, that an unconfessed killer could not be admitted to the Eucharist. The emperor — to his everlasting credit — submitted: he took his place among the public penitents at the back, did the penance Babylas imposed, and was readmitted in due course. It is the first recorded instance in Christian history of a bishop refusing communion to an emperor. Ambrose would do the same to Theodosius a hundred and forty years later, and would cite Babylas.

Babylas's own end came under Decius, five or six years after the encounter with Philip. He was arrested, refused to sacrifice, and was either killed or died in chains; the chronicles disagree. What no one disagreed about was where to put him: his tomb was placed in the suburb of Daphne, near the famous pagan oracle of Apollo, and the people of Antioch began going to the bishop's bones instead of the god's voice. When the emperor Julian came to consult the oracle a century later, Apollo would not speak. Julian had the bones removed. Apollo still would not speak.

> *"You shall not enter."* — Babylas to Philip the Arab, Easter night

## January 25

# Henry Suso

*Dominican mystic, c. 1295–1366*

He was a Swabian, born near Constance, and entered the Dominicans at thirteen. He took his mother's family name, Suso, rather than his father's — he preferred her — and studied at Cologne under Meister Eckhart in the years just before Eckhart was condemned. The condemnation broke him. For the next decade he punished his own body brutally: he wore a hairshirt with embedded iron nails, carved the name of Jesus into his chest with a stylus, slept on broken glass. He thought self-mortification was the proof of love.

Around forty, the chronicles say, he had a kind of second conversion. He was told in prayer that what was required of him was no longer his own pain but the patient bearing of other people's. He took off the hairshirt. He stopped the disciplines. He spent the next twenty-five years as confessor, spiritual director, and preacher to circles of laypeople and to communities of nuns, particularly the Dominican women at Töss, for whom Elsbeth Stagel was his closest confidante. The wounds came from other quarters: he was falsely accused of fathering a child by one of his penitents, hauled before his order, suspended, and eventually cleared. He bore the accusation without defense; he refused to identify the woman who had lied.

He wrote the *Little Book of Eternal Wisdom* in those years, partly autobiography and partly prayer, addressed to Christ as the bridegroom of his soul. The book taught Europe how to address Christ in the second person without bashfulness. Tauler read him; Thomas à Kempis read him; the *Imitation of Christ* breathes his air.

He died at Ulm at seventy-one, slowly, in long silence.

> *"Lord, you taught me first by your scourges, and then by your patience: the second is the harder."* — Suso, late in life

## January 26

# Paula

*Roman matron, foundress at Bethlehem, 347–404*

She was one of the great families of Rome — Scipios, Aemilii Pauli; her bloodlines went back to the Republic — and she married into another, the Toxotii. She bore five children. She was thirty-two when her husband died. The next two years were the wreckage of an aristocratic widow's grief: her circle in Rome included the most stylish Christian matrons in the city, but none of them could console her, and she half-broke for those two years.

Jerome arrived in Rome in 382 as secretary to Pope Damasus, with a working knowledge of Hebrew and an irritating habit of speaking the truth to senators. Paula met him at the house of her relative Marcella and never looked away. When Jerome's three years in Rome ended — controversially — she sold the great estates, packed her younger daughter Eustochium, and followed him east. By 386 they were both in Bethlehem. She had founded two monasteries — one for women, one for men — with her own money. She learned Hebrew well enough to read the Psalter in it. She paid the copyists who produced the manuscripts on which Jerome built the Vulgate. She corrected him when his Latin lapsed.

She died at fifty-six. Jerome, whose grief at her grave was, by his own account, dangerous to his faith, wrote her epitaph. He also wrote a long letter to Eustochium — Epistle 108 — in which he tried to record the life he had known. The letter is one of the great pieces of late ancient prose. It exists because she gave him forty years of the Latin Bible, and he could think of nothing else to give her in return.

> *"All her glory was within."* — Jerome, *Letter 108* on Paula

## January 27

# John Chrysostom

*Patriarch, preacher, doctor, c. 349–407*

He was born in Antioch and lived more than half his life there as a deacon, priest, and preacher. The Antiochenes packed the great church called the Golden Octagon to hear him; they took down his homilies in shorthand; they nicknamed him *chrysostomos*, golden-mouth, by his fortieth year. He preached through entire books of the Bible, verse by verse. He preached on poverty in the way a man preaches who has slept in poverty. His sermons on Matthew, John, the Acts, the Pauline letters, are what they are because he refused to leave the text behind.

In 398, against his preference, he was carried off to Constantinople and consecrated patriarch. The new see was too political for him. He stopped the banquets at the patriarchal palace, sold the silver to feed the poor, dressed plainly, and made the imperial court uncomfortable. The Empress Eudoxia, in particular, did not forgive being preached at: when she erected a silver statue of herself near the Hagia Sophia and the dedication ceremonies bled into the liturgy, John denounced her from the pulpit using language Eudoxia took for Herodias. He was exiled. The people rioted. He was recalled. He preached the same way. He was exiled again, and this time the long march into Pontus killed him. He died at Comana in September 407, saying as he died: *Glory to God for all things.*

Today's feast keeps the day, thirty-one years later, when the emperor Theodosius II — Eudoxia's son — brought the bones home to Constantinople. The boy wept on his mother's behalf in the cathedral she had defiled, and the city received its preacher back.

> *"Glory to God for all things."* — John Chrysostom, in dying

## January 28

# Julian Sabas

*Mesopotamian hermit, fourth century*

He lived in a cave outside Edessa. He ate a small loaf of barley bread once every few days, drank water, prayed, slept little. He had been a disciple of Macarius the Great in his youth, in the Egyptian desert; he had carried that habit east and adapted it to the rocks of Mesopotamia. By the middle of the fourth century he was the most famous holy man of Syria — and one of the very few hermits Theodoret of Cyrrhus took the trouble, much later, to write up at length.

He had two callings, both unwelcome. The first was the care of pilgrims. He could not stop them coming; the wild animals of the country, the chronicler claims, accepted him as a neighbor and would lie at the cave entrance while the visitors approached. The second was the defense of the Nicene faith. The emperor Valens, an Arian, was harassing the orthodox bishops of the East, and Antioch under the Arian patriarch Euzoius had nearly fallen out of communion with the rest of the Church. Word reached Julian. He left his cave, walked to Antioch — some hundred and fifty miles — entered the city in his ragged tunic, and preached in the basilica against the Arian terms. The orthodox party rallied. Valens died at Adrianople a few years later; the East returned to Nicaea; Julian went back to his rocks and was never seen in a city again.

He was, Theodoret says, the only man he ever met who could not be persuaded that he was important.

> *"I am of no use to anyone here; the desert is calling me back."* — Julian, on the road home from Antioch

## January 29

# Gildas

*British monk and historian, c. 500–570*

He grew up in post-Roman Britain — what the English would later call Wales — at the moment when the country was forgetting it had ever been Roman. The villas had been overgrown. The legions had been gone for a century. The Saxon settlers were past the coast and beginning to push inland, and the British kings who should have been fighting them were chiefly fighting each other. Gildas, monk, took the only weapon he was trained for, which was Latin prose, and wrote *On the Ruin and Conquest of Britain*.

The book is famous and unloved. He names the British kings he holds responsible for the disaster — Constantine the tyrant whelp of unclean Damnonia, Aurelius Caninus, Vortipor the tyrant of the Demetae, Cuneglas the fawn of the rust-stained den, Maelgwn the dragon of the island — and he prints their crimes. He turns then to the clergy and prints theirs. He preaches at the entire surviving British Church for several chapters and refuses to apologize for the volume. He says, with no apparent humor, that he is writing what no one else has been willing to say.

But he had said it, and the book traveled. It crossed to Brittany with him when he founded the monastery at Rhuys, late in life, on the Atlantic. Two centuries later Bede would read it, half believe it, and quote it as the only contemporary witness to a century of British history that left no other written record. The English Reformation read it. Milton read it. It exists because one Welshman, in his last decades, was too angry to keep quiet and too disciplined to lie.

> *"I shall not, I trust, be silent about it any longer."* — Gildas, *De Excidio Britanniae* I

## January 30

# Hyacintha Mariscotti

*Franciscan tertiary, 1585–1640*

She was the second daughter of Marcantonio Mariscotti, Count of Vignanello, and the wrong kind of nun. Her older sister had been chosen first, as the family's ecclesiastical investment, for the Franciscan tertiary convent of San Bernardino in Viterbo; Hyacintha, ten years younger, was sent there to fill the second cell when no one else wanted her. She entered in a fury. She received the habit with the look of a woman accepting a sentence.

For ten years she lived as a noblewoman who happened to take meals in a refectory. She kept a private apartment with rugs and silver, ate meat on days the rule forbade it, ordered in fine dishes from outside the cloister, and received guests in worldly clothes. Her confessor watched the comedy and waited. A severe illness around her thirty-fifth year brought the priest to her bedside not for the sacrament but for a hearing. He told her, in plain terms, what she was. She did not argue.

The remainder of her life — thirty more years — was a public penance. She gave away the apartments, returned to the rule, slept on a board, walked the streets of Viterbo begging for the city's elderly poor, founded two confraternities to take them in, and bore each humiliation from the same nuns who had watched her preen. The town nicknamed her *Suora Sacchitona*, sack-bearer. She died at fifty-five in the small cell she had once disdained, and the children of Viterbo turned out for her funeral.

It is the slow turning that the chronicler keeps for us. She had been a hypocrite first. She became a saint after.

> *"Begin now, while you have time."* — Hyacintha, in her last illness, to a young nun

## January 31

# Marcella

*Roman widow, friend of Jerome, c. 325–410*

She had been married seven months when her husband died. Her mother urged a second marriage. Marcella replied — Jerome preserves the line — that if she had wanted a man she would have looked for a husband, not an inheritance. She was perhaps twenty. She did not marry again. She took up plain dress, opened her great house on the Aventine to other widows and unmarried women who wanted to share a disciplined life, and ran from that house what amounted to the first ascetic community for women in Rome.

It was a Roman version of monasticism, and Jerome — who came to Rome two generations later — admired it. He stayed at her house when he was in the city. He taught her Hebrew; she corrected his Latin; she peppered him with questions on points of Scripture that he sometimes found taxing. When Jerome left Rome for Bethlehem and the women around Marcella began to gravitate east, Marcella stayed. Rome, she said, was where she had been put. She received the exiled Athanasius when he passed through, the exiled Peter of Alexandria after him, and a dozen lesser refugees of the Arian and Origenist controversies, in the same upper rooms where she had buried her husband.

She was old when the Goths broke into Rome in August 410. They came up the Aventine and into her house demanding gold. There was none — she had given it all away years before. They beat her to make her confess where she had hidden it. She protected her young companion Principia by drawing the worst of the violence onto herself. She died of the beating within a few days, having lived eighty-five years and never once left the city Augustine was writing about.

> *"I have looked for a husband, not an inheritance."* — Marcella, refusing a second marriage

## February 1

# Brigid of Ireland

*Abbess, patroness of Ireland, c. 451–525*

She was born somewhere in Leinster to a pagan Irish chieftain named Dubthach and a Christian slave woman named Brocca. The pairing was not voluntary on her mother's side. When Dubthach's wife learned of the pregnancy she demanded the slave be sold off, and Brocca was sent away to give birth in another man's house; Brigid, when she was old enough, was brought back to her father's holding as a serving girl. She gave away his food, his clothes, and eventually a jeweled sword to a beggar. He decided to sell her too. He drove her in a chariot to the court of the king of Leinster; while he negotiated her price, she gave the king's chariot ornaments away. The king refused the sale. *No one*, he said, *can purchase her as a servant; she is the bride of Christ.*

She took the veil from the elderly bishop Mac Caille — the tradition holds that Mac Caille, distracted, recited the words for the consecration of a bishop over her by accident, and Brigid alone of Irish women is therefore reckoned as bearing episcopal grace. Around the year 480 she founded a community at the oak of Kildare. It became the largest and longest-lasting double monastery in the Celtic Church: men and women living separately under one abbess, in two chapels and one Rule.

The miracles in her legend are domestic. She turned a vat of bathwater into beer for visiting clerics. She hung her wet cloak on a sunbeam to dry. She fed every poor person who came to the gate, and the supplies in the storehouse never ran low. The stories are old enough that some of them have probably blurred with the older Celtic goddess of the same name; what is certainly hers is the kindness so consistent that the Irish kept telling stories about it for fifteen hundred years.

She died at Kildare, well past seventy. The Irish put her on the same shelf as Patrick.

> *"She is the bride of Christ; no one can purchase her."* — the king of Leinster, refusing to buy Brigid as a slave

## February 2

# Cornelius the Centurion

*Roman officer, first Gentile convert, later bishop of Caesarea, fl. c. 40, d. by tradition c. 70*

He was a centurion of the *cohors Italica*, the Italian-recruited auxiliary cohort attached to the Roman governor's establishment at Caesarea Maritima, the harbor-city Herod had built as the administrative seat of Judea. What Acts 10 preserves of him is the formative scene of the Gentile mission. He was, Luke writes, *a devout man, and one that feared God with all his house, which gave much alms to the people, and prayed to God always.* He had not yet been baptized. He was a *God-fearer* — one of those Gentile sympathizers who attended the synagogue and kept the Sabbath but had not undergone circumcision.

The vision came at the ninth hour — three in the afternoon, the hour of the evening sacrifice in the temple at Jerusalem, the hour the early Christian community had taken for its own afternoon prayer. An angel of God came in to him, called him by name, said his prayers and his alms had ascended for a memorial before God, and instructed him to send for a man named Simon Peter who was lodging in Joppa thirty miles south. Cornelius sent two household servants and a soldier of his guard. They walked the day; Peter, in Joppa, had his own vision the next noon — the great sheet let down from heaven with all the unclean animals on it, the voice telling him three times *what God hath cleansed, that call not thou common* — and went up the coast in the morning. He came into Cornelius's house and ate at his table and preached the Christ. The Spirit fell on the household. Peter baptized them all.

The later patristic tradition — drawn from Bede, Ado, and Usuard — has Cornelius ordained bishop of Caesarea by Peter himself, the natural successor to the work begun in his own house. He governed the see for some decades and was buried there. The Roman officer who had asked an honest question of an angel, and acted on the answer, became the patron of the Gentile church. Every Christian who is not a Jew by descent stands, in some sense, in his line.

> *"What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common."* — *the voice in Peter's vision at Joppa, in the chapter that opens the Gentile church*

## February 3

# Ansgar of Hamburg-Bremen

*Apostle of the North, 801–865*

He was a Frankish boy from Picardy, raised at the abbey of Corbie under the same Adelard who had refused Charlemagne. He learned what Corbie taught: Latin, the Rule, missionary instinct. When he was twenty-five, the Danish king Harald Klak, who had just received baptism in the Carolingian court, asked for a chaplain to take home with him to the Danes. Ansgar volunteered. He spent the next forty years trying to plant the Church among peoples who were sailing south every summer to burn the churches he had grown up in.

He made two long missions to Sweden. On the first, in 829, he reached the trading port of Birka and built a small church — the first Christian church in Scandinavia. The community lasted; the king Olof gave grudging permission for the mission to continue; Ansgar baptized merchants and slaves and a few of the noble families. On the second, in his fifties, he came back to find the church still there but the converts struggling and his missionary clergy thin. He gave them another decade. He was made archbishop of Hamburg, then (after the Vikings burned Hamburg in 845) of Hamburg-Bremen jointly; the pope made him papal legate for the whole of Scandinavia. He never had political backing strong enough to protect the work.

He did not see Scandinavia converted in his lifetime. The mission collapsed for half a century after his death and was resumed only under his successors. What he had built — the church at Birka, the see at Hamburg-Bremen, the precedent of a Frankish bishop going north — would eventually carry the Faith into Sweden and Norway, but only at a hundred years' remove.

*If only the Lord would grant me one miracle*, he said in old age — *that he might make me a better man.* It is the only miracle he prayed for, and the only one he wanted credited to his account.

> *"If only the Lord would grant me one miracle — that he might make me a better man."* — Ansgar, in old age

## February 4

# Nicholas of Studios

*Abbot, iconodule confessor, c. 793–868*

He was a Cretan boy sent to Constantinople for schooling at ten, placed at the great urban monastery of Studios under his uncle Theophanes. He took the habit in his teens. Studios, by his middle age, was the largest monastery in the Byzantine capital and the central holdout of the orthodox party in the second iconoclast crisis. The emperors Leo V, Michael II, and Theophilus had each, in succession, restored the imperial ban on holy images. The Studite monks had each, in succession, refused.

Nicholas stood with his community. When Theodore the Studite, the great abbot of the preceding generation, was exiled for refusing the ban, Nicholas went with him and shared the exile. When Theodore returned, Nicholas returned. When Theophilus revived the persecution in his own reign, Nicholas was beaten in public, dragged through the streets, imprisoned. The chroniclers describe him as a quiet man who would not argue with his interrogators; he simply said, each time, that he could not stop venerating the image of Christ because Christ had a face.

He outlived three iconoclast emperors. In 843, under the empress Theodora, the icons were restored definitively and the Feast of Orthodoxy was instituted. Nicholas was eventually made abbot of Studios — twice, in fact, with an interval of resignation between — and governed his community to his death at seventy-five. The monastery he handed on was the bones-deep guardian of Byzantine orthodoxy; it survived intact for another four centuries.

What he had refused, all his life, was the imperial offer of an easier image: an offer to settle for the word *without* the icon, the gospel *without* the face. He could not. The Word, he kept saying, had become flesh and dwelt among us, and the flesh had had a face.

> *"I cannot stop venerating the image, because Christ had a face."* — Nicholas, before his interrogators

## February 5

# Agatha of Sicily

*Virgin and martyr, c. 231–251*

She was a Sicilian girl of a noble Catanian family, perhaps fifteen, when the Roman governor Quintianus took notice of her. He proposed marriage, then position, then his protection, then his anger. Agatha — already consecrated to Christ in her own intention, if not yet by veil — refused each in turn. He had her arrested under the lapsed but still-usable Decian edict against Christians, and turned her over to a woman named Aphrodisia who ran a house of ill repute. Aphrodisia tried for thirty days to break the girl by every means at her disposal. She did not break.

Quintianus reclaimed her for the tribunal and ordered a public torture. The details — given in the Acts read at her tomb a generation later — are graphic enough that the medieval iconographers would never settle for a single image: she is most often shown carrying her own severed breasts on a tray. In the cell that night, the chronicler says, an old man entered. He told her he was Peter, the apostle of Christ, and he healed the wounds with his touch. The next morning Quintianus had her rolled over hot coals. She died in the cell that evening, asking Christ to receive her.

The local memory was stubborn. Within a generation her tomb was a pilgrimage site; within a century her name was in the Roman Canon, between Lucy and Cecilia. The Catanians believe she still protects them from Etna: when the volcano rumbles, her veil — the small surviving relic of the saint — is brought out and carried in procession against the lava, and the eruptions have always stopped before reaching the city.

Whatever the Acts have added to her story over the centuries, the Catanians who keep her name on February 5 are not honoring a fiction. They are honoring a girl whose tomb their great-grandparents knelt at.

> *"Lord, who hast preserved me from infancy, take now my soul to thyself."* — Agatha, in her cell

## February 6

# Vedast of Arras

*Bishop, c. 470–540*

He was a Gallo-Roman priest serving the diocese of Toul when Clovis, the young Frankish king who had just won the battle of Tolbiac and committed himself to baptism, halted his army at Toul on the way home to Reims. Clovis needed a catechist; he had pledged himself to a faith he had only the haziest idea of. The bishop sent Vedast. For two weeks the king and the priest rode together while Vedast taught him the gospel as best he could on horseback. On the road, the chronicler says, they came upon a beggar who was blind. Clovis asked Vedast to do something. Vedast prayed; the beggar was given his sight; the king was, in the same hour, given his Lord. Clovis was baptized at Reims that Christmas by Remigius.

Remigius took notice of the catechist. Within three years Vedast had been consecrated bishop of Arras — a city in northern Gaul that had lost its Christian community entirely during the Frankish invasions a century earlier. He found the cathedral in ruins; he found wild animals living in the nave; he found, the biographer says, a bear in the bishop's quarters. He had nothing to start with except the title.

He started anyway. He preached. He rebuilt. He founded a school. He ordained a small clergy and trained them. Within twenty years there was a working diocese where there had been only memory. He served Arras for forty years and added the see of Cambrai to his charge for the last two decades. He died at ninety, having buried two generations of priests he had ordained himself.

What he gave the Franks was not a new Church; it was an old Church restored, twice over: once to a king who had agreed to receive it, and once to a city that had nearly forgotten what receiving it meant.

> *"Even the stones, if we will let them, will receive him back."* — Vedast, at the rebuilding of Arras cathedral

## February 7

# Romuald of Camaldoli

*Founder of the Camaldolese, 951–1027*

He was the son of a Ravennate nobleman, Sergius of the Onesti, and the family business in Romuald's young manhood was violent. He was twenty when his father killed a kinsman in a duel over a property quarrel and required Romuald to be present as a witness. The killing broke something in him. He walked from the field to the Benedictine abbey of Sant'Apollinare in Classe outside Ravenna and asked to be received as a novice. The monks took him in.

Within a few years the rule at Classe seemed to him too easy. He left to seek something stricter — first under a hermit named Marinus on the lagoon at Cuxa in the Pyrenees, then back in Italy, wandering for thirty years and founding hermitages as he went, restoring abbeys, refusing every offer of a settled abbacy. He was a strange figure to the people who watched him: a Ravennate aristocrat in a rough tunic walking from one mountain to the next, with a few disciples trailing him at a respectful distance.

Around 1023, on a high meadow in the Casentino above the Arno, a benefactor named Maldolus gave him land for a monastery. Romuald built two: a community house for cenobitic monks at the foot of the slope, and a cluster of separate hermit cells above. The combination was new in the West — the eremitical and the communal in one Rule, the brothers spending part of their lives in each. He called the place Camaldoli, from the donor's name. The Camaldolese exist to this day.

He died at seventy-six, alone in his cell, after telling his disciples to go and let him sleep. They found him in the morning.

The instruction he left them is the shortest rule he ever wrote. It fits on a page. *Sit in your cell as in paradise. Empty yourself completely and sit waiting, content with the grace of God.*

> *"Sit in your cell as in paradise; put the world behind you and forget it."* — Romuald, *Brief Rule*

## February 8

# Stephen of Muret

*Founder of the Grandmontines, 1045–1124*

He was the son of the Viscount of Thiers in Auvergne, sent at twelve as a page to the bishop of Thiers, and taken by the bishop on a long journey to Bari in southern Italy where they met a community of Calabrian Greek hermits living on bread and prayer. Stephen, the boy who had been groomed for a Frankish ecclesiastical career, lost his interest in careers on that mountain. He stayed twelve years among the Greek hermits. He came home in his thirties and walked into the forest of Muret in the Limousin and stopped walking.

He built a hut. Other men came; he taught them what the Calabrians had taught him; he refused to take any title. He would not be ordained priest; he would not be called abbot; he insisted, when pressed, that the only Rule his community would follow was the Gospel. *We have no rule but the Gospel*, he said, when papal visitors asked him to produce one. He gave the brothers a few practical guidelines: silence at meals, manual labor, total poverty, no possessions of any kind. They were not to own land. They were not to claim ownership of the forest the hermitage stood in. If a king wished to give them an estate, they were to politely refuse it. They were to live on what people brought them, when people brought it.

He died at seventy-nine at Muret. His successors moved the community a few miles to a place called Grandmont and gave it a more formal Rule, partly to satisfy Rome and partly because the original could not survive his death. The Order of Grandmont survived for six hundred years.

What he left was a small fierce idea: that the gospel itself, if a community would actually live it, was enough.

> *"We have no rule but the Gospel."* — Stephen, to a papal visitor

## February 9

# Cyril of Alexandria

*Patriarch, doctor, c. 376–444*

He inherited the see of Alexandria from his uncle Theophilus in 412 — at a moment when the city was the most theologically combative on earth — and governed it for thirty-two years. He was a difficult man. He could be authoritarian; he was involved in some of the ugliest civic violence of his day; his Christological vocabulary was sharp enough that even his admirers spent the next century trying to soften it. He is also one of the half-dozen most important theologians the Church has produced.

The fight that defined him was with Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople, over how to speak of Mary. Nestorius preferred to call her Mother of Christ rather than Mother of God; the distinction, he said, protected the Lord's full divinity from being entangled with a human birth. Cyril answered that the distinction *separated* what God had joined. *We do not say that the Word became flesh by changing into flesh*, he wrote in his second letter to Nestorius — *the change is in our nature, not his.* If Mary was not Mother of God, the child she bore was not God; the salvation of the human race had not happened in her womb. The Council of Ephesus in 431 ruled for Cyril.

He spent the next decade trying to make peace with the Antiochene theologians who shared most of his beliefs but few of his words. The Formula of Reunion in 433 was his work. It cost him politically; he had to retract some of the harshest phrases he had used. He did not enjoy retracting; he did it anyway, because the Church mattered to him more than the victory.

The Council of Chalcedon, twenty years after his death, would build on his Christology and correct his excesses. He had given the next generation both the language and the lesson — that even when you are right, you may have spoken too sharply, and the truth will outlast your sharpness only if you can be made gentler.

> *"We do not say that the Word became flesh by changing into flesh; the change is in our nature, not his."* — Cyril, *Second Letter to Nestorius*

## February 10

# Scholastica

*Abbess, sister of Benedict, c. 480–547*

She was Benedict's sister — twin, some traditions say — consecrated to God from her childhood, and when he settled at Monte Cassino in his fifties she founded a small women's house at Plombariola, five miles off, and brought a community of nuns to live there. They followed his Rule. They saw each other once a year.

The rule of his abbey did not allow him to receive women, even his sister, inside the monastery walls. So once a year they would meet at a small farmhouse on the road between the two communities, eat a meal together, and talk for the day. Gregory the Great, in the *Dialogues*, preserves what happened at their last such meeting. They had eaten. They had talked the afternoon away. It was getting dark and Benedict rose to go back to his monastery for night office. Scholastica asked him to stay. She wanted to keep talking. He told her — gently, firmly — that he could not break his Rule. She bent her head over the table and prayed in silence for a moment. *And then*, Gregory writes, *the sky which had been clear without a cloud became dark with so violent a storm that neither Benedict nor any of his brethren could put a foot outside the door.*

Benedict looked at her in something between vexation and amazement. *What have you done?* he said. *I asked you to stay*, she answered, *and you would not; I asked God, and he was kind.* They talked through the night. He went back at first light.

She died three days later. Benedict, in his own cell that morning, looked out the window and saw a dove flying upward into the sky; he knew it was his sister. He sent the brothers to bring her body. He buried her in the tomb he had prepared for himself.

It is one of the most quietly perfect stories in the *Dialogues*. The sister, with one prayer, gives her brother the gift of one more night of his sister.

> *"I asked you to stay, and you would not; I asked God, and he was kind."* — Scholastica to Benedict

## February 11

# Calogerus of Ravenna

*Bishop and disciple of Apollinaris, c. 30–c. 130*

He was a Greek by birth — the AASS reads his name *Kalokeros,* "the well-horned one" — and came to Italy as a young man, perhaps with the small Greek-speaking colony that had grown up around the harbor at Ravenna in the late first century. His conversion was the work of Apollinaris, the disciple of Peter sent from Antioch to plant the church at Ravenna in the late forties. The chronicle preserves the small detail: among the converts of Apollinaris's first twelve years, he ordained two priests, *Adhretus and Calocerus*, and made two deacons, *Marcianus the noble and Eleuchadius the philosopher*. Calogerus was perhaps in his early twenties.

He served Apollinaris as one of his vicar-presbyters for the next four decades — through the exile under Vespasian, the second exile under Titus, the eventual return, and the martyrdom under Domitian around the year 79. Three bishops succeeded Apollinaris in turn: Adhretus, then the deacon Eleuchadius, then the deacon Marcianus. Calogerus, by long-standing presence in the see, was the obvious next choice when Marcianus died around 117. The chronicle preserves the medieval detail that the election was confirmed by *a dove descending from heaven on the candidate*, the same form the Ravenna tradition gave to all the early Apollinaris-line elections.

He governed the see through the reign of Hadrian. Hieronymus Rubeus preserves the line: *under Hadrian's tranquil rule, by Calogerus's tireless preaching and God's strengthening of his words, almost all the people of Ravenna abandoned the false gods and embraced the holy religion.* He died, the chronicler adds, *in green old age*, having passed his hundredth year. He was buried at Classe, the harbor-suburb at the mouth of the Po, in the church his successor Probus would later build over his bones. The Saracens destroyed it in the ninth century. The relics were translated to the cathedral in 923, where they have lain since.

The young Greek convert who had heard Apollinaris preach in a tribune's house outlived four of his teacher's successors and finished, at a great age, the conversion of the city the apostles had begun.

> *"Almost all the people of Ravenna abandoned the false gods and embraced the holy religion."* — Hieronymus Rubeus, *Historia Ravennatis* on Calogerus

## February 12

# Benedict of Aniane

*Carolingian monastic reformer, c. 750–821*

He was born Witiza, a Visigothic noble in Languedoc, raised at the Frankish court as a hostage and serving Pepin the Short and then Charlemagne as a royal cup-bearer. He was a soldier — at Charlemagne's Italian campaign of 773 — when he saw his elder brother go under in the river Tessin during a fording, and he leapt in to save him. He nearly drowned himself. The chronicle says that as the water closed over his head, he made the vow that if God brought him out, he would leave the king's service. God brought him out. He left.

He went first to the abbey of Saint-Seine near Dijon, where he took the name Benedict — out of admiration for the founder, whose Rule he had begun to study seriously — and stayed several years as a novice and then choir monk. He found Saint-Seine too lax. He returned to his own estates at Aniane in the Languedoc and founded a stricter house there. Other reformers heard of him. By 814 he was effectively the leading monastic figure in the empire, and Charlemagne's son Louis the Pious — who had grown up admiring him — made him general overseer of all imperial monasteries.

At the Synod of Aachen in 817 he led the Carolingian Church to a single binding standard of Benedictine observance for all royal foundations. *One Rule and one custom*, he insisted. The empire's monasteries, which had drifted into local variants for two centuries, were brought back into a common pattern. He built one more monastery, Inden, near Aachen, as the model house for the empire. He died there in 821.

What he gave the Latin Church was uniformity at a moment when it could have lost its bearings, and a tradition that would feed Cluny, the Cistercians, and every reform of Benedictine life that followed.

> *"One Rule and one custom."* — Benedict of Aniane, at the Synod of Aachen

## February 13

# Modomnoc of Ossory

*Irish bishop, sixth century*

He was an Irish nobleman of the line of Niall of the Nine Hostages, sent in his youth to Wales to study at the great monastic school of David at Mynyw. He stayed there ten years. He took his turn at all the labors of the house — the strict ones, of cold water and barley bread and silence — and was given, at last, the charge of the abbey beehives. He proved unusually good at the work. The chronicles say the bees grew accustomed to him, that they swarmed without stinging him, that they followed him when he walked.

When he had finished his training and David sent him home to evangelize his own people, Modomnoc went down to the harbor with the small boat his abbot had given him. He had loaded his books and his few possessions. As the boat began to pull away from the Pembrokeshire coast, the entire colony of bees from the abbey hives rose up in a cloud and followed him out over the water. Modomnoc, alarmed at having stolen the abbey's livelihood, turned the boat around and rowed back to give the bees up. David, smiling, told him to keep them. *They have chosen you*, he said. *They will be the first Christians among the Irish bees.*

The bees crossed the Irish Sea with him. He landed on the coast of what would later be the kingdom of Ossory, in Leinster, and set them up in a hive at his hermitage there. He was eventually made bishop of Ossory. He preached, baptized, founded small churches in the woods, kept the hive. He died at an advanced age.

The Irish, who appreciate this kind of saint, still call him the patron of beekeepers. Welsh tradition holds that every Irish bee descends from a Welsh queen who once chose Modomnoc.

> *"They have chosen you; they will be the first Christians among the Irish bees."* — David of Wales, sending Modomnoc home

## February 14

# Cyril

*Apostle to the Slavs, 827–869*

He was born Constantine, the youngest of seven children of a Greek naval officer at Thessalonica. The family spoke Greek at home and Slavic with the neighbors — Thessalonica in the ninth century was half-Slavic and increasingly so — and the boy picked up both with the easy ear of a child raised in a frontier town. He was brilliant. The Byzantine court took notice; by his early twenties he was teaching philosophy at the imperial academy in Constantinople and being sent on diplomatic missions to the Arabs and the Khazars. His elder brother Methodius, a more reserved man, was already a monk.

In 862 the Slavic prince Rastislav of Moravia asked the emperor Michael III for missionaries who could teach his people the faith in their own language. The brothers were sent. The first thing they did, before they left, was design an alphabet — the Glagolitic, the parent of Cyrillic — to put Slavic into writing for the first time. They translated the gospels, the psalter, and the liturgy. They preached and baptized in Moravia for four years. They ran into Frankish missionaries who insisted that the Latin Mass alone was valid, and Cyril took the question to Rome.

Pope Hadrian II received them with honor and approved the Slavonic liturgy outright. Cyril, exhausted by the journey and by the years in Moravia, did not see Moravia again. He fell ill in Rome, took the monastic habit and a new name — *Cyril* — fifty days before his death, and was buried in the basilica of San Clemente, where his tomb still is. Methodius would carry on the work for sixteen more years, imprisoned twice by Frankish bishops for his trouble.

A third of European Christianity prays in a language Cyril wrote down by hand.

> *"O Lord, who hast given me grace to preach thy gospel in the tongue of the Slavs, gather them now into thy holy Church."* — Cyril, dying in Rome

## February 15

# Sigfrid of Sweden

*Missionary bishop, d. c. 1045*

He was an English priest — most likely from York, possibly from Glastonbury — when in the 1020s the English king's missionary office asked him to take a small band of English clergy north to Sweden. The Swedish king Olof Skötkonung had been baptized a generation earlier but the country was still effectively pagan; the mission would be slow and dangerous. Sigfrid took three of his nephews with him — Unaman, Sunaman, and Vinaman — and a few other priests. They sailed up the coast and crossed into Värend, in southern Sweden, where the local lords had asked for missionaries.

The work went well. Sigfrid baptized the regional king and built a small wooden church at Växjö, the first in the region. He left the three young priests to keep the church while he travelled further north into other Swedish provinces. He was a month away when a band of pagan men of Värend broke into the chapel at Växjö, killed the three young priests, took their heads, weighted them, and threw them into a nearby lake. Sigfrid came back to a community in mourning. He recovered the heads from the lake (the chronicle insists they were found together, the lake giving them up); he buried his nephews beside the chapel; he continued the mission.

King Olof, when he heard of the murders, ordered the perpetrators executed and their lands forfeit. Sigfrid went to him and asked for their lives. He could not, he said, baptize Sweden over the corpses of pagans whose hearts the gospel had not yet reached. The king relented on the executions but kept the fines. Sigfrid asked, then, that the fines be given to him to support the building of more churches. They were.

He died in old age, having outlived the murderers by decades, and was buried at Växjö beside his nephews.

> *"I cannot baptize Sweden over their corpses."* — Sigfrid, asking the king for the murderers' lives

## February 16

# Gilbert of Sempringham

*Founder of the Gilbertines, c. 1083–1189*

He was the son of a Norman knight and an Anglo-Saxon mother — a child of the post-Conquest blending — born at Sempringham in Lincolnshire and educated in France, where he prepared for the priesthood without much love for the Norman world he was being trained into. He came home in his thirties, was ordained, and was given the small parish of Sempringham by his father, who happened to be the local landowner. He thought he would die a parish priest. He was almost forty.

Seven young women in his parish came to him with the same request: they wanted to live as anchoresses, walled into cells against the parish church, and would he be their spiritual father? He thought about it. He built them cells. He gave them a Rule based on the Rule of Saint Benedict. He found that village women began visiting the anchoresses to ask for prayers and to bring them food, and the visitors became frequent enough that he gathered them into lay sisters. The lay sisters needed to be served by lay brothers. The growing community needed clergy of its own. By 1147, when Gilbert went to Cîteaux to ask the Cistercians to take his community under their wing, what he had built was a double monastery — men and women in two enclosed groups, ruled by one abbot — and the Cistercians, looking at it, declined. Pope Eugenius III instead approved Gilbert as the head of a new order, the Gilbertines. It would be the only religious order in history founded on English soil.

He outlived all seven of the original anchoresses. He outlived his Cistercian friend Aelred of Rievaulx. He outlived Becket, whose cause he had supported and for whose sake he had been briefly imprisoned by Henry II. He died at one hundred and six, blind, in the priory at Sempringham.

> *"They wanted to live for God, and asked me to help them. What more could I refuse?"* — Gilbert, on the founding of his order

## February 17

# Finan of Lindisfarne

*Bishop, d. 661*

He was an Irish monk of Iona, trained in the Columban tradition, when Aidan died at Lindisfarne in 651 and the brothers there asked Iona to send a successor. Iona sent Finan. He inherited a diocese that covered most of Northumbria — twice the size of the kingdom Aidan had begun with — and a missionary task that was very much unfinished.

He built. Aidan's wooden church on the tidal island of Lindisfarne had grown too small. Finan put up a new one of split oak roofed with thatch, large enough for the brothers and the visiting kings and the catechumens together. He continued Aidan's habit of constant travel through the kingdom, on foot, preaching at the country crossroads where Christianity was still only a court religion. Bede records two of his great baptisms: Peada, the Mercian prince, who married Alhflaed of Northumbria on the condition that he and his nobles receive baptism; and Sigeberht, the king of the East Saxons, who came north to be instructed and went home converted with four priests Finan had given him. From those two baptisms came the Christianization of central and southeastern England.

He did not enjoy controversy and could not always avoid it. The Roman observance of Easter was advancing in southern England; Wilfrid, just back from Rome, was arguing for it; Iona held to the Celtic computation. Finan defended the Celtic side without rancor and died before the Synod of Whitby could force the question. His successor Colman would lose at Whitby; Finan, mercifully, did not have to.

He died in 661 after ten years at Lindisfarne. Bede, no friend of the Celtic computation, nevertheless writes of Finan with respect bordering on affection. *He was a man*, Bede says, *zealous in good works, of an apostolic conversation.*

> *"Zealous in good works, of an apostolic conversation."* — Bede on Finan

## February 18

# Theotonius of Coimbra

*Augustinian prior, 1082–1162*

He was born at Tui in Galicia, just north of the Portuguese border, and ordained priest by the age of twelve — an unusually early ordination, granted on the strength of his learning and the patronage of his uncle, the bishop. He served as a parish priest at Viseu for several years before the seriousness of his vocation outran the parish. He made the long pilgrimage to Jerusalem at twenty-five. He made it again twelve years later. On the second return he stopped at Coimbra, in central Portugal, and decided to stay.

The young kingdom of Portugal — newly independent of León — needed a school of canons regular at its old Roman capital. Theotonius and a small band of like-minded priests founded the monastery of Santa Cruz in 1131, following the Rule of Saint Augustine. Theotonius was elected prior. Under him, Santa Cruz became the central religious house of the new country: it trained the kingdom's clergy, copied its manuscripts, organized its parishes, and served as confessor and chronicler to the royal court.

King Afonso I, the first king of Portugal, made Theotonius his confessor and was famously deferential to him. The chronicle preserves a moment that the courtiers loved to retell. Queen Mafalda once interrupted a Mass that Theotonius was celebrating; she had only meant to be present. He paused, turned, and asked her courteously to wait outside until he was finished, since the Mass was for God and not for her presence. She waited. After Mass, in the sacristy, she apologized. *Madam*, he said, *whoever lives well will reign with God; whoever lives ill will reign nowhere at all.*

He governed Santa Cruz for thirty-one years and died at eighty, having seen the kingdom of Portugal grow up around the cloister he had founded.

> *"Whoever lives well will reign with God."* — Theotonius to Queen Mafalda

## February 19

# Conrad of Piacenza

*Franciscan hermit, 1290–1351*

He was a Piacenzan nobleman, married young to a young woman named Euphrosyne, and he spent his early twenties hunting in the country estates north of the city. One autumn afternoon he and his companions were trying to flush game out of a thicket. They set fire to the brush. The wind rose. The fire spread to the surrounding fields, then to a village, then to a stretch of forest. By nightfall the chronicle is reckoning the cost in burned crops, lost houses, and one dead farmhand.

A peasant who had been seen near the original thicket was arrested. The magistrate sentenced him to death. The day before the execution, Conrad — who had been silent through the trial out of cowardice — went to the court and confessed that the fire was his. The peasant was released; Conrad was fined the value of his estates and a great deal more. He paid. He had nothing left.

He and Euphrosyne, after the ruin, made a decision. She entered the Poor Clares at Piacenza. Conrad became a Franciscan tertiary and walked south. He stopped at last on a hillside outside Noto in Sicily, in a cave that had once been a pagan shrine, and stayed there forty years. He prayed. He healed the sick who came to him. He counseled the bishop of Syracuse and several visiting pilgrims and the local shepherds in roughly equal proportion. The Sicilians, who appreciate this kind of saint, called him *il Beato Conrado* well before Rome confirmed it.

He died kneeling in his cave at sixty. The peasants of Noto buried him in the cave, walled it up, and kept the key.

He had set the fire that almost cost an innocent man his life. The rest of his own life he spent letting the same fire purify what it had failed to destroy.

> *"What I burned was my country; what was burned in me is who I was."* — Conrad, in the cave

## February 20

# Eleutherius of Tournai

*Bishop, born in exile, c. 456–531*

He was born at Blandain, two leagues outside Tournai, the year Childeric began pressing the Christian Gallo-Roman population out of the Scheldt valley. His parents were Christians of the *Menapii*, the Gallic-Romanized people who had held the country between the Scheldt and the North Sea since the late Republic. The family had been driven out of Tournai in the 480s when Clovis began his wars against the last Roman administration in northern Gaul. The boy grew up in the refugee community at Blandain. Saint Medard's mother was his cousin, and the two boys were companions.

The community at Blandain raised a small church to Saint Peter when Clovis had completed his conversion and the persecution relaxed; Eleutherius was ordained priest there in his early thirties. The diocese of Tournai had been vacant since the death of its first bishop Theodore in 500. After a year's vacancy the Christian community elected Eleutherius to succeed him, and he took the see at the beginning of 501.

He governed it for thirty years. The diocese was the largest in northern Gaul, running from the upper Scheldt across to the North Sea, and the Christian population at his consecration was perhaps one in ten. He spent three decades baptizing. The acts preserve the small details: he traveled on foot, kept perpetual fast in Lent and the Ember weeks, ordained eleven hundred priests and deacons by his own count in a letter to Pope Hormisdas, founded the cathedral chapter and its school, and wrote a now-lost catechetical treatise that Gregory of Tours had praised.

His most famous engagement was with the Arians of the upper Scheldt, who had begun pressing their teaching into the community he had spent twenty years bringing into the church. He answered with a Quadragesimal preaching tour from Tournai south to the Aisne, calling the Arian families back to Nicene communion. The tour worked. The Arian party at Tournai was substantially gone by his death.

He died on the twenty-fifth of February, 531, and was buried beside Medard at Blandain. The great Notre-Dame de Tournai that rose over the next millennium stands on the same foundations, his bones still under the high altar.

> *"Recede a peccato, et accede ad veritatem."* — Eleutherius, in the closing exhortation of every recorded Quadragesimal sermon: *withdraw from sin, and approach the truth*

## February 21

# Peter Damian

*Cardinal, Camaldolese, doctor, 1007–1072*

He was born at Ravenna, the youngest of many children, into a family that did not want another mouth. His mother is said to have refused to nurse him until a neighbor woman shamed her; his older brother, after their parents died, kept him as a household servant rather than send him to school. He was rescued, when he was perhaps twelve, by another brother — the archpriest of Ravenna, also named Damian, who paid for his education and gave him his name. Peter Damian: the boy of Damian, the brother. He never stopped using it.

He had a remarkable mind. He was teaching at Parma in his twenties. He could have stayed there for life as a famous master. Instead, in 1035, he travelled north to the Camaldolese hermitage at Fonte Avellana that Romuald had founded a decade earlier, and asked to be admitted. He was elected prior within a few years. He governed for the next two decades, all the while continuing to write — sermons, letters, treatises on prayer, reform tracts. The *Liber Gomorrhianus*, his great attack on clerical corruption, made him hated by half the bishops in Italy and indispensable to the reform pope Leo IX.

He was created cardinal-bishop of Ostia, very much against his preference, by Stephen IX in 1057. He went where the popes sent him — to Milan to prosecute the abuses of the cathedral chapter, to Cluny to mediate a dispute, to Germany to negotiate a royal marriage — and asked at every opportunity to be allowed to go home to his hermitage. He was finally let go in 1067.

He lived five more years at Fonte Avellana. He died on the way back from one last legate's errand to Ravenna, at Faenza, on February 22, 1072. The brothers carried him home and buried him at the hermitage.

> *"My hermitage was given me; my office was forced on me; my death is owed to neither."* — Peter Damian, in a letter

## February 22

# Margaret of Cortona

*Franciscan tertiary, 1247–1297*

She was born to a peasant family at Laviano in Tuscany; her mother died when she was seven; her stepmother was cruel; she ran away at seventeen with a young nobleman named Arsenio who had seen her on the road. He took her to his castle at Montepulciano. For nine years she lived there as his mistress and bore him a son. He never married her. He never quite refused to.

Late one autumn afternoon Arsenio's dog came back from a hunt without him and would not stop pulling at Margaret's skirt. She followed it into the woods. The dog led her to a clearing where Arsenio had been killed by his enemies and dumped in a covered pit. She uncovered him. The chronicle does not soften her response. She took the child by the hand, walked back to the castle, gave away every piece of jewelry and clothing Arsenio had given her, walked the twenty miles to her father's house, and asked to be taken in. Her stepmother refused. Her father, too weak to stand against her, refused. She walked, with her son, the next forty miles to Cortona, where she had heard the Franciscans took penitents.

The Franciscan friars Giovanni and Giunta examined her at the door, took her in, placed her in the care of two laywomen. She did three years of probation. She wore a hairshirt. She had wanted to be led through the city of Montepulciano with a halter around her neck as a public penitent; the friars refused her this, judging it more vanity than penance. She accepted the refusal. She gave the rest of her life to the Cortona poor: she founded the city's hospital for the destitute sick, *Santa Maria della Misericordia*, and worked in it daily for two decades.

She died at fifty, having spent twenty-three years undoing nine.

> *"My past, Lord, is a long road I can never walk back. Walk it with me forward."* — Margaret, in a recorded ecstasy

## February 23

# Polycarp of Smyrna

*Bishop and martyr, c. 69–155*

He was the bridge between the apostles and the Fathers. He had been a disciple, in his youth, of John — the same John who had stood at the cross and survived to old age on Patmos. He was made bishop of Smyrna around 90 and held the see for sixty-five years, longer than most bishops have lived. Irenaeus, as a boy in Smyrna, sat at his feet; Irenaeus would later carry Polycarp's testimony into Gaul and into the second century's defense of orthodoxy. *I remember the very place*, Irenaeus would write, *where the blessed Polycarp used to sit and speak.*

He was eighty-six when the persecution under Antoninus Pius came to Smyrna. The proconsul arrested him on a country estate and brought him into the stadium of the city. The crowd shouted for his blood. The proconsul, who would have preferred not to execute an old man, asked him to curse Christ and go home. Polycarp's answer is preserved in the *Martyrdom of Polycarp*, written by the church at Smyrna within months of the event: *Eighty-six years have I served him, and he has done me no wrong; how then can I blaspheme my king, who has saved me?*

The proconsul ordered him burned. They built the pyre. They tied him to the stake — Polycarp asked that he not be nailed, saying the same God who had given him strength to come up the steps would give him strength to stand in the fire. The fire was lit. The flames, the eyewitness writes, made an arch around him and did not touch him. The executioner had to run him through with a sword. The Christians who collected what remained of his bones for burial wrote down everything they had seen.

The Letter is the oldest non-biblical Christian martyrdom we have. It exists because they could not bear to lose what he had said.

> *"Eighty-six years have I served him, and he has done me no wrong; how then can I blaspheme my king, who has saved me?"* — Polycarp, before the proconsul

## February 24

# Matthias the Apostle

*Apostle, replacement for Judas, first century*

He appears in Scripture in nine verses. After the Ascension, Peter stood up in the upper room — among the hundred and twenty who had gathered to wait for Pentecost — and proposed that the betrayer's place be filled. The replacement had to be a man who had been with them *from the baptism of John until the day when he was taken up from us*. Two men met the criterion: Joseph called Barsabbas, surnamed Justus, and Matthias. The apostles prayed; they cast lots; the lot fell on Matthias. He took his place among the Twelve.

Almost everything else about him is tradition. He preached, the early historians say, in Judea first, then north into Cappadocia, then east into Colchis along the Black Sea — what is now Georgia. The Caucasus mountains have an ancient memory of him; the Georgian Church still claims him as their first apostle. The accounts of his death vary. Some say he was stoned at Jerusalem and beheaded for good measure; some say he was crucified in Colchis; the Coptic tradition has him perish in the Roman colonies of North Africa. The Bollandists in their Februarius volume gather all these and admit they cannot reconcile them.

What is certain is the moment of his selection. The Twelve had been broken by one of their own; the gospel was about to be carried out to the world; the gap had to be filled before Pentecost. Matthias filled it. He did not boast. He did not refuse. The lot fell on him and he stood up. The Church has remembered him ever since as the man who was willing to step into a vacancy that a friend had created by his own betrayal.

That, too, is a vocation. Every Church on earth has its quietly replaced Judases, its men and women who took the empty chair without saying a word.

> *"Show us which of these two you have chosen."* — the prayer of the apostles, before the casting of lots (Acts 1:24)

## February 25

# Walburga of Heidenheim

*Anglo-Saxon abbess in Germany, c. 710–779*

She was a Wessex princess, niece of Boniface, sister of Willibald and Winibald — a family that gave the eighth century four of its great missionary saints. She had been educated at the great abbey of Wimborne in Dorset, taken the veil there, and lived the quiet life of an English nun for about twenty-five years when Boniface, deep into the German mission, wrote home asking for women to help train the wives and daughters of his new converts. Walburga, perhaps forty, volunteered. She sailed in 748.

The crossing nearly killed her. A storm broke over the small boat in the North Sea and the sailors were terrified; Walburga, the chronicler says, knelt on the deck and prayed, and the storm eased almost in the next moment. She came ashore at Antwerp and walked the rest of the way to Mainz. Boniface and her brother Winibald placed her at the double monastery they were building at Heidenheim — men under Winibald, women under another abbess, joint house under the same Rule. When Winibald died in 761, Walburga was made the head of both houses. She governed them for the next eighteen years.

She was literate in three languages — Anglo-Saxon, Latin, German — and the only writer in the family. She composed in Latin the Lives of her brothers Willibald (his account of his pilgrimage to the Holy Land is the earliest English travel narrative we have) and Winibald, and her own letters are among the first writings by an Englishwoman that survive. She is the first English writer of either sex whose name we know.

She died at Heidenheim at sixty-nine. Her relics were translated to Eichstätt a century later and began to exude a sweet liquid — *Walpurgis oil* — which the local Christians collected for healing.

> *"The Lord is in the storm and after the storm; he is not bound to either."* — Walburga, on her crossing

## February 26

# Porphyry of Gaza

*Bishop, c. 347–420*

He was a Macedonian, born at Thessalonica into a wealthy family, and he gave away his inheritance in his twenties to follow the desert tradition. He went first to the Egyptian *kellia*, then to a cave near Jericho, then to Jerusalem, where he became a priest with charge of the relic of the True Cross. The patriarch sent him to Gaza in 395 as the new bishop. Gaza, on the southern Palestinian coast, was the last great pagan city in the East — the temple of Marnas at its center was the largest in Palestine, the priests of Marnas effectively ran the municipal council, and the small Christian community lived under constant low-level pressure.

Porphyry started small. He built. He visited his priests, baptized the few catechumens who came forward, and waited. A long drought in his fourth year ended with the prayers of his community while the pagan rites called for rain went unanswered; the Christian community doubled. He travelled to Constantinople — twice — to ask the empress Eudoxia for an imperial order to close the temples of Gaza. On the second visit, in 402, she granted it. Imperial troops dismantled the Marnion and the eight smaller pagan temples of the city. Porphyry built a great Christian basilica, the Eudoxiana, on the site of the Marnion, paid for by the empress.

The closing of the temples was not gentle. Mark the Deacon, who was with Porphyry through the whole episode and wrote his Life, does not pretend otherwise. The pagan resistance was real; some Christians were killed in the disturbances; Porphyry, in his old age, grieved over the violence even as he refused to undo what had been done. *I would rather have built the church without tearing down the temple*, he said, *but the temple was being held against the city, not for it.*

He died at Gaza twenty-five years after his consecration. The basilica he had built lasted four centuries, until the Arab conquest.

> *"I would rather have built the church without tearing down the temple."* — Porphyry, in old age

## February 27

# Procopius of Decapolis

*Iconodule confessor, d. c. 750*

He was a Byzantine monk of the Decapolis — the cluster of Greek-speaking cities east of Galilee that still kept their Hellenistic names six centuries after Pompey had organized them — when the iconoclast emperor Leo III the Isaurian, in 726, issued his edict against the veneration of holy images. Procopius was at that moment perhaps fifty, a settled monk in a settled monastery; he had no public role; the edict reached him as it reached every monastic community of the empire.

He refused it. The eastern monasteries, almost to a man, refused it: the icons had been part of the monastic life for as long as anyone remembered, and the case for their veneration had been made carefully by John of Damascus and others. Procopius and a fellow monk named Basil — his lifelong companion and prayer partner — were brought before the imperial commissioners. They did not argue. They stated their conviction. They were beaten. They were imprisoned. The chroniclers do not specify how long, only that the imprisonment lasted into the next reign.

When Leo III died in 741 and his son Constantine V, who would prove an even harsher iconoclast, took the throne, the emperor's first act in matters of religion was to release some of his father's prisoners. Procopius and Basil were among those let out. They returned to their monastery and lived another decade in quiet observance. Procopius died around 750. Basil, who had shared his cell and his suffering, died a few years later.

He had not written a treatise. He had not preached. He had only refused, when asked, to stop praying before the face of Christ.

The Church remembered him for the refusal. The Greek hymns to him still call him *the one who did not yield in the small matter so that he might be ready in the great.*

> *"I have prayed before the face of Christ for thirty years; I will not stop because the emperor asks me to."* — Procopius, before the commissioners

## February 28

# Proterius of Alexandria

*Patriarch and martyr, d. 457*

He was a priest of the Alexandrian Church, the senior presbyter under Dioscorus, when the Council of Chalcedon in 451 deposed Dioscorus for refusing the Chalcedonian definition of Christ as one Person in two natures. Alexandria had been, for generations, the most theologically distinctive see in the East — Cyril's see, Athanasius's see — and the Egyptian clergy and laity were overwhelmingly committed to a single-nature Christology of the Lord. They had not wanted Chalcedon. They had not wanted Dioscorus deposed. The imperial party put Proterius in his place over the protests of the city.

He governed for six years under siege. The Monophysite faction — the majority — refused him communion, refused to recognize his authority, rioted when he attempted to install priests in the city's churches. He held the see by imperial military protection. He was, by all reports, a learned and humble man trying to do an impossible job; he insisted on the Chalcedonian definition because he believed it true, and he refused to leave the see because he believed it his.

When the emperor Marcian — who had backed him — died in January 457, the Alexandrian Monophysites took the death as the moment to act. On Maundy Thursday, during the evening Eucharist in the cathedral, a mob broke through the doors and forced their way to the sanctuary. They killed Proterius at the altar. They dragged his body through the streets of Alexandria. They burned what remained in the public square.

The Egyptian Church, after his death, fell completely out of communion with Constantinople and Rome and remained so. The split that began with the rioting against Chalcedon has not entirely healed in our day.

Proterius did not choose his end; he chose the doctrine, and the end came with the choosing. He is the rare patriarch whom both the Chalcedonian and the non-Chalcedonian sides remember — one as a martyr, the other as a memory still painful to touch.

> *"I have not held this office for the office's sake."* — Proterius, in his final Easter homily

## February 29

# Oswald of Worcester

*Bishop, Benedictine reformer, c. 925–992*

He was an Anglo-Dane, born of a Danish father who had settled in England in the generation after Alfred's wars and an English mother of noble blood. His uncle Oda was archbishop of Canterbury and recognized in the boy what the boy did not yet see in himself; he sent Oswald, when he was old enough, to the great Benedictine reform house of Fleury on the Loire to learn the new continental observance. Oswald came back ten years later with the Fleury Rule in his head and a slight French accent he never lost.

He was made bishop of Worcester in 961 and, eleven years later, archbishop of York as well, holding both sees with papal permission because the English Church did not yet have enough reformed bishops to spare. Together with Dunstan of Canterbury and Ethelwold of Winchester, Oswald drove the monastic reform that remade tenth-century English Christianity — replacing the lax secular canons of the cathedral chapters with Benedictine monks, founding new abbeys, copying manuscripts, restoring the daily office in houses where it had not been kept for fifty years. He founded or rebuilt Westbury, Ramsey, Pershore, Worcester, Winchcombe. He served as bishop for thirty-one years.

He kept a private practice that the people of Worcester knew about. Every week — Maundy Thursday or no — he would have twelve of the city's poor men brought to the cathedral cloister, and he would wash their feet on his knees. He kept it up to extreme old age, when his hands could barely hold the basin. He died doing it. On the morning of February 29, 992, he had washed the feet of eleven men. He bent to begin the twelfth and did not rise. The brothers who carried him to his bed said he was already gone.

> *"He died at the work he had always done."* — Byrhtferth, *Life of Oswald*

## March 1

# David of Wales

*Bishop, patron of Wales, c. 500–589*

He was born on a cliff above the Pembrokeshire sea in a storm, the son of a Cardiganshire prince named Sandde and a girl named Non. She kept the child and raised him in her own small cell. He grew up among monks. When he was old enough to take a rule of his own, he founded a monastery at Mynyw on the westernmost peninsula of Wales — the place that would later carry his name — and gave it a rule so severe that other Welsh houses called it *the Egypt rule*.

The monks of David pulled their own plows. They drank no beer. They drank only water — David himself was nicknamed *Aquaticus*, the Waterman. They prayed standing in the cold sea up to the chest. They ate one meal a day of bread, salt vegetables, and small fish from the strand. They worked in absolute silence. The point of the regime was not the suffering but the absence: David wanted his monks to have nothing of their own to think about, so that they had room for God.

He preached at the Synod of Brefi against the Pelagians and was so soft-spoken that the men at the back of the crowd could not hear him; the ground beneath his feet, a chronicler says, rose into a small hill so that his voice would carry. He outlived most of his disciples and died at well past eighty.

His last sermon — preserved in the Welsh of the *Buchedd Dewi* — was three sentences: *Be joyful. Keep your faith. Do the little things you have heard and seen me do.* The Welsh have lived on those three sentences ever since.

> *"Do the little things you have heard and seen me do."* — David, in his last sermon

## March 2

# Chad of Mercia

*Bishop, c. 634–672*

He was a Northumbrian, the youngest of four brothers, all of whom became priests; the two best-known were Chad and his elder Cedd. They had been trained at Lindisfarne under Aidan, the Irish missionary bishop, and they took from Aidan the same lifelong habit: that a bishop walks, does not ride, and is poor.

Chad was for a few months bishop of York under disputed circumstances — the see had been awarded to him during the absence of Wilfrid, who returned to find his place taken. When Theodore of Tarsus arrived as archbishop of Canterbury and ruled the consecration irregular, Chad simply stepped down without protest. *I never thought myself worthy of it*, he said. *I undertook it only out of obedience.* Theodore was so moved by the answer that within a year he sent Chad north again, properly consecrated this time, as bishop of the Mercians.

He governed Mercia from a small wooden cell at Lichfield. He walked. Theodore eventually ordered him to ride — the diocese was vast and the bishop was no longer young — and Chad would only ride at the archbishop's express command, dismounting whenever he reached a settled place.

He died after three years. Bede preserves what he said on his deathbed. He had heard, he told the brothers, the music of his welcome; his elder brother Cedd was there waiting for him. *I am ready to go*, he said. *Let the singing be cheerful.*

> *"Let the singing be cheerful."* — Chad, on his deathbed

## March 3

# Cunegunde of Luxembourg

*Holy Roman Empress, c. 975–1040*

She was a daughter of Siegfried of Luxembourg and a wife of Henry II from her teens. The marriage was political and consequential; Cunegunde brought the imperial line a link to the western Frankish nobility, and her husband would become the last emperor of the Ottonian house. By every chronicler's account the marriage was also celibate — by the mutual choice of both spouses, who had agreed at the betrothal to live as brother and sister. The agreement held for thirty years.

She was crowned empress in 1014, at the same Mass at which Henry was crowned emperor by Benedict VIII. She used the imperial dignity for founding houses: the convents at Kaufungen and Kassel, the monastic chapter at Bamberg her husband was endowing as a new bishopric, hospitals in Saxony and Bavaria. She received the diplomatic correspondence of half of Europe in her own hand. When a court rumor accused her of adultery, she submitted to the ordeal of red-hot ploughshares — the same ordeal that Edward the Confessor's mother Emma would face a generation later — and walked unburned. Henry, who had not believed the rumor and had not asked for the ordeal, wept.

When he died in 1024, Cunegunde transferred the imperial regalia to his successor Conrad II, withdrew to the convent at Kaufungen she had founded, and took the habit. She lived sixteen years as a sister there. The other nuns did not know, for a long time, that the new oblate had ever been the empress.

> *"I came here to learn humility, not to teach it."* — Cunegunde, at Kaufungen

## March 4

# Casimir of Poland

*Prince, 1458–1484*

He was the second son of King Casimir IV of Poland and a careful, melancholy young man from his early youth. The court tutors found him an apt pupil; the king's marshals found him an unwilling soldier; the courtiers found him a poor courtier and a strange friend. He preferred the cathedral to the council chamber. He slept on the floor beside his bed when no one was watching. He gave away the contents of his purse so often that the chamberlain stopped replenishing it.

His father sent him at fifteen to invade Hungary at the head of an army of twelve thousand men: the Polish-Lithuanian succession claim against Matthias Corvinus, the Hungarian king, had become a question of arms. Casimir, on the road, came to the judgment that the war was unjust and that the dispute should be settled at the negotiating table. He halted the army at the frontier and refused to cross. His father was furious and banished him to a country castle for a year. Casimir went without protest.

He was twenty-five when consumption took him. The chronicles say that as the illness deepened he asked daily for the singing of the Latin hymn *Omni die dic Mariae*, the daily hymn to the Virgin, which some traditions credit to him as composer; he sang it himself until he could no longer sing. He died at dawn on March 4, 1484, at Hrodna in Lithuania. They buried him at Vilnius, where his tomb has been the central pilgrimage shrine of the Lithuanian Catholic Church ever since.

He had been asked, once, why he never married. *I will marry one*, he had told his confessor, *who will not be widowed when I die.*

> *"I will marry one who will not be widowed when I die."* — Casimir, on his celibacy

## March 5

# Gerasimus of the Jordan

*Abbot, fifth century*

He was a Lycian, born on the southern coast of Asia Minor, and he came east as a young man to the Egyptian desert and then to the wilderness east of the Jordan, near Jericho, where the river opens out into the long dry valley before the Dead Sea. The hermit caves of that country were filling up by the 450s with monks who had come from every part of the empire; Gerasimus, sober and gentle, was made superior of a great *laura* — a settlement of separate cells around a common church — and governed it for the last decade of his life.

He had, like many monks of his generation, leaned briefly toward the Monophysite position; the elderly Euthymius the Great, his neighbor in the desert, talked him out of it. He spent the rest of his life — as if making up for the lapse — defending the Chalcedonian definition of Christ as one Person in two natures.

The story by which he is remembered involves a lion. He met it on the road, lame from a thorn in its paw; he drew the thorn out and bound the wound, and the lion, instead of leaving, followed him home and took up residence at the gates of the laura. The community fed it. The lion learned to carry water-jars on its back. When one day a passing trader stole the laura's donkey, the lion was blamed and exiled in disgrace; months later the donkey was found and the lion's name was cleared. When Gerasimus died, the lion came to the grave, the chronicler John Moschus says, and would not leave, and would not eat, and died there.

It is not the story they told about him in his own life. It is the story they began to tell once they had buried him. *He was the kind of man*, they were saying, *whom even animals could read.*

> *"He drew the thorn out, and the lion would not leave him."* — John Moschus, *Spiritual Meadow* 107

## March 6

# Colette of Corbie

*Poor Clare reformer, 1381–1447*

She was an only child, born late to elderly parents at Corbie in Picardy and named Nicolette; she shortened it herself, in her late teens, when both parents died within a year of each other and she had no further reason to be called by their pet name. She tried the Benedictine novitiate. She tried the Urbanist branch of the Franciscans. Neither held her. She had herself walled up as an anchoress against the wall of the parish church at Corbie, in a small cell, with a single grilled window for confession and communion, intending to die there.

She did not die there. She had a vision — Francis and Clare standing in the cell with her — which told her, plainly, that the Poor Clares had drifted from the strict observance of their first foundresses and that she was being sent to put them back. She left the cell. She walked to Nice and presented herself to the antipope Benedict XIII, then recognized in France, to ask for his commission. He gave it.

For the next thirty-eight years she walked across France and Flanders reforming Poor Clare convents one at a time. She reformed seventeen houses in all. The Colettines, as her reform was called, ate no meat, drank no wine, slept on boards, kept silence outside the office, and refused property. She wrote two abbesses a week, in her own hand, and a constant stream of letters of spiritual direction; six hundred have survived. She received the stigmata in middle age; she hid them.

She died at the convent at Ghent at sixty-six. She had been an anchoress who had been told to come out. The reform she led has outlasted her by six centuries.

> *"Begin with yourself; the others will follow."* — Colette, in a letter

## March 7

# Thomas Aquinas

*Dominican doctor, 1225–1274*

He was the youngest son of the Count of Aquino, born at the family castle of Roccasecca between Naples and Rome, and his family had a plan for him from the cradle: he would be a Benedictine abbot of Monte Cassino, then a bishop, then a cardinal. He was fifteen when he met the Dominicans at Naples and refused the plan. His brothers kidnapped him on the road north and locked him for fifteen months in a tower at Roccasecca, hoping to break his vocation. They sent a prostitute into his cell one evening; the chroniclers say he chased her out with a burning log from the fire and traced a cross on the door with the soot.

He went at last to Cologne under Albert the Great, who took one look at the silent enormous Italian and said: *we call him the dumb ox, but the bellowing of this ox will be heard throughout the world.* He taught at Paris, at Naples, at Orvieto, at Rome. He wrote — by dictation, often to several secretaries at once, in different languages — more theology than most men have read. The *Summa Theologica* was the central work; the *Summa contra Gentiles*, the commentaries on Aristotle, the disputed questions, the hymns for the new feast of Corpus Christi, are next to it.

On the feast of Saint Nicholas in 1273, at Mass in Naples, something happened. He stopped writing. He told his secretary Reginald, who pressed him: *I cannot. Compared to what I have seen, all that I have written seems like straw.* He died three months later, on the road to the Council of Lyons, at the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova on March 7. He had been hearing the Song of Songs read aloud, slowly, since dawn.

> *"I receive thee, the ransom of my soul. For love of thee I have studied and watched, toiled and preached and taught."* — Aquinas, receiving the viaticum

## March 8

# Felix of Dunwich

*Apostle of East Anglia, d. 647*

He was a Burgundian by birth, a priest already when he met Sigebert of East Anglia in Gaul. Sigebert was at that moment a king with no kingdom — exiled by his half-brother, baptized in his exile by Frankish bishops, waiting for the call home. The call came in 631. Sigebert recovered his throne, and Felix went with him.

Archbishop Honorius of Canterbury formally consecrated Felix bishop and sent him to the East Anglian kingdom, where Christianity had reached the court only and not yet the country. Felix set up his see at Dunwich, on the Suffolk coast — then a sizeable trading port, long since lost to the North Sea — and built churches. He travelled. He founded a school on the model of the Frankish episcopal schools he had grown up in, the first English school to teach Latin grammar, and Bede records that the kingdom was almost entirely Christian within the seventeen years of Felix's episcopate. Sigebert himself eventually resigned the crown to become a monk at the monastery Felix had helped him establish at Beodricsworth — modern Bury St Edmunds.

Bede plays with the name. *Felix*, he writes — *as his name signified, he brought happiness to that province.* East Anglia was Christian and literate when Felix died in 647; both gifts came in through the same door.

> *"His name signified what he was: he brought happiness to that province."* — Bede, *Ecclesiastical History* II.15

## March 9

# Frances of Rome

*Roman matron, mystic, 1384–1440*

She was born into a wealthy Roman family at the beginning of the Great Schism and married off at twelve to Lorenzo Ponziani, son of another wealthy Roman family, who turned out to be the best gift God had given her — a husband who let her live her vocation. Frances had wanted the cloister; her parents had refused. She made her peace with the marriage by deciding that whatever her husband would let her do for the poor of Rome, she would do.

She did a great deal. Her sister-in-law Vannozza became her closest friend; the two of them visited the sick together, distributed the family's grain in famine years, walked the streets at night to bring food to the destitute, and emptied the linen presses to clothe them. When Lorenzo, suspecting nothing of the inventory, asked one winter where his winter cloaks had gone, Frances answered without looking up: *they are warmer on the poor.* Lorenzo, who loved her, said no more.

She bore six children; two died in plague. She survived the sack of Rome by the Neapolitan condottieri; the family fled, the house was looted, Lorenzo went into exile, and Frances remained in the ruined household to run it for the children. When at last he returned, broken in health, she nursed him for the last years of his life. She kept seeing — and her confessor finally believed her, after years of testing — her guardian angel: a small boy in white, walking just before her with a lamp, lighting her way through the streets of Rome at night.

After Lorenzo's death she joined the community of Oblates she had founded at Tor de' Specchi. She died at fifty-six, in the house of her son, having spent half her life leaving God at the altar to find him in the kitchen.

> *"It is most laudable in a married woman to be devout, but she must never forget that she is a housewife. Sometimes she must leave God at the altar to find him in her housekeeping."* — Frances of Rome

## March 10

# The Forty Martyrs of Sebaste

*Roman soldiers, c. 320*

They were a unit of the Twelfth Legion stationed at Sebaste in Armenia, far up the Black Sea coast — forty men, mostly Cappadocian, all Christian. When the emperor Licinius issued a fresh edict in 320 commanding all soldiers to sacrifice publicly to the Roman gods, the unit refused as one. The commanding officer, who knew them all by name, gave them every opportunity to relent. They did not relent.

He chose his punishment for its theatre. The lake outside Sebaste froze hard in March; he had the forty men stripped naked and made to stand on the ice overnight. On the bank he set up a row of heated baths, lit and steaming. Any man who walked off the ice to a bath would be spared. The soldiers, the chroniclers say, prayed aloud through the long evening. Toward midnight one of them broke, ran for a bath, and died in it; the cold had gone too deep.

A pagan guardsman at the post that night had been watching. As the runaway died, he saw — in vision, the chroniclers insist — thirty-nine crowns descending from the sky on the heads of the men still standing on the ice, and a fortieth crown waiting unworn. He stripped off his own armor, professed Christ, and walked out onto the ice in the dead man's place. The forty died together at first light.

Their bodies were burned and their ashes scattered to keep their relics from the Christians who were already pressing for them; the Christians collected the ashes anyway. By the end of the century the brothers Basil and Gregory of Nyssa were preaching on them; Ephrem the Syrian preached on them; Gaudentius of Brescia preached on them. They are still the standard image, in Eastern iconography, of friends who chose to die rather than have one of them missing.

> *"We are forty in number; let no one separate us."* — the soldiers, on the ice

## March 11

# Eulogius of Córdoba

*Priest and martyr, c. 800–859*

He was a Mozarab — a Spanish Christian under Muslim rule — born into one of the old Cordobese families that had kept the faith through more than a century of caliphate. His father was a senator of the city; his brothers were merchants; he himself trained for the priesthood under the abbot Speraindeo and the deacon Alvaro, who would later be his biographer. He read Latin and Arabic both. He preached, taught at the cathedral school, and travelled briefly into Christian Navarre, where he brought back manuscripts of Augustine, Virgil, and Juvenal that the Cordoba Christians had not had access to.

In the 850s a movement began in Córdoba that no one quite expected. Christians, some of them very young, walked into the caliph's court or the great mosque and proclaimed Christ aloud and Muhammad falsely. The penalty under Islamic law was death; they knew it; that was the point. They wanted to declare the gospel publicly in a city that had silenced it. The caliph, embarrassed and angry, executed them. The bishops of Córdoba, who wanted civic peace, condemned the movement.

Eulogius took the other side. He wrote two books — the *Memoriale Sanctorum* and the *Apologeticus Martyrum* — defending the men and women who were dying, recording their names, preserving their last words. He was elected archbishop of Toledo but could not reach his see; the Muslim authorities would not let him travel. In 859 he was sheltering Leocritia, a young Muslim woman who had been baptized Christian and fled her family. They were found together. He was beheaded on March 11. She was beheaded four days later.

He had written, for the martyrs, the only kind of book that mattered: their names.

> *"The crown of martyrdom is not granted to all; but to those who carry the cross every day, it is promised."* — Eulogius, *Memoriale Sanctorum*

## March 12

# Gregory the Great

*Pope, doctor, 540–604*

He was the son of a senator and the great-grandson of a pope, born into the kind of Roman family that, in the sixth century, was old enough to have memories no one else had. By thirty-three he was prefect of the city — the senior civil magistrate of Rome — and in the next year of his life he resigned the office, sold the family estates, founded seven monasteries from the proceeds, kept one of them as his own, and put on a monk's habit. He had wanted, his biographer says, to be left alone with God for the rest of his life. The Church would not allow it.

He was sent as papal envoy to Constantinople. He was made deacon. He was elected, at fifty, to a papacy he tried hard to refuse. Rome at his accession was visibly dying: the Tiber had flooded, the plague was killing thousands, the Lombard armies were at the walls, the imperial bureaucracy in Ravenna no longer answered letters. Gregory took the city in hand. He organized the grain dole. He led the penitential procession across the city during the plague, and saw — or the Romans saw, watching him — an angel above the Castel Sant'Angelo sheathing a sword. He negotiated with the Lombards. He invented the title *servus servorum Dei*, servant of the servants of God, to describe what he thought a pope was.

He sent a band of monks from his own monastery on the Caelian Hill, under one named Augustine, to evangelize the English. He wrote the *Pastoral Rule*, which the medieval Church would use to teach bishops for a thousand years; he wrote the *Moralia in Job* and the *Dialogues* on the saints of Italy; he wrote eight hundred and fifty letters that survive. He died at sixty-four, in the same monk's habit he had put on at thirty-four.

> *"Servant of the servants of God."* — the title Gregory chose for himself

## March 13

# Leander of Seville

*Archbishop, c. 534–600*

He was the eldest of a remarkable family: a Hispano-Roman household at Cartagena that produced, in one generation, four canonized children — Leander, his younger brother Isidore who would succeed him, Fulgentius who would become bishop of Astigi, and a sister Florentina who became an abbess. The family had fled Byzantine reconquest and Visigothic pressure to Seville while the children were young. All four ended up serving the Spanish Church.

Leander was made archbishop of Seville in the 580s. His great work was the reconversion of the Visigothic monarchy. The Goths had been Arian for two centuries; the Catholic–Arian split in Spain mapped exactly onto the Goth–Roman split, and the kingdom was effectively two churches. Leander cultivated, patiently, the friendship of the Visigothic prince Hermenegild, who converted; when Hermenegild was killed by his father for the conversion, Leander did not give up. He worked next on Hermenegild's brother Reccared, who succeeded to the throne. Reccared converted in 587. At the Third Council of Toledo in 589, with Leander preaching the opening sermon, the entire Visigothic nation followed.

Leander had spent four years in Constantinople in the 580s on diplomatic business and had become close friends with another foreigner there, a Roman deacon named Gregory who would later be pope. Gregory wrote to him for decades; Leander's request was the reason Gregory wrote the *Moralia in Job*. When Gregory needed to send missionaries to England, Leander was one of the first he consulted.

He wrote his sister Florentina a treatise on monastic life for women — *De institutione virginum* — which would shape Spanish convents for centuries. He died around the year 600 at Seville, leaving the see to Isidore.

> *"Let no one fear to convert; for what God has prepared, no royal hand can prevent."* — Leander, sermon at the Third Council of Toledo

## March 14

# Matilda of Saxony

*Queen, c. 895–968*

She was raised at the Saxon abbey of Herford, where her grandmother had placed her, and where she learned to read and to keep her hours and to expect a quiet life. The arrangement was disrupted when Henry the Fowler — Duke of Saxony, soon to be king of East Francia — saw her and asked for her hand. She was sixteen. The marriage was happy. She bore five children, the eldest of whom would become Otto I, the second of whom would be the duke of Bavaria, the third of whom — Bruno — would be archbishop of Cologne.

She gave away too much. Henry tolerated her almsgiving with affection; her sons, after Henry's death in 936, did not. Otto and Henry decided their mother had emptied the royal treasury for the poor and accused her of mismanagement. They stripped her of her dower lands and confined her, for a time, in a country house. She did not protest. She wept in private and gave the courtiers no satisfaction. Within a year the brothers had reconciled with her, weeping themselves; their wives had refused to entertain them so long as their mother was disgraced.

She lived another thirty-two years as queen dowager. She founded the abbeys at Quedlinburg, Nordhausen, and Pöhlde — Quedlinburg, where she retired and where her granddaughter Mathilde would later be abbess, is the one that survived the centuries. She died there at seventy-three, having outlived two of her five children, three of her sisters, and most of the courtiers who had known her as a young queen.

She had governed without title, by being unmovable. Her household had been the Saxon court, the Saxon poor, and the Saxon convents in equal share.

> *"What I have given is not lost; only what I have kept is in danger."* — Matilda, on her almsgiving

## March 15

# Zachary, Pope

*Bishop of Rome, c. 679–752*

He was a Greek — born in Calabria, of Greek-speaking parents, the last of a long line of Greek and Syrian popes who had governed Rome through the seventh and early eighth centuries. He was a deacon under his predecessor Gregory III when he was elected in 741. He inherited a papacy that had to face two great northern powers at once: the Lombards under King Liutprand, who had been pressing on Rome for decades, and the Franks under the rising house of Charles Martel, who had become the actual rulers of Francia though the Merovingian kings still wore the crown.

Zachary made his peace with the Lombards by personal diplomacy. He travelled north to meet Liutprand and his successor Ratchis face to face — an extraordinary thing for a pope to do in that age — and persuaded both to return captured territory and stop raiding the Duchy of Rome. Ratchis, after the meetings, abdicated his throne and entered the monastery of Monte Cassino. Zachary, who could be hard, could also be the kind of man before whom kings reconsidered their lives.

The other intervention was decisive. Pippin the Short, the Frankish mayor of the palace, wrote to Zachary in 751 asking whether it was right that the man who held the power should not have the title. Zachary's reply was that he should. Pippin deposed the last Merovingian, Childeric III, and was crowned king. The Carolingian dynasty began with that papal letter. Half a century later it would crown Pippin's son Charlemagne, and Zachary's quiet sentence would echo through the Western political imagination for a thousand years.

He died at seventy-three, having ruled the Roman Church for eleven years. He had translated Gregory the Great's *Dialogues* into Greek as a private undertaking. He died at the table where he had been writing.

> *"It is better that he be called king who exercises power than he who has no power."* — Zachary to Pippin the Short, 751

## March 16

# Heribert of Cologne

*Archbishop, 970–1021*

He was the son of Count Hugo of Worms and a Lotharingian noblewoman, and he was given the standard education of the imperial nobility: cathedral school at Worms, advanced studies at the abbey of Gorze, an early post as chancellor to the German chancery. He was at court when the young Emperor Otto III, having just turned sixteen, made him imperial chancellor for Italy and Germany both. Heribert was twenty-eight. For the next decade he was the most powerful clerical bureaucrat in the empire, and Otto's closest friend.

In 999 the see of Cologne fell vacant. Otto wanted Heribert there. Heribert resisted; the diocese was too political, the see too rich. Otto insisted. Heribert went, but on his own terms: he ate at the monastic table at his cathedral, gave away the rents he could spare, slept on a hairshirt under his episcopal vestments, and visited every parish of his diocese on foot in his first year. He continued the chancellor's work alongside the archbishopric. When Otto died suddenly at Paterno in 1002 at twenty-one — perhaps poisoned, more likely of malaria — Heribert was the man at the bedside.

He carried the imperial insignia back across the Alps and gave them honorably to Otto's successor Henry II, with whom he would have a more difficult working relationship. They quarreled, reconciled, quarreled again, reconciled at last. Heribert outlived Henry's first calm decade. He founded the monastery at Deutz on the right bank of the Rhine, opposite Cologne, and was buried there.

He had ruled the see for twenty-two years and the chancery for almost thirty. What he taught his contemporaries was that an archbishop could also be a monk, and that an imperial chancellor could also be a friend.

> *"Lord, you have given me this office twice over. Let me serve you in both."* — Heribert, on his consecration

## March 17

# Patrick of Ireland

*Apostle of Ireland, c. 387–461*

He was the son of a Romano-British deacon, raised in a respectable Christian household somewhere along the western coast of Britain, perhaps near modern Carlisle. He was sixteen when Irish raiders crossed the sea, attacked the estate, and took him with the other young captives. They sold him in Ireland to a local chieftain. For six years he watched sheep on the Slemish hills in Antrim, in any weather, half-fed and friendless, learning Irish and praying — *up to a hundred prayers a day*, he writes, *and almost as many at night.*

A voice in a dream told him a ship was waiting. He walked nearly two hundred miles to the south coast, talked his way onto a vessel, and eventually reached home. He was twenty-two. He could have ended the story there. He went instead to Gaul to study for the priesthood, was eventually consecrated bishop, and in 432 — over the protest of friends who pointed out that he would be returning to the country that had enslaved him — he sailed back to Ireland.

He spent thirty years there. He preached and baptized in every kingdom of the island, ordained Irish priests, founded sees, suffered theft and the threat of murder more than once, and wrote — for the bishops of Britain who had questioned his fitness — the small autobiographical *Confessio* that is his great gift to us. It is the only voice we have from the fifth century in the British Isles. In it he tries to explain why he went back. *I was carried here as a slave*, he says, *and the Lord has made me a slave for him.*

He is the first person in history we know about whose central life act was to return, of his own will, to a people who had wronged him, in order to give them God.

> *"I owe it to God's grace that so many people through me should be reborn for him."* — Patrick, *Confessio* 38

## March 18

# Cyril of Jerusalem

*Bishop, doctor, c. 315–386*

He was born in the city he would later govern, in the years just before Constantine's mother built the church of the Holy Sepulchre over what the Christians believed was the empty tomb. Cyril grew up watching pilgrims arrive at the new shrine, learning to read in Greek and Aramaic both, and entered the clergy of Jerusalem in his twenties. He was made bishop around 350.

His great work was teaching. He preached to the catechumens — the adult candidates for baptism — in the great church his predecessors had built, and his lectures were taken down in shorthand by those listening. The twenty-three lectures that survive, the *Catechetical Lectures*, are the most complete record we have of how the fourth-century Church taught its converts: the creed line by line, the meaning of baptism, the structure of the Mass, the discipline of the Christian life. They are the catechism of Jerusalem at the moment when Jerusalem first had pilgrims again.

His episcopate was not quiet. The Arian controversy was raging; Cyril, who held the Nicene faith but was suspicious of certain Nicene phrasings, was deposed and exiled three times — by Acacius of Caesarea, by Constantius, by Valens — and reinstated three times. He lost sixteen of his thirty-six years as bishop to exile. He attended the Council of Constantinople in 381, the council that confirmed Nicaea and added the clauses on the Holy Spirit, and signed its decrees with what must have been a tired hand.

He died at Jerusalem at seventy. His lectures had gone everywhere by then; Jerome cited them, Egeria the pilgrim cited them, the next century's catechumens were still being taught with his book in hand.

> *"You have been called a Christian. Be aware of the name you bear."* — Cyril, *Catechetical Lectures* I

## March 19

# Joseph, Husband of Mary

*Carpenter of Nazareth, first century*

He was a carpenter in a small town in lower Galilee, of the house of David, descended from kings whose names had long been irrelevant to the men he hired by the day. He was probably young; the apocryphal traditions that would later make him an old widower are inventions of a later church embarrassed by the idea of Mary's marriage. He was engaged to a girl named Mary, also young. He learned that she was pregnant.

The Gospel of Matthew gives us his thoughts. He was a just man. He did not want to expose her to the law — under which she could be stoned — and he resolved to dismiss her quietly. In the dream that ended that resolve, an angel told him whose child she carried. He took her into his home. He named the boy as the angel had instructed. He took the family to Egypt to escape Herod, settled them back in Nazareth when Herod was dead, and disappears from the gospel narrative after the finding in the temple, when Jesus is twelve. He was the boy's first teacher in the trade — every healing miracle in the gospels uses verbs Jesus must have learned from him: *I will, be made whole; arise; come forth.* The carpenter teaches each of these to an apprentice.

He says nothing in the gospels. Not a single word is attributed to him. What he does is everything: he listens, he obeys, he protects, he provides, he is silent at the right moments and decisive at the right moments. He gives us the model of the man who is not himself the protagonist of the story, and who serves the story anyway.

He is the patron of fathers and of the dying. He died, the tradition holds, before the public ministry; Jesus was probably with him at the end.

> *"He took her as his wife, but knew her not until she had borne a son."* — Matthew 1:24–25

## March 20

# Cuthbert of Lindisfarne

*Bishop, hermit, c. 634–687*

He was a Northumbrian shepherd boy, watching the hills above the river Lauder, when at the age of about fifteen he saw — in vision, on the night that Aidan was dying at Bamburgh some fifty miles to the east — a soul carried up into the sky surrounded by light. The next morning he learned of Aidan's death. He walked the next day to the abbey of Melrose and asked to be received as a novice.

He was a Melrose monk for fifteen years, then prior. He travelled the countryside on horseback when the abbey could spare him a horse and on foot when it could not, preaching in remote villages where Christianity was only nominally present. Bede, who knew people who knew him, records that he would sometimes preach all day, stand vigil all night, and start again the next morning. He could not refuse anyone who came for confession. He could not stop blessing.

After the Synod of Whitby in 664, when the English Church accepted the Roman observance over the Irish, Cuthbert went as prior to Lindisfarne to model the change without bitterness. After ten years there he asked permission to live as a hermit on Inner Farne, the small island half a mile offshore where the seabirds were the only neighbors. He built a small stone cell with a high wall — he wished to see only sky — and lived there alone for nine years.

The diocese pulled him out. He was consecrated bishop of Lindisfarne against his will in 685. He served two years, returned to Farne to die, and was buried at Lindisfarne. When the monks opened the coffin eleven years later his body was, in Bede's phrase, *as one sleeping*. The Vikings would come for the bones a century later. The monks carried him in a portable coffin for two hundred years across northern England, never quite letting him rest, until they finally laid him at Durham.

> *"Have peace and divine charity always among you."* — Cuthbert, his last words to the brothers

## March 21

# Benedict of Nursia

*Father of Western monasticism, c. 480–547*

He was a teenager from Nursia in Umbria, sent to Rome to study by his parents, when he gave up on the city. The Rome of 500 was Christian in name and dissolute in fact; the schoolfellows around Benedict struck him, his biographer Gregory the Great says, as men hurrying toward a cliff they could not see. He left the schools without finishing, took an old servant with him, and walked east into the Sabine hills.

He stopped at a cave above the river Anio at a place called Subiaco. A monk from a nearby monastery, Romanus, helped him survive the first months by lowering bread to the cave on a rope. Benedict stayed three years. Local shepherds came to wonder at him; disciples began to gather. He emerged at last, ordered them into twelve small monasteries of twelve monks each, and governed them collectively from his own cell. A jealous local priest tried to poison him; Benedict gave the bread to a raven, kept moving, and around 529 settled at last on the high crown of Monte Cassino, where there had been a temple to Apollo. He built his great monastery on the same rock.

What he wrote there has changed more lives than any book Western Christianity has produced after the New Testament. The *Rule of Saint Benedict* is seventy-three short chapters of practical wisdom — when to pray, what to eat, how to receive a stranger, what to do when a brother sleeps in. It assumes that holiness is not for heroes but for the weak, that work and prayer are not opposed, that the abbot exists for the brothers and not the brothers for the abbot. For fifteen hundred years it has taught the West how to live in a community.

He died standing at the altar of his abbey, supported by the arms of his brothers, his last prayer cut off mid-word.

> *"Listen, my son, to the precepts of the master, and incline the ear of your heart."* — *Rule of Saint Benedict*, Prologue

## March 22

# Lea of Rome

*Roman widow, d. 384*

We know about her from a single letter — Jerome's Letter 23, addressed to Marcella — and we know nothing else. The letter is two paragraphs long. Lea had died suddenly; the news had reached Jerome that morning; Marcella had asked him to write something. He wrote what came to him.

She had been a Roman widow, like Marcella herself, like Paula. After her husband's death she had taken charge of a small community of women — perhaps the same one Marcella ran, perhaps a related one — and the household she headed lived under a strict rule of fasting, vigils, and silence. *She used to be the slave of all*, Jerome writes, *she who had once had slaves of her own.* She wore rough clothes; she slept on the floor; she ate sparingly. Her food, her clothes, her time, her possessions, even her name — she gave them all away as if she had never had them.

Jerome contrasts her death with another that had reached Rome the same week. The pagan consul Vettius Praetextatus, a great public figure, had died at the height of his honors; the Roman senate was draped in mourning; orators were preparing eulogies. Jerome — never gentle — sets the two deaths against each other in two sentences. The consul, he writes, is now being tortured for his crimes. Lea is now seated at the feet of Christ.

The letter is most of what we have of her. The Bollandists kept her feast on March 22 because Jerome kept her name on March 22. She made it into the calendar of the Western Church because one of the great writers of late antiquity could not let her die obscure, and wrote her in.

> *"While she was held in low estimation by men, she was great in the sight of God."* — Jerome, *Letter 23* on Lea

## March 23

# Nicon and the 199 Sicilian Monk-Martyrs

*Roman officer turned abbot, with his community, d. c. 250*

He was born at Naples around 200 to a Christian mother and a pagan father, and grew up in the Roman military world the father had wanted for him. He went into the legion as a young man, served in the eastern provinces under the late Severans, and rose to a centurion's command of the *cohors prima Italica* in Thrace. He was perhaps thirty-five when he discovered that the new prefect was distributing the persecution edict, and that several of his own soldiers — Christians by his mother's instruction, secret in their practice — were on the arrest list.

He resigned his commission and crossed the Aegean alone, making his way into the small Greek-speaking monastic communities on Mount Ganos in Thrace, under an Egyptian abba whose name the *acta* do not preserve. He took the habit and spent a decade in monastic discipline. When the abba died around 245 the brothers elected him hegumen, and the community grew from twenty to nearly two hundred. He trained them in the desert form: the night office in their cells, the day office in choir, manual labor in the fields, two meals a week of bread and salt, the rest in silent prayer.

The Decian persecution caught up with the community in the early autumn of 250, mid-harvest. The prefect's troops surrounded the monastery at dawn and gave them an hour to come out and sacrifice. Nicon assembled them in the chapel for the eucharist — his last Mass — and afterward led them out single file to the marshy ground below the cliffs of Taormina across the strait in Sicily. They were beheaded one by one in the order of their entry into religion. Nicon was the last. One hundred and ninety-nine brothers and their abbot died on the same morning. The disciple Chaeromen, absent on a journey for supplies, came back two days later, gathered the bodies, and buried them in a single mass grave below the cliffs.

He wrote down what he had heard from the soldiers, the executioner, and two slaves who had themselves been baptized within the year. The *acta* he produced are among the few certifiably eyewitness-derived monastic martyrologies of the third-century church.

> *"Their names are written in the book of life."* — *the manuscript heading of the Ambrosian* acta

## March 24

# Catherine of Sweden

*Abbess, daughter of Bridget, 1331–1381*

She was the fourth child of Bridget of Sweden, the great Swedish mystic and prophetess, and she was raised in a household where it was assumed that piety was the family business. Bridget had borne eight children; Catherine was the daughter who would inherit her mother's mantle. She was placed at the Cistercian convent at Riseberga to be educated, and at thirteen, as her parents arranged, she was given in marriage to a German nobleman named Eggart von Kürnen who happened to be both pious and chronically ill. Catherine and Eggart agreed at the wedding to live as brother and sister. The arrangement held for seven years until Eggart's death.

In 1349 Catherine went to Rome to find her mother, who had moved there to await an audience with the pope. She found instead a city in plague, a mother she barely recognized for severity, and the rest of her own future. She stayed. For twenty-four years she travelled with Bridget — to Naples, to Cyprus, to Jerusalem, to every Italian shrine her mother's revelations sent them to — serving as the older woman's secretary, daughter, and protector. The Romans who saw them together said Catherine was as gentle as her mother was fierce.

When Bridget died at Rome in 1373, Catherine carried her body back to Sweden in a small reliquary and laid her at Vadstena, the abbey her mother had founded but had never seen completed. Catherine became abbess. For eight years she ran the new community, the first Bridgettine house, and worked through the papal courts for her mother's canonization. She lived to see the cause moving forward; she did not live to see it close. She died at Vadstena at fifty.

Bridget was canonized ten years after Catherine's death. Catherine herself was beatified by popular acclaim; the formal cause closed only in the seventeenth century. She would have minded neither delay.

> *"My mother taught me to wait."* — Catherine of Sweden, in a letter

## March 25

# Dismas, the Good Thief

*The penitent crucified beside Christ, d. c. A.D. 30*

The medieval church kept his day on the twenty-fifth of March because it believed Christ had been crucified on the same calendar day on which he had been conceived — that the mystery of the Incarnation closed where it had opened, on the spring equinox. Augustine, Bede, and the whole medieval *computus* preserve the reckoning. Dismas, by inheriting Good Friday's traditional date, became the saint of the equinox itself. The Bollandists print the *acta* under the formula *DE SANCTO LATRONE CRUCIFIXO CUM CHRISTO HIEROSOLYMIS* — *the holy thief crucified with Christ at Jerusalem* — and add the proper-name *Dismas* the *Gospel of Nicodemus* gave him.

What we have of him in the canonical text is fifty-five Greek words. Luke 23 preserves the scene at Calvary as none of the other gospels do. The two thieves — *kakourgoi*, *evildoers* — are crucified on either side of Jesus. One of them, joining the mockery, calls out: *If thou be the Christ, save thyself and us.* The other rebukes him: *Dost not thou fear God, seeing thou art in the same condemnation? And we indeed justly, for we receive the due reward of our deeds: but this man hath done nothing amiss.* He turns his head, with what strength he has left, toward the man on the central cross. *Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom.* The answer comes back at once: *Verily I say unto thee, today shalt thou be with me in paradise.*

The legend that grew around him gave him a back-story: a young thief of the Galilee road who had once spared the Holy Family on their flight into Egypt, who had recognized in the infant something he could not place. The Bollandists print the legend without endorsing it, and observe that the gospel reality — a condemned criminal who in his last hour saw what most of the priests of Israel had not — is more striking than any back-story.

He is the patron of the dying, of repentant criminals, of funeral directors. Every priest hearing the deathbed confession of a man who has lived badly is, in the long tradition, hearing it through Dismas's words. *Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom.*

> *"Today shalt thou be with me in paradise."* — Christ to Dismas, *Luke* 23:43

## March 26

# Ludger of Münster

*Apostle of Saxony, c. 742–809*

He was a Frisian, born near Utrecht into a family that had become Christian only a generation earlier through the preaching of Willibrord. He went as a boy to the cathedral school at Utrecht under Gregory, Boniface's old companion, and later crossed the Channel to York to study with Alcuin. He came back to the continent with the Carolingian world's best Latin education and a settled vocation to mission. The Frisians of the coast — his own people, half-pagan still, suspicious of the German Church — were his first field.

He preached among them for a decade. When the Saxon revolts of the 780s drove him out and into Italy, he stayed three years at Monte Cassino, learning the Benedictine rhythm; when Charlemagne broke the revolts by force, Ludger was the man the emperor wanted back. He returned not as a chaplain to a conquering army but as the man who would clean up the conquest. Charlemagne had baptized Saxons at sword-point and was now imposing tithes on the new converts to fund the new churches. Ludger refused to enforce the tithes. The Saxons, he wrote to the emperor, would not love the faith of those who collected from them; they should be taught first, taxed never. The emperor, to his credit, listened to him.

He founded the monastery at Werden in 799 and the see at Münster in 805. He preached in Saxon when his preachers could not yet preach in it. He visited his villages on foot. He buried his own monks and his Saxon godchildren by his own hand. He died early one morning in 809, having celebrated Mass at Coesfeld in the evening and at Billerbeck at dawn.

The faith he planted in Saxony stayed planted, partly because the Saxons could see that the planter loved them more than he wanted their money.

> *"Teach them first, take from them never."* — Ludger, in a letter to Charlemagne

## March 27

# Rupert of Salzburg

*Bishop, Apostle of Bavaria, c. 660–718*

He was a Frankish nobleman, possibly of Merovingian descent, who served as bishop of Worms in his thirties — a quiet, learned man with a face the chroniclers describe as habitually amused. Around the year 696 Duke Theodo of Bavaria, who had begun to convert his people and needed a man to organize the work, sent for Rupert. The duke wanted churches, priests, schools. Rupert came with a dozen monks.

He travelled the duchy. He preached as he went. He came at last to a deserted Roman town on the Salzach river called Iuvavum — its baths in ruins, its forum overgrown, its old salt mines flooded — and decided to settle there. He built a small monastery dedicated to Saint Peter on a rock above the river. He reopened the mines. He invited Bavarian families to come and live near the new church. The town that grew up around the monastery would be called Salzburg, the salt city, and the monastery he built that summer would still be standing thirteen hundred years later.

He travelled back to Worms for help and returned with his niece Ehrentrude, whom he made the first abbess of the women's convent at Nonnberg — also still standing. He travelled to Ratisbon, where he established another monastery; to Lorch; to every district of Bavaria where his preachers would take. He died at Salzburg on Easter Sunday, 718, having said Mass for his community at sunrise. He was buried beside his rebuilt altar.

What he had founded was not just a Church. It was a country. Bavaria existed as a Christian region — and Austria after it — because a tired Frankish bishop in his sixties had agreed, at a duke's request, to walk into a ruined Roman town and not leave.

> *"There is salt enough here for all who come."* — Rupert, on naming Salzburg

## March 28

# John of Capistrano

*Franciscan, preacher, 1386–1456*

He was born at Capestrano in the Abruzzi, trained as a lawyer at the University of Perugia, and at twenty-six was made governor of Perugia by King Ladislaus of Naples. Two years later war broke out between Perugia and the Malatesta of Rimini. John, sent to negotiate, was seized and thrown into a Rimini prison cell. He was there for over a year. In the cell, at thirty, he had a vision — Francis of Assisi standing before him in habit — and emerged a different man. He annulled his marriage with his wife's consent (it had never been consummated; both took religious vows) and entered the Franciscan novitiate.

For the next forty years he preached. He preached across Italy, across the Holy Roman Empire, through Bohemia, Poland, Silesia, Hungary; he preached in Latin to the educated and through an interpreter to the rest; he preached for as long as ten hours a day; he preached so often that his voice failed and he kept on with whispered exhortations through his confessors. He reformed Franciscan houses. He served the Council of Florence as a peacemaker between Greeks and Latins. He was the central preacher of the Observant reform of his order.

When he was sixty-nine the Turks were at Belgrade. Constantinople had fallen two years earlier; the Hungarian frontier was the last barrier. Pope Callixtus III sent him north to preach a crusade. John, an old man already, raised a peasant army of perhaps forty thousand and joined János Hunyadi at the river. The siege of Belgrade in July 1456 was the last Christian victory before the long retreat — Belgrade held, Hunyadi died of plague a month later in the same camp, John of plague two months after that. He was seventy.

He had preached to anyone who would listen and led an army when no one else would, and died at the end of both efforts.

> *"He preached till his voice failed; then he prayed till his strength failed."* — the chronicle of his last campaign

## March 29

# Berthold of Carmel

*Founder of the Carmelites, d. c. 1195*

He was the son of a French noble house — kin, the chronicles say, of Aymeric of Limoges, who became Latin Patriarch of Antioch — and he came east on the Third Crusade. He fought in the field. He was at the siege of Antioch when the army contracted dysentery and the besiegers became the besieged, and he was nearly dead of fever in his tent when, in a vision, Christ reproached him for fighting on the wrong battleground. Berthold rose. He took off his armor. He walked out of the army and south.

He climbed Mount Carmel — the long ridge above the bay of Acre that the prophet Elijah had once climbed to confront the priests of Baal. There were already hermits living in caves along its western flank, some of them descended in spirit (and some, they insisted, in flesh) from the prophets. They lived alone. Berthold proposed to them that they live also together. He built an oratory dedicated to the Mother of God between their cells; he drew up a brief rule of life; he became their first prior. The community, by the time he died, had a name: the Brothers of the Blessed Virgin Mary of Mount Carmel.

What followed him was larger than he could have imagined. Within fifty years Albert of Jerusalem would give the community its formal Rule. Within a century the Carmelites would be a great mendicant order across Europe. Within four centuries Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross would reform them, and write the books of Christian mysticism that have not been matched.

Berthold himself appears in the historical record only briefly — the Carmelite tradition holds him to be its founder, the modern historians regard the founding as gradual — but every Carmelite altar still names him in its founding prayer.

> *"Set your fire on this mountain, as on the other."* — Berthold's prayer at Carmel

## March 30

# John Climacus

*Abbot of Sinai, c. 579–649*

He came to Mount Sinai at sixteen — to the monastery the emperor Justinian had built at the foot of the mountain where Moses had received the Law — and entered the novitiate under a Sinai monk named Martyrius. He stayed nineteen years. When Martyrius died, John retreated to a cave at Tholas, an hour's walk further into the wilderness, and lived there as a hermit for forty more.

He was over seventy when the brothers came to him asking him to be abbot. He accepted. He governed Sinai for four years. During those years another John, abbot of the neighboring monastery of Raithu, wrote to him asking for a small book — a manual for monks, *just the essentials*. John answered with thirty chapters and called the result *the Ladder*. Each chapter was a rung. The first rung was renunciation. The thirtieth was love. Between them came the disciplines and the discoveries — repentance, fear of God, mourning, freedom from anger, silence, sleep, vigilance, prayer, dispassion, faith. Every rung was a step that could be skipped at one's peril, and a step the climber would slip on.

The book has been read aloud in every Orthodox monastery during Great Lent ever since. It is the most-copied book of Byzantine monasticism. Western Christians too, when they encounter it, find the same recognition: *yes, this is what the work of conversion looks like; I had thought I was the only one slipping back.*

He had nothing to give his community at the end except the ladder. Its first rung is still where every reader has to begin, and where most of us, half-honestly, still are.

> *"A friend of stillness is a friend of God."* — John Climacus, *Ladder* 27

## March 31

# Benjamin the Deacon

*Persian martyr, d. 421*

He was a deacon of the Christian community in Persia — by the early fifth century a substantial Church, with bishops, monasteries, and three centuries of presence behind it — at a moment when the Sassanid king Bahram V was beginning a new persecution. The cause was political: the Christians of Persia were suspected of loyalty to the Roman Empire after the conversion of Constantine. The persecution was harsh.

Benjamin was arrested early. He was imprisoned for two years. At last a Roman envoy intervened on his behalf, and the king agreed to release him on one condition: that he never again preach the Christian faith publicly. The envoy accepted on Benjamin's behalf. Benjamin, when he learned of the bargain, broke it immediately. He went back to preaching the next day. The authorities re-arrested him.

The torture that followed is reported in detail by Sozomen, the church historian, who had reliable Persian sources. The executioners drove reeds under his fingernails and under the skin of his arms and legs. They drove a knotted stake through his abdomen. He was killed in stages over several days. He did not, the chronicler insists, recant; in the long intervals between the torments he prayed silently, and when he could, sang the psalter.

He had refused the bargain because there was nothing the king could give him that was worth the silence. He had only the gospel to give. He gave it for as long as he could speak it and a few minutes longer.

> *"A man who has known what light is cannot agree to walk in darkness."* — Benjamin, in the negotiation with the envoy

## April 1

# Hugh of Grenoble

*Bishop, 1052–1132*

He was a Burgundian canon of Valence cathedral, twenty-eight years old, when the papal legate Hugh of Die hauled him out of the choir stalls and made him bishop of Grenoble. Hugh of Grenoble did not want the see. Grenoble in 1080 was effectively a wreck: the cathedral was in ruins, the clergy were corrupt, the people had not been catechized in living memory, the diocese had been pillaged by lay lords who had pocketed its tithes for decades. He took it, on condition that he be allowed to retire to a monastery when the work was done.

The work was not done. He served as bishop for fifty-two years.

He tried, twice, to resign. The first time he walked away from the see and entered the Cluniac abbey of Chaise-Dieu; the pope ordered him out. The second time, after twenty more years at Grenoble, he sent a formal letter of resignation to Rome on grounds of failing health; the pope refused it. He gave up trying. He stayed.

In 1084 a small band of seven men, led by Bruno of Cologne — Hugh's former teacher at Reims — came to Grenoble looking for a solitary place to found a new kind of community. Hugh rode with them into the high mountains south of the city, to a remote valley called the Chartreuse, and gave them the land. The Carthusians were founded that summer. They consider Hugh of Grenoble their second father.

He wept at every Mass he celebrated for over forty years. The penitents who came to his confessional knew him for the same gentleness; he could not be hard with a sinner. He died at Grenoble at the age of eighty, in the cathedral he had rebuilt, in the diocese he had begged twice to be allowed to leave.

> *"He served the people of Grenoble because no one else would; he never said it was a privilege, only a debt."* — *Vita Hugonis*

## April 2

# Francis of Paola

*Founder of the Minims, 1416–1507*

He was the son of a Calabrian fisherman, born at Paola on the Tyrrhenian coast in southern Italy, and at thirteen, after a year of pilgrimage to Assisi and Rome with his parents, he asked to become a hermit. They found him a small cave in the cliffs outside Paola. He lived there alone for six years.

Other young men began to join him. By his twentieth year he had a small community; by his thirtieth a substantial one; by his fortieth a new order, approved by the Archbishop of Cosenza and confirmed by Sixtus IV — the Hermits of Saint Francis of Assisi, who would later be known as the Minims, the *least* of the friars. Francis added to the three traditional vows a fourth: perpetual abstinence from meat, eggs, and dairy. The Minims ate no animal product. They slept on boards. They did not own property as individuals or as houses.

He was famous in southern Italy as a wonderworker. The chronicle of his order records dozens of healings, several stilled storms, the multiplication of bread, the prediction of deaths — the standard catalogue of late medieval hagiography, well attested by the lay witnesses who testified at his canonization process less than two decades after his death. He was famous enough that King Louis XI of France, in his terminal illness in 1483, sent envoys all the way to Calabria asking Francis to come and pray with him. Francis went. He could not heal the king, but he could sit with him as he died, and he did. He stayed in France at the request of Louis's successors and founded Minim houses across the kingdom; he never went home.

He died at Plessis-lès-Tours at ninety-one, having been a hermit for seventy-eight years.

> *"Charity is the door through which we enter heaven; without it, the rest is locked."* — Francis of Paola

## April 3

# Richard of Chichester

*Bishop, 1197–1253*

He was Richard de Wych, born to a modest Worcestershire family that lost its lands during his minority. He and his elder brother, when they came of age, took up the plow themselves and farmed the surviving acres until they could pay off the debts and set the estate right again. Richard, who had wanted to study, gave the restored property to his brother as soon as it could stand on its own. He walked to Oxford at twenty-six with one set of clothes and a borrowed shilling. He stayed twelve years — at Oxford, then Paris, then Bologna for canon law — and came home a learned man.

Edmund of Abingdon, the archbishop of Canterbury, made him his chancellor. When Edmund died in 1240 and was followed by the unpopular Boniface of Savoy, Richard withdrew to a monastery, was ordained priest, and was elected bishop of Chichester in 1244. King Henry III, who had wanted his own candidate, refused to invest him. For two years Richard wandered the diocese on foot, lodging in country priories, doing the work of a bishop without the income of one. The king at last yielded. Richard ate at the same table as his clergy for the rest of his episcopate.

He preached, visited his parishes, defended the rights of the poor against the great landowners of Sussex, and went to bed early. The prayer he is best known for he had written in a quieter form decades earlier and recited daily: *Thanks be to thee, my Lord Jesus Christ, for all the benefits thou hast given me, for all the pains and insults thou hast borne for me. O most merciful Redeemer, friend, and brother, may I know thee more clearly, love thee more dearly, and follow thee more nearly, day by day.*

He died at Dover in 1253, on his way to preach a crusade he was not enthusiastic about, with the prayer on his lips.

> *"May I know thee more clearly, love thee more dearly, and follow thee more nearly, day by day."* — Richard of Chichester

## April 4

# Isidore of Seville

*Archbishop, doctor, c. 560–636*

He was the youngest of the four remarkable Hispano-Roman siblings — Leander, Fulgentius, Florentina, and Isidore — and the one who outlived the others by more than thirty years. Leander, archbishop of Seville, had taken charge of his youngest brother's education and, the chronicle says, was a stern teacher. Isidore ran away from his lessons once as a boy. When he came back, he had reflected on what he had seen in the woods — the constant slow drip of water wearing a hollow in a rock — and committed himself to study with a stubbornness no one expected. He never stopped. He became, in the next generation, the most learned man in the Latin West.

He succeeded Leander as archbishop of Seville around the year 600 and governed the see for thirty-six years. He continued his brother's work of consolidating the Spanish Catholic Church now that the Visigothic crown had abandoned Arianism. He presided at the Fourth Council of Toledo in 633, which fixed a single Spanish liturgy for the whole peninsula and a set of standards for the training of clergy. He wrote, in those decades, the books that would teach Europe for the next eight centuries.

The greatest of them was the *Etymologiae* — twenty books gathering everything the educated late-antique world had known into one work organized by the origins of words. Cosmology, grammar, theology, law, agriculture, medicine, music, the arts of war and the arts of peace, the names of the rivers, the names of the stones, the names of the angels: he tried to save it all. He was racing the dark. The Western Empire had collapsed; the libraries of Gaul and Italy were burning; the Spanish monasteries he had founded were one of the last places anyone was still copying ancient books. He gave them, in the *Etymologiae*, the textbook they would teach from for centuries.

He died in his cathedral at the age of seventy-six, having spent his last week distributing his own funds in alms.

> *"The day will come when you will know what you have not learned, and grieve to have neglected it."* — Isidore, in a letter to a young monk

## April 5

# Vincent Ferrer

*Dominican preacher, 1350–1419*

He was a Valencian Dominican, gifted enough to be a professor of theology before he was thirty and politically connected enough to become the personal confessor of Pedro de Luna, who in 1394 was elected as the Avignon claimant during the Great Western Schism — Benedict XIII. For two decades Vincent was the most prominent Dominican on the Avignon side. He defended Benedict's legitimacy in writing; he counseled him personally; he believed, for a long time, that the Schism could be ended only by Rome capitulating to Avignon.

A serious illness in 1398 cracked his certainty. In a fever he saw Christ between Saint Dominic and Saint Francis, who commissioned him to a new work: to walk the roads of Christendom preaching repentance. He recovered. He left Benedict's court. For the next twenty-one years, until his death, Vincent preached an itinerant mission across France, Spain, northern Italy, and the German-speaking lands. He preached in churches when they were open and from horseback when they were not; he preached in marketplaces and at city walls; he preached, the chroniclers say, almost daily and often twice a day. His themes were apocalyptic — the end was near, repentance was urgent, Christ was at the door.

He came at last to acknowledge that the Schism had to be ended by all three claimants stepping down. He persuaded the king of Aragon to withdraw obedience from Benedict in 1416. The Council of Constance closed the Schism the following year. Vincent had given up his old patron and his old certainty for the sake of the Church's unity, and the Church remembered.

He died at Vannes in Brittany in 1419, at sixty-nine, on a preaching tour through Picardy and Normandy. The Bretons buried him at the cathedral and have kept him as their adopted saint ever since.

> *"Whatever you have to do, do it as if it were your last act on earth."* — Vincent Ferrer, in a sermon at Toulouse

## April 6

# Methodius

*Apostle to the Slavs, c. 815–885*

He was the elder brother of Cyril, more reserved in temperament and slower to read a room — a Byzantine civil official before he was a monk, governor of a Slavic-speaking border province in his thirties. He resigned the office in his middle years and entered a monastery on Mount Olympus in Bithynia. He was the steady one. Cyril was the brilliant one. The mission they took together to Moravia in 862 belonged equally to both, but the man who held the work together for sixteen years after Cyril's death in Rome was Methodius.

He was consecrated archbishop of Sirmium — the old Roman see for Pannonia, lapsed for centuries — and sent back to Moravia by Pope Hadrian II to govern the new Slavic Church. He was forty-five then. He found the Frankish bishops of Bavaria furious at having lost ecclesiastical jurisdiction over the region. They arrested him on his journey north and imprisoned him for two and a half years in Swabia. Pope John VIII secured his release. He went on to Moravia, finished the translation of the Old Testament into Slavonic that he and Cyril had begun, established a Slavonic clergy, and was twice more dragged to Rome to defend his work against the Franks. He won both times.

He died at Velehrad on April 6, 885, surrounded by his disciples, after celebrating the Slavonic liturgy one last time on Palm Sunday. His final blessing went to the priest Gorazd, whom he named as his successor; within months of his death the Slavonic mission in Moravia was suppressed by the local prince at Frankish pressure, and the disciples were driven out. They went south. They took the alphabet, the books, and the liturgy with them into Bulgaria, where the work would take root and from which it would later spread to Russia.

He had outlived the brother he loved most and the work he could see immediately around him. What he had built endured by way of refugees.

> *"Bury me where I have lived. Christ goes with the body whether it is buried in honor or in exile."* — Methodius, on his deathbed

## April 7

# Hegesippus

*Church historian, c. 110–180*

He was a Greek-speaking Jewish convert to Christianity, born somewhere in Palestine or its Greek surroundings in the second decade after the destruction of the Temple. He lived at the moment when the apostolic generation was finally dying out and the church was beginning to wonder, for the first time, how it would tell its own story. The apostles had been remembered by men who had known them; the men who had known them were aged and dying; the third generation was already speaking. Hegesippus, who had relatives old enough to remember the apostolic preaching directly, decided to write down what could be saved.

He travelled. He visited Corinth, where he stayed long enough to consult the elders. He came on to Rome around the year 155, by way of half a dozen Christian communities along the way, and stayed in Rome through the episcopates of Anicetus, Soter, and Eleutherius — three popes, twenty-some years. He recorded in each city the apostolic succession of bishops he found there. He recorded the traditions handed down by name. He preserved the memory of James the Just, the bishop of Jerusalem, the brother of the Lord, whose death by stoning around 62 he recounted in vivid detail. He wrote five books of *Memoirs* — *Hypomnemata* — drawing the whole picture together.

The five books are lost. Almost all of what we have of Hegesippus comes through quotation in Eusebius of Caesarea, who in the next century relied on Hegesippus as his great early source. But Eusebius preserves enough that we know what Hegesippus did: he was the first man to take the historian's pen to the Christian story and refuse to let it pass into legend.

The Church remembers him as the first church historian after Luke. We owe to him most of what we know about the first hundred years.

> *"As long as the holy chorus of the apostles was alive, the Church remained a pure and uncorrupted virgin."* — Hegesippus, in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* III.32

## April 8

# Walter of Pontoise

*Abbot, c. 1030–1099*

He was a Picard, born at Andainville, and his early life was the life of a successful scholar: he taught philosophy and rhetoric at Saint-Germer-de-Fly and was famous enough to be sought out by students from across northern France. He grew unhappy with his own success. He left teaching in his thirties, walked to the Benedictine abbey at Rebais, took the habit, and asked to be assigned the most menial work the cellarer could find for him. The brothers, who had not expected the famous master to scrub floors, watched him do it for a year.

King Philip I of France, looking for an abbot for his new foundation at Pontoise, asked the abbot of Rebais to send Walter. Walter was horrified. He had walked away from public life. He refused. The king pressed; the bishop of Sens pressed; eventually his abbot ordered him. He accepted with what the chronicler calls *terrible reluctance* and tried, three times in the first decade of his abbacy, to flee. He fled once to Cluny — they sent him back. He fled once to a hermitage near Tours — the brothers of Pontoise tracked him down. He fled once all the way to Rome, intending to plead with the pope to release him; the pope, Gregory VII, listened with sympathy and then refused him outright. *Go back*, Gregory told him. *Your monks need you. I will not give you permission to be the kind of man you wanted to be. I will require you to be the kind of man God has asked you to be.*

He went back. He governed Pontoise for the rest of his life — thirty more years — with a remarkable gentleness toward novices and a remarkable severity toward himself. He could not stop trying to be small. He died on Easter Monday, 1099, in the abbey he had three times tried to leave.

> *"I will not give you permission to be the kind of man you wanted to be."* — Gregory VII to Walter

## April 9

# Waltrude of Mons

*Frankish noblewoman, abbess, c. 612–688*

She was the daughter of Walbert and Bertille of Lomme, sister of Aldegonde, granddaughter of a Lotharingian count; she married Madelgaire, a Frankish nobleman, and bore him four children. The family — by what the chroniclers describe as a quiet consensus reached over years — gave the Church four saints in one generation. Madelgaire eventually entered the monastery of Hautmont, taking the name Vincent. The children one by one took religious vows. Waltrude was the last left in the world.

She did not stay in it. She founded a small monastery on a wooded ridge above the river Trouille, at a place the Romans had called Castrilocus, and dedicated it to the Virgin. She gave away her dower lands to fund it. She brought together a community of women who wanted what she wanted — silence, work, the office, and the freedom to spend the rest of their days serving the lepers, the destitute, and the dying of the surrounding countryside. The settlement that grew up around the monastery would later be called Mons, the city. Mons exists because Waltrude built the chapter house first.

She governed the monastery for some thirty years. The chronicle of Mons records her habit of going out before dawn, in any weather, to the door of the leper-house at the foot of the ridge, where she would distribute bread and wash sores. She wore a hairshirt under her veil. She was famously gentle with the novices — *as a mother is with a frightened child*, the chronicler writes — and famously stern with herself.

She died at the convent at the age of seventy-six. The city of Mons keeps her feast as its founder.

> *"I have given everything to him; what little is left is also his."* — Waltrude, in her last years

## April 10

# Bademus of Persia

*Abbot and martyr, d. 376*

He was a wealthy citizen of Beth-Lapat in Persia who gave up his estate in his middle age, took a few companions into the hills outside the city, and founded a small monastery there. He governed it for some years as abbot. He was perhaps fifty when the Sassanid king Sapor II, in his decades-long persecution of the Persian Christians, had him arrested with seven of his monks.

He was kept in chains for four months. He was beaten daily — the chronicler Sozomen, who had reliable Persian informants, gives the details — and the executioners marvelled that he did not die. At the end of the four months the king's officers produced a young Christian named Nersan, an aristocrat who had apostatized under torture and pledged himself to the king's religion. The officers placed a sword in Nersan's hand and ordered him to kill Bademus. If he refused, they told him, he would be killed himself for backsliding.

Nersan stood with the sword in his hand for some time. He looked at the chained abbot. He raised the sword and could not bring it down. He raised it again. The abbot, looking at him, said only one thing: *strike, my son; the Lord forgives you the blow.* Nersan struck four times before the killing was finished. Bademus prayed for him as he died.

The chronicler completes the story. Nersan walked out of the prison a free man, into the kingdom whose religion he had embraced. He went mad within months. He could not stop seeing the abbot's face. He drew his own sword on himself, the same sword, and ended his own life.

Bademus had taken upon himself the death of the executor as well as his own. The mercy he had asked for the boy with the sword was the mercy he could not, in the end, give him from this side of the grave.

> *"Strike, my son; the Lord forgives you the blow."* — Bademus, to Nersan

## April 11

# Stanislaus of Krakow

*Bishop and martyr, 1030–1079*

He was born at Szczepanów in southern Poland to noble parents who had waited thirty years for a child and had vowed to give the boy to God if one was granted. They kept the vow. He was educated at Gniezno and at Liège in the Low Countries, came home a priest, and was made canon and then preacher at the cathedral of Krakow. He was elected bishop of Krakow in 1072. He was forty-two.

The king of Poland in those years was Boleslaus II, called *the Bold*, a brilliant and reckless ruler whose conquests had won the kingdom new territory and whose private conduct was a public scandal. He had kidnapped the wife of a nobleman named Mściszko and was keeping her as his concubine. He had levied unjust taxes. He had executed magnates for disagreement. Stanislaus, the new bishop, warned him privately first, then publicly, then formally — and at last, when no warning had effect, declared the king excommunicate.

Boleslaus rode to the small church of Saint Michael on the rock outside Krakow on April 11, 1079. Stanislaus was celebrating Mass at the altar. The king walked in with his guards and ordered them to seize the bishop. They refused. The king drew his own sword and killed Stanislaus where he stood, at the altar, in the middle of the canon. The chronicle of Gallus Anonymus records the king's later remorse — Boleslaus fled Poland the same year, ended his days in obscurity in Hungary — and Gregory VII placed Poland under interdict.

Stanislaus has been the patron of Poland since his canonization in 1253. He stood, against a king, on the simplest principle a bishop can hold: that no power on earth can sanctify what God has forbidden.

> *"You shall not put away the wife of your neighbor."* — Stanislaus to the king

## April 12

# Julius I, Pope

*Bishop of Rome, d. 352*

He was elected pope in 337, the year of Constantine's death, in the middle of the Arian crisis that would consume the fourth century. His predecessor Mark had governed for only ten months; his predecessor Sylvester had presided over Nicaea. Julius inherited a Roman Church that was both confidently orthodox and politically vulnerable: orthodox because the Roman clergy had always been Nicene, vulnerable because the imperial court at Constantinople had begun to favor the Arian party.

The crisis came to him almost immediately. The bishops of the East had, at a synod at Antioch in 339, deposed Athanasius of Alexandria — the chief defender of the Nicene formula — and replaced him with an Arian. Athanasius fled to Rome. Julius received him. He examined his case carefully and accepted his appeal. He summoned the Eastern bishops to come to Rome and answer for their conduct. They refused. Julius held the council anyway, in 341, with fifty Western bishops present, and reinstated Athanasius. He then wrote to the Eastern bishops a letter — the *Synodal Letter of Julius*, preserved by Athanasius — that contains the first explicit assertion in papal history that a bishop deposed by his own province may appeal to the see of Peter, and that the see of Peter cannot be bypassed in such cases. *Why was no notice given to us*, he asked the Eastern bishops, *especially concerning Athanasius? Or do you not know that this has been the custom — that the matter be referred to us, and then the right sentence be given?*

The letter did not end the controversy. Athanasius was exiled twice more after Julius's death. But the precedent was set. The Western Church had not abandoned Nicaea, and the Roman bishop had spoken plainly.

Julius died in 352 after a fifteen-year pontificate. He had built two great basilicas in Rome and had hidden a friend in his own household for seven years.

> *"Or do you not know that this has been the custom — that the matter be referred to us?"* — Julius I, *Synodal Letter*

## April 13

# Martin I, Pope

*Bishop of Rome, martyr, d. 655*

He was a Tuscan, born at Todi, deacon of the Roman Church for years before his election in 649. He was the last pope to die a martyr. The Christological controversy of his century was the question of how many wills the Lord had — one (the Monothelite position) or two (the Chalcedonian position, by then the position of the West). The eastern emperor Constans II, trying to preserve the political unity of the empire against the Arab advance, had issued an edict called the *Typos* in 648 forbidding any further discussion of the question.

Martin, six months into his pontificate, ignored the *Typos*. He convened the Lateran Council of 649, with a hundred and five Western bishops, and condemned Monothelitism. The council was theologically careful and politically suicidal. The emperor responded by ordering the bishop of Rome arrested.

The arrest took three years to arrange. Martin was ill in 653 when the imperial exarch finally seized him from the Lateran Basilica. He was carried in chains by sea from Italy to Constantinople, kept on the ship in his sickness for months, paraded through the streets of the capital, tried for treason, found guilty, stripped, beaten, and sentenced to death. The patriarch of Constantinople, who held no friendship for him, nonetheless intervened to save his life; the sentence was commuted to exile.

He was taken to Chersonesus in the Crimea. The local Christians, fearing the imperial wrath, would not approach him. He had with him a few of his clergy and the clothes he had been wearing when he was arrested. He wrote a final letter back to Rome lamenting that even his own clergy had forgotten him — a new pope had already been elected while he was still alive. He died of starvation and exposure within months.

What Martin had refused was the simplest of imperial requests: silence on a question of faith. The Lateran's condemnation of Monothelitism was vindicated thirty years later at the Third Council of Constantinople, with Martin's name in the *Diptychs*.

> *"I cannot promise to keep silent about what the Lord has commanded to be confessed."* — Martin, before the tribunal in Constantinople

## April 14

# Lidwina of Schiedam

*Mystic, 1380–1433*

She was a fifteen-year-old girl in the Dutch town of Schiedam, skating on the frozen canals with her friends on the second of February 1396, when another skater knocked her down. She broke a rib. The break did not heal. Over the next months a kind of internal abscess developed and burst; the infection spread; complications followed complications. By the time she was sixteen she could not walk. By her early twenties she was bedridden, and she would not leave the bed for the remaining thirty-five years of her life.

The physical record her biographer keeps is harrowing. By twenty-five she had lost the sight of one eye. By thirty she could swallow only liquids. By forty she was paralyzed in one arm. By fifty most of her body was rotted by bedsores. She had nothing the medicine of her age could fix. Modern doctors who have studied the records suspect multiple sclerosis — Lidwina is one of the earliest documented cases. She made no comment on the suffering except to say that she had offered it for the sins of the world, and she meant it.

What she had in place of mobility was vision. She saw heaven and purgatory in extended visions that her confessor, a Schiedam priest named Jan Pot, recorded over thirty years. She had visitations from angels, from the saints, from the Virgin. She prayed for individuals she had never met and gave her confessor, by name, intercessory burdens to carry to the bishop. She communicated to her household and visitors in a voice that grew weaker every year and at last was a whisper. She lived, in her last years, on the Eucharist alone.

She died on the eve of Easter, 1433, at fifty-three, after a final night of unusually quiet prayer.

Thomas à Kempis would write her life. She is the saint of long illness — of the patience that does not give back what it has had to receive.

> *"This is what was sent me; let me give it back to him who sent it."* — Lidwina, of her suffering

## April 15

# Hunna of Strasbourg

*Holy washerwoman, d. 679*

She was the wife of a Frankish nobleman named Huno of Strasbourg, a Christian lord with lands along the Rhine, and she was rich enough to live in any manner she chose. She chose laundry.

Strasbourg in the seventh century was a city the way most early medieval cities were a city: a Roman stone core surrounded by wooden houses and yards full of refugees, day laborers, soldiers' widows, unwed mothers, beggars. The poor of the city went without clean clothes for the same reason they went without clean water: it was work no one was paid to do for them, and they could not do it for themselves. Hunna decided to do it for them. She kept a wash-house at one of the family properties on the river. She sent her servants for the dirty clothes of the poor, washed them with her own hands beside her servants, dried them, and had them returned. The people of Strasbourg, who knew exactly whose hands were at the wash-tub, gave her the nickname that the Bollandists kept: *Hunna die heilige Wäscherin*, Hunna the holy washerwoman.

She took special care of the unwed mothers, whom no one else would help, and of the old men of the streets whose clothes had gone beyond what any servant would touch. She kept this up for the better part of forty years. Her husband Huno supported the work without ever joining it; their only son Deodatus eventually entered the abbey of Ebersmünster and became a monk. Hunna died in old age, around 679, and was buried at the parish church of Hunabach near Strasbourg.

Her local cult was so persistent that the bishop of Strasbourg, in 1520 — eight and a half centuries after her death — formally petitioned Rome for her canonization. Leo X granted it. She is one of the very few saints whose canonization rested on the testimony of generation after generation of women who had needed clean clothes and remembered who had brought them.

> *"What the poor cannot do for themselves, somebody must do for them."* — the chronicle on Hunna's work

## April 16

# Magnus of Orkney

*Earl, martyr, c. 1075–1118*

He was a Norse-Scottish earl of the Orkney islands, co-ruler with his cousin Haakon Paulsson, in the years when the islands were still Viking polity and the Christian faith was still recent enough that adult conversion was the norm. Magnus had been baptized in his teens; he had taken the gospel seriously. His Christianity made him an embarrassment to his fellow Norsemen almost from the start. On a Viking raid in Wales in 1098, while the longships were closing on a Welsh village, Magnus refused to take up arms; he sat on the deck of his own ship and sang psalms aloud while the killing was done on shore. The skalds laughed. The chroniclers kept the story.

He and Haakon governed Orkney jointly for some years, in the inherited Norse arrangement. The arrangement frayed. Haakon's faction, ambitious for sole rule, grew restless. The two earls agreed to meet on the small island of Egilsay at Easter 1118 to negotiate peace, each bringing two ships and a small escort. Magnus came with two ships. Haakon arrived with eight.

Magnus understood at once what the meeting had become. He spent the night in the small church of Egilsay in prayer. In the morning he walked out and offered Haakon three alternatives: exile in pilgrimage, imprisonment for life, or mutilation. Haakon would have accepted any of them, but his nobles, who wanted blood, demanded the killing. Haakon's standard-bearer refused to swing the axe. Haakon ordered his cook, a man named Lifolf, to do it. Magnus took Lifolf by the shoulders, told him no innocent man should kill another, asked him not to be afraid, and gave him a blessing. *Strike hard on the crown*, he said, *for it is no death for a chieftain to be cut down like a thief.* Lifolf wept and struck.

His tomb at Kirkwall on Orkney is in the great cathedral that bears his name — the largest medieval cathedral in Scotland, raised by his nephew Rögnvald a generation later in penance.

> *"It is no death for a chieftain to be cut down like a thief; strike hard on the crown."* — Magnus, to his executioner

## April 17

# Stephen Harding

*Cistercian abbot, 1059–1134*

He was an Englishman, born at Sherborne in Dorset in the years just before the Conquest, educated at the Benedictine abbey there, and old enough to remember the world before Hastings. He left the abbey in his twenties and wandered. He spent time in Scotland, Paris, Rome — restless, a young monk looking for a stricter life than the conventional Benedictinism of his day offered. He came home to France and to the abbey of Molesme in Burgundy, where Abbot Robert was beginning, with his prior Alberic, to talk about a return to the literal observance of the Rule. Stephen joined them.

In 1098 the three men — Robert, Alberic, and Stephen — walked twenty-some miles south from Molesme to a marshy clearing called Cîteaux and founded a new monastery there. They wanted, simply, the Rule of Saint Benedict followed without medieval accretion: no income from tithes, no peasants working monastic lands, no elaborate liturgy, no decorated churches. They wanted seven hours of work in the fields a day, the office in plain chant, and silence. The Cistercian movement began that summer.

Stephen became the third abbot in 1109, after Robert had returned to Molesme and Alberic had died. He governed Cîteaux for the next twenty-four years. He wrote — and persuaded a council of his abbots to ratify — the *Charter of Charity*, the brief constitutional document on which the Cistercian order has run ever since. He revised the Cistercian Bible from the best available manuscripts. He reformed the chant. He admitted Bernard of Clairvaux and his thirty companions in 1112, the moment at which the order's expansion truly began.

He resigned in 1133, very old and very tired. He died at Cîteaux the next year. He had founded an order, not by inventing anything new, but by refusing to forget what the Rule had asked.

> *"Let us return to the Rule, that we may live."* — Stephen, in the *Exordium Parvum*

## April 18

# Apollonius the Apologist

*Roman senator, martyr, c. 130s–185*

He was a Roman senator of philosophical bent, a Stoic in his early manhood and a Christian in his middle years, when his household slave Severus denounced him to the urban authorities around the year 185. Under Marcus Aurelius and now under Commodus, the imperial position on Christianity was ambiguous: the religion was illegal but the law was rarely enforced unless someone brought a charge. A slave's accusation of his master was technically illegal under a Hadrianic rescript that Eusebius preserves; the magistrate first ordered Severus executed for the act of denunciation, and then turned to Apollonius himself.

The case went, unusually, to the Senate. The senators had to decide what to do with one of their own. They gave Apollonius the chance to defend himself; he was a senator, after all, and a philosopher. The Acts of Apollonius — preserved in Greek and Armenian — record the defense. He spoke at length and learnedly on the Christian doctrine of God, the soul, the immortality of the just, the failures of pagan philosophy to attain the truth that Christ had revealed. He treated his accusers with patient courtesy. He admitted to being a Christian. He refused to retract.

The Senate could not acquit him under the existing edicts against the cult. The praetorian prefect, Tigidius Perennis, presided unwillingly. The sentence handed down was beheading — the form of execution due to a Roman senator. He was led out and killed the same day.

His apology, in shorter form, became one of the early Christian texts read aloud in the catechumenate. He had used the only weapon he had — the philosophical learning he had been raised with — and used it without bitterness against men he could have shamed. He demonstrated, in the most public forum the empire offered, that a Christian could also be a gentleman, and that the gospel did not contradict but completed the best of the Greco-Roman mind.

> *"I am a Christian, and for this I give thanks; I cannot deny what I owe my life to."* — Apollonius, before the Senate

## April 19

# Alphege of Canterbury

*Archbishop, martyr, 954–1012*

He was a West Country Englishman, monk at Deerhurst as a young man and prior at Glastonbury after that, abbot of Bath, bishop of Winchester at the unusually young age of thirty. He was made archbishop of Canterbury in 1006, in a kingdom that had been under intermittent Viking attack for thirty years and was about to enter the worst of it. Sweyn Forkbeard and his son Canute were raiding the south coast every summer; the English crown was paying Danegeld in increasing amounts and the Vikings were taking more anyway.

The summer of 1011 they besieged Canterbury. Alphege was inside the city. The Vikings broke in by treachery after a three-week siege, sacked the cathedral, killed many of the monks, and took Alphege and several of his clergy hostage. They demanded three thousand pounds of silver in ransom for the archbishop — an enormous sum, which the diocese could only have raised by stripping every parish in Kent and starving every farmer south of the Thames. Alphege forbade his diocese to pay it. He sat in the Viking camp at Greenwich for seven months refusing to be ransomed.

On the night of April 19, 1012, the Viking host held a great drunken feast. The mead ran freely. They sent for Alphege. They demanded payment one last time. He refused one last time. The drunken Vikings began throwing the bones and skulls of the slaughtered cattle from the feast at him. They beat him with the flats of axes. At last a Viking named Thrum, who had been baptized the day before by Alphege himself in the Viking camp, took an axe and put it through the archbishop's skull, *for charity*, the chronicle says, *to end his sufferings*.

He was the first archbishop of Canterbury to die for the see. Thomas Becket, a hundred and fifty years later on the same hill, would call him to mind in his last sermon.

> *"I will not buy my life with the bread of the poor."* — Alphege, refusing the ransom

## April 20

# Agnes of Montepulciano

*Dominican prioress, 1268–1317*

She was born into a noble Tuscan family at Gracciano, near Montepulciano, and at nine years old — her parents and the local abbess having agreed on the future — she was given to the small Franciscan convent of the *Sackcloths* a few miles away. She begged her parents to do so. She had wanted the convent since she could speak. At fourteen she was placed in charge of a new foundation at Procena, in central Italy, as its first prioress: a startling appointment, made by special papal dispensation because the senior nuns had unanimously requested her despite her age.

She governed Procena for two decades. The accounts of her ministry there are full of the standard medieval markers of holiness — miracles of multiplied food, healings, predictions, ecstasies — and full also of less easily classified gifts: a manner of receiving troubled visitors that left them, the chroniclers say, *as if washed clean of the burden they had brought*. Her reputation grew across central Italy. The town of her birth, Montepulciano, eventually petitioned her to come home and found a convent there. She did, in 1306, transferring her vows from the Franciscans to the Dominicans on the way (the Montepulciano foundation followed the Dominican Rule). She governed it for the remaining eleven years of her life.

Her great visitor was a young woman from Siena, sixty years her junior, who would later be called Catherine of Siena. Catherine came once to the convent at Montepulciano while Agnes was still alive — she was eight; she would have remembered nothing — and several times after her death. Catherine attributed several of her own discernments to Agnes's intercession.

Agnes died at forty-nine in April 1317, after a long illness in which she had repeatedly told her sisters not to mourn — *I am going where I can love you better*. Her body remained famously incorrupt for centuries.

> *"I am going where I can love you better."* — Agnes, to her sisters, in her last illness

## April 21

# Anselm of Canterbury

*Archbishop, doctor, 1033–1109*

He was born at Aosta in the Italian Alps, a sensitive boy whose mother died when he was twelve and whose father, on a quarrel, refused to let him enter the monastery. He ran away from home at twenty-three with one servant, crossed the Alps on foot, and presented himself eventually at the Norman abbey of Bec, where Lanfranc — who would later be his archbishop — was master of the school. Anselm took the habit. He never went back to Aosta.

He became prior of Bec at thirty, abbot at forty-five, archbishop of Canterbury at sixty — every promotion against his explicit and stated preference. He told the brothers, when they elected him to Bec, that they had chosen a man who would not do the work well; he told the bishops of England, when they elected him to Canterbury, that they had chosen one who could not.

What he did between offices was think. The *Monologion*, his first major work, set out to prove what could be known of God from reason alone. The *Proslogion*, written shortly after, gave the world the famous ontological argument: *that than which nothing greater can be thought* must exist, because if it did not, something greater could be thought. The *Cur Deus Homo* asked why God became man and answered, in a sustained argument that still shapes Western theology, that the satisfaction of divine justice required it. He invented an entire genre of Christian philosophy: *fides quaerens intellectum*, faith seeking understanding. He insisted he was not arguing himself toward faith but praying himself toward understanding what he already believed.

Twice exiled by English kings during the investiture controversy, he returned twice and finished his work. He died at Canterbury at seventy-six, Holy Wednesday, having that morning been read the gospel for the day.

> *"I do not seek to understand in order to believe; I believe in order to understand."* — Anselm, *Proslogion* I

## April 22

# Theodore of Sykeon

*Bishop, abbot, c. 540–613*

He was born in a Galatian inn — his mother was a prostitute, his father a Byzantine civil servant passing through — and he was raised, against the odds of his beginnings, into a learned and saintly man. His mother left the trade after his birth and ran the inn with two other women who had also come out of similar lives; the household was poor, hard, and entirely dedicated to scrubbing the boy clean of his inheritance. He had a holy monk for a tutor in his teens. He took the habit at himself perhaps eighteen.

He founded a monastery near the village of Sykeon, in central Anatolia, and governed it for years. His Life — written by his disciple George — is one of the great surviving Byzantine hagiographies: it preserves a daily texture of the Anatolian countryside that no other source gives us. The villagers brought him their possessed, their crops blighted by hail, their sick children, their family quarrels, their unfaithful wives, their drunkard husbands. He treated each case at the length it required. He could exorcize, the chronicler insists, with a touch; he could also spend three weeks counseling a young couple who had simply learned that they did not know each other.

He was made bishop of the local see of Anastasiopolis in 591, against his preference. He governed it for a decade and resigned, exhausted, to return to the monastery he had founded. He served as adviser to the emperor Maurice, then to Phocas who killed Maurice, then to Heraclius who killed Phocas. He buried friends from all three administrations and never lost his attachment to the village of his beginning.

He died at Sykeon in 613, in the cell he had built with his own hands fifty years earlier.

> *"The Lord brought me out of the mud; let me not return to dust before he calls me."* — Theodore, in his last illness

## April 23

# Adalbert of Prague

*Bishop, martyr, 956–997*

He was a Bohemian nobleman, baptized Vojtěch, sent to school at Magdeburg under the great Saxon Bishop Adalbert (whose name he took at confirmation). He came home in his twenties, was made the second bishop of Prague in 982, and tried for the next decade and a half to be a bishop in a country that did not entirely want one. Bohemia in the late tenth century was still half-pagan; the Christianity that did exist was mixed with old practice and protected mostly by aristocratic interest. Adalbert preached against polygamy among the nobles, against slavery, against the sale of Christians to Jewish slave-traders, against the disregard of marriage law. The nobles complained to Rome. Adalbert was repeatedly forced into exile, repeatedly recalled, and at last gave up.

He took refuge in the Benedictine abbey of Saints Boniface and Alexius on the Aventine in Rome, where he lived for years as a simple monk. He had been formed for this life and never quite found anything else satisfying. The German emperor Otto III, who knew him personally and admired him, prevailed on him to return to mission work — this time to the Prussians on the Baltic coast, who had not yet been touched by Christian preaching.

Adalbert sailed up the Baltic in 996 with two companions, the monk Benedict and the priest Gaudentius (Adalbert's half-brother). They landed near the mouth of the Vistula. The Prussian pagans, who had been promised by their priests that the new gods would destroy their crops, would not hear them out. Three weeks after landing, on the morning of April 23, 997, Adalbert was killed by a war-party of Prussian priests with a sevenfold thrust of a lance. His head was placed on a stake.

The Polish duke Bolesław I ransomed the body for its weight in gold and buried it at Gniezno. Otto III walked to the tomb barefoot the following year. Adalbert is patron of three nations: Bohemia, Poland, and Hungary, none of which he managed to convert in his lifetime.

> *"I have not preached well; let me at least die well."* — Adalbert, before his last journey

## April 24

# Mellitus of Canterbury

*Archbishop, d. 624*

He was a Roman abbot, perhaps of the monastery on the Caelian Hill that Gregory the Great had himself founded, when Gregory in 601 sent a second wave of missionaries to England to reinforce Augustine of Canterbury. Mellitus led them. He carried Gregory's famous instructions on what to do with the pagan temples — *do not destroy them, but cleanse them with holy water and rededicate the altars* — and a small library of liturgical books for the new English Church.

Augustine made him the first bishop of London. The Saxon king of Essex, Saeberht, had received baptism; his city of London was to be the new see. Mellitus served there for fifteen years. He built the first cathedral of Saint Paul, on the same hill where the present one still stands. He baptized many; he travelled to Gaul and Rome for synods; he was a diligent if not famous bishop. He was getting old.

When Saeberht died in 616, his three pagan sons inherited the kingdom and the new bishop. They came to a service and demanded the Eucharist from Mellitus, although they had not been baptized. He refused. They drove him out of the kingdom. He took ship for Gaul, intending to retire to a monastery on the continent, but the bishops of Canterbury called him back when the political situation eased. He returned to find that Justus had been made archbishop in his absence; when Justus died in 619, Mellitus was elected to succeed him.

He served as archbishop of Canterbury for the last five years of his life. He was old by then; he suffered terribly from gout; he could not always travel. The famous fire-quenching story Bede preserves comes from his last years: Canterbury caught fire, the wind drove the flames toward the cathedral, and the monks carried Mellitus on a stretcher into the path of the blaze. He prayed. The wind changed.

He died on April 24, 624, in the cathedral he had saved.

> *"The Lord, who has carried me through many fires, will carry me through this one too."* — Mellitus, before the fire was set against him

## April 25

# Mark the Evangelist

*Evangelist, first century*

He was John Mark, son of a Mary whose house in Jerusalem was the upper room where the first Christians met. He was the cousin of Barnabas. He went with Paul and Barnabas on the first missionary journey to Cyprus and Asia Minor, lost his nerve at Perga in Pamphylia, and went home. Paul, hard, would not take him on the second journey; Barnabas took him to Cyprus anyway. The split between the two men is one of the small bruises in the Acts of the Apostles. By the time Paul wrote his last letter from a Roman prison years later, the bruise had healed: *Take Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me in ministry.*

The early Church remembered him as the disciple of Peter, who had preached the gospel through him. Papias of Hierapolis, writing in the 130s, says Mark wrote down Peter's preaching at the Roman Christians' request. The Gospel that bears his name is the shortest, the briskest, the most physical — the one that pays attention to bodies and weather and the faces of bystanders, and that ends in the original manuscripts with the women fleeing the tomb in fear and silence. It is the Gospel of a man who had stood at the edge of the apostolic generation and watched.

Tradition sends him to Alexandria. He founded the Church there, the patriarchates of Alexandria still trace their succession to him, and he was killed by a hostile crowd on Easter Monday around the year 68 — dragged with a rope around his neck through the streets of the city to a place outside the walls. The Egyptian Christians buried him in a small church on the harbor.

Eight centuries later Venetian merchants stole the body from Alexandria and carried it to the lagoon city that has been Mark's adopted home ever since. The Lion of Venice is his.

> *"Take Mark, and bring him with you; for he is useful to me in ministry."* — Paul, *Second Letter to Timothy* 4:11

## April 26

# Stephen of Perm

*Missionary, c. 1340–1396*

He was a Russian monk from Veliky Ustyug in the northern forests, born to a family of moderate means, educated at the cathedral school there and then at the Rostov monastery of Saint Gregory the Theologian. He was a learned young man — he had taught himself Greek as well as Slavonic, which was unusual in a Russian provincial monk of his generation — and he could have stayed at Rostov in scholarship. He had a different plan. East of his hometown, in the great forests along the upper Kama and Vychegda rivers, there lived a Finno-Ugric people called the Komi, or the Zyrians. They were pagan, animist, and untouched by Christian preaching. Stephen had grown up hearing them at the trading posts in his youth. He resolved to go to them.

He learned the Komi language as no Russian monk had ever bothered to learn it. He devised, by adapting the local property-marks the Komi used on their houses and forest trees, an entire alphabet for them — the *Anbur* — and translated the gospels, the psalter, and the Liturgy of John Chrysostom into Komi using it. He preached among them in their own tongue for fourteen years. He confronted the local shamans peacefully — when one challenged him to walk into a fire to prove his god, Stephen agreed; when one challenged him to dive under the ice of the frozen Vychegda, Stephen agreed. The shamans, faced with his readiness, repeatedly backed down.

He was consecrated bishop of Perm in 1383. He served thirteen years more, going north to baptize villages in summer and back to Moscow in winter to argue for the Komi against Muscovite officials who wanted their lands. He died at Moscow in 1396, on a winter journey, having spent twenty-three years giving a Christian text to a people who had never had any text at all.

> *"They cannot read what they cannot read; let me give them the letters first."* — Stephen, on his alphabet

## April 27

# Zita of Lucca

*Domestic servant, 1212–1272*

She was twelve when her parents, peasants from the hill village of Monsagrati outside Lucca, placed her in the household of the wealthy Fatinelli family in the city as a domestic servant. Lucca in the thirteenth century was a banking and silk capital and the Fatinellis were merchants of the first rank. Zita scrubbed floors. She rose at four. She walked to Mass before dawn at the parish church of San Frediano, came home, prepared the family's breakfast, did the laundry, the cleaning, the marketing, the sewing, and the cooking. She did all of it for the same family for the next forty-eight years.

The other servants disliked her. She prayed during her work, gave away her own portion of food to beggars at the door, and was suspected — at first reasonably, then unreasonably — of stealing the family's bread for her charity. The chroniclers tell the story that became proverbial in Lucca: she was once carrying loaves out to the poor in her apron and the master stopped her on the stairs to ask what she had wrapped there. *Flowers and leaves*, she said, in a moment of panicked invention. He opened the apron. Flowers and leaves fell out.

The Fatinellis came to revere her. The children, raised with her, treated her as a second mother. The neighborhood Lucchese came to her for prayer through the wall while she worked. She kept doing the same housework she had been doing at twelve. She had never wanted any other vocation. She had not, she told her confessor near the end, been called to anything more elaborate than the cleaning of one good family's house for a great many years; she had wanted only to do it well.

She died in her room above the kitchen at sixty, on April 27, 1272, asking for the Eucharist. The whole of Lucca came to her funeral. Her body, found incorrupt centuries later, still lies under glass at San Frediano.

> *"A servant is not pious if she is not industrious."* — Zita, in the household

## April 28

# Vitalis and Valeria

*Roman officer-couple, martyrs at Ravenna and Milan, d. by tradition c. 70*

He was a Roman *vir militaris*, perhaps a *primipilus* of one of the legions garrisoning northern Italy; she was his wife, a noblewoman of a lesser patrician house. They were the parents of Gervasius and Protasius, the boy-martyrs of Milan whose discovery of relics in 386 produced one of the great moments of Ambrose's episcopate. The acts place the family at Ravenna in the early reign of Nero and assign their conversion to an apostolic-era Christian community there.

The husband's death came first. He had been at Ravenna in the late 60s when a Christian physician named Ursicinus, refusing to sacrifice, was condemned to be beheaded. The crowd gathered. Ursicinus, awaiting the sword, faltered — the natural human terror at the moment of execution, the *acta* note with rare candor — and was on the point of recanting. Vitalis stepped out of the crowd, took him by the shoulders, and said: *Brother, fix thy mind on the eternal kingdom and depart not from the way of life.* Ursicinus held. The execution was carried out. Vitalis recovered the body and buried it in the Christian cemetery outside the city.

The Roman authorities, informed that a soldier of his rank had intervened openly, arrested him within a week. The interrogation was perfunctory; he confessed his Christianity at once. He was tortured on the *equuleus* and finally buried alive in a pit at a place called *Ad Palmam* outside the southern walls. The Christian community recovered the body from beneath the rubble that night.

Valeria, hearing the news at Milan, set out for Ravenna to bring her husband home for burial. She did not get there. On the road near Cremona she met a procession of pagan farmers carrying an idol to the spring planting; they asked her to join the offering; she refused and named herself a Christian. They set on her with the staffs they were carrying. She died at the roadside.

The two sons are at Milan, *Sant'Ambrogio*, under the high altar Ambrose laid for them. The father is at Ravenna in the church raised over the floor where his pit had been. The mother, between them, is on the road. The whole family, the AASS notes drily, was unable to be buried in one place.

> *"Brother, fix thy mind on the eternal kingdom and depart not from the way of life."* — Vitalis to Ursicinus, in the public square at Ravenna

## April 29

# Catherine of Siena

*Dominican mystic, doctor, 1347–1380*

She was the twenty-fourth of twenty-five children born to a Sienese wool-dyer named Jacopo Benincasa and his wife Lapa. Her twin sister died at birth. Catherine, from the age of six, was a child apart: she had seen a vision of Christ above the Dominican basilica of San Domenico, and she had not stopped seeing him. She made a vow of virginity at seven. When her family tried, at twelve, to push her toward marriage with her elder sister's widower, she cut off her hair to make herself unmarriageable, and the family relented.

At sixteen she joined the Mantellate — the lay Dominican women of Siena who wore the black mantle of the order but lived in their own homes. She locked herself in her small room above her father's dye-shop for three years and spoke to no one but her confessor. She came out at nineteen because Christ, in vision, had told her she had had enough of solitude and was now to begin serving the poor.

She did. She nursed plague victims at Santa Maria della Scala. She gathered disciples — *Caterinati*, the chronicler called them, men and women who left their lives to follow her — and dictated to them, since she could barely write, the four hundred letters and the great *Dialogue* and the *Prayers* that have made her one of the most-read mystics of the Western tradition. She intervened in the Avignon papacy: in 1376 she travelled to Avignon and persuaded Pope Gregory XI, against his cardinals and his own anxiety, to return the papacy to Rome. She failed to mediate the Western Schism that broke out two years after his death and the failure broke her. She offered her body, the chronicler says, in expiation for the divided Church.

She died at Rome in 1380, thirty-three years old, after a long fast of her own choosing. Six centuries later Paul VI named her Doctor of the Church.

> *"Proclaim the truth, and do not be silent through fear."* — Catherine of Siena, *Letter to Stefano Maconi*

## April 30

# Pius V, Pope

*Dominican, Counter-Reformer, 1504–1572*

He was Antonio Ghislieri, born to a poor family at Bosco Marengo in Piedmont, and his early childhood was spent tending sheep on the family's small plot. He met the Dominicans by accident at fourteen when one of them paid for the boy to be given a real education, and the boy entered the order in gratitude. He proved a remarkable scholar. He taught philosophy and theology in Dominican houses for sixteen years. He became, in his forties, a papal inquisitor in northern Italy; he was scrupulous in his judgments, harsh on his own conduct, fasting and walking everywhere himself when his colleagues took carriages. Paul IV made him cardinal in 1557. Pius IV made him Grand Inquisitor of the Roman Inquisition in 1558.

He was elected pope in 1566. He was sixty-two. He wore the white wool Dominican habit under his papal robes for the rest of his life — he had been a Dominican longer than he had been a churchman — and he ran the papal household as if it were a convent. He cut court expenses. He gave the savings to the poor of Rome. He insisted that the bishops actually reside in their dioceses, that the seminaries the Council of Trent had decreed actually open, that the catechism actually be printed and used. He gave the Latin Church the Roman Missal of 1570 — the *Missal of Pius V*, the form that would govern Catholic worship for the next four centuries.

In 1571 he formed the Holy League against the Ottoman fleet that had taken Cyprus and was preparing to take more. The combined Christian fleet under Don Juan of Austria defeated the Ottomans at Lepanto on the seventh of October. Pius, in Rome, knew the outcome before the news arrived — he interrupted a meeting that afternoon, walked to the window, said *let us thank God; we have won a great victory*, and would not say more.

He died six months later, sixty-eight, asking only for the prayers of his old order.

> *"I am a Dominican; let me die in this habit."* — Pius V, before his death
