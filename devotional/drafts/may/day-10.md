## May 10

# Comgall of Bangor

*Abbot, founder of Bangor, c. 516–601*

Saint Patrick, the Irish said, had stood in the valley two generations before Comgall was born and seen there a vision of fire, and had prophesied that a son of life would come and raise a great house on the spot. When the boy was born and grew, the sign came again: his mother, finding him asleep one afternoon in the field, saw a column of fire let down from heaven to the sleeping child, and was afraid to wake him. He woke with a ruddy face and shining eyes and told her to tell no one yet what she had seen.

He built his monastery at Bangor on the Ulster coast — Benchor, the Height of the Ulstermen, by the eastern sea — and the monks came to it in such numbers that one place could not hold them. Three thousand of them, the Life says, were under his care across Ireland before he died, and out of Bangor went the men who carried Irish monasticism to the Continent: Molua, who founded a hundred houses, and Columbanus, who founded Luxeuil and Bobbio and lies in Italy still. Comgall was a man of wondrous abstinence — bread and water and herbs, and a midnight river he stood in to pray, where a proud young monk who tried to match him found he could bear neither the cold of the water nor the heat that came off the old man's body, and went away humbled.

His rule was so severe that on one island retreat seven of his monks died of hunger and cold, and the elders of Ireland came and begged him to soften it. He softened it — for them. For himself he would not loosen what he had taken up for God. He made each of his brethren, with his own hands, a small wooden bed to die in; he lived to be eighty-five; and he was buried at the Bangor he had raised out of a prophecy of fire.

> *He would not relax, for any man's sake, the rigour he had taken up for God's; but the rigour of the brethren he gently relaxed, that they might be kept for the service of Christ.* — from his Life
