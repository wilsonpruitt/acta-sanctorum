## October 16

# Gall of St. Gallen

*Irish hermit-monk, apostle of the Allemanni, c. 550–c. 645*

He was the youngest of the twelve Irish monks who left Bangor in Ulster around the year 590 with their abbot Columbanus to evangelize the continent. They went first to Brittany, then up the Rhone to Burgundy, then over the Alps to Lake Constance — a journey of thirteen years, through three kingdoms and four languages, planting monasteries as they went. By 612 they had reached Bregenz on the eastern shore of the lake, where Columbanus's abrasive insistence on the Irish style of penance had finally put him beyond the patience of the Frankish-Burgundian queen Brunhilde. The little community broke up. Columbanus crossed the Alps south into Lombardy and founded Bobbio. Gall, who had fallen ill with a fever and could not travel, was left behind with two Allemanic disciples and his abbot's parting rebuke — *as long as I live, you shall not say Mass* — for his refusal to follow.

He recovered slowly through the winter of 612 in a small wooden cell near Arbon. In the spring he walked east into the heavily-forested upper Steinach valley with the deacon Hiltibod, found a clear stream and a high spring above a cliff, set the foundation-stones of a chapel, and stayed for the rest of his life. The legend preserves the small details: the bear who tried to take his fire and whom he rebuked into bringing him firewood instead and who was venerated for centuries afterward in the abbey arms; the local Allemanic chieftain who gave him the surrounding woodlands when he saw the cell; the Frankish bishop of Constance who tried to make him take the see and whom he refused with a smile. He kept his abbatial silence after Columbanus's death in 615, when a Bobbio messenger arrived with the abbot's pastoral staff and the news that the rebuke had been lifted. He said his first Mass at sixty-five.

He died perhaps in 645, an old man among his Allemanic disciples, and was buried in the wooden chapel he had built. A century later the abbey of Saint Gall would rise on the spot — one of the great monastic foundations of Carolingian Europe, the model house of the Benedictine reform north of the Alps, the producer of the *Plan of St. Gall,* of the *Antiphonary,* of historians and hymn-writers, of a school whose graduates staffed the cathedrals of Cologne and Mainz and Liège for three hundred years. The valley that had been an empty forest when Gall set down his stones has been a center of Christian Europe for a thousand four hundred years.

The disobedient disciple who would not cross the Alps, who stayed where he was sick and built a hut, planted the most fruitful Irish foundation on the continent.

> *"Maneas in Christo, frater, ego in his terris peregrinabor."* — Gall to Columbanus, on parting at Bregenz: *Stay you in Christ, brother; I will pilgrim in these lands*
