## October 20

# Acca of Hexham

*Northumbrian bishop, scholar of Bede's circle, c. 660–740*

He had been raised in the household of Bishop Bosa of York, taken into the cathedral school as a child, brought along when Wilfrid — the great, contentious bishop of York whose long career filled the second half of the seventh century in Northumbria — moved his episcopal household first to Ripon, then back to York, then to Hexham on the south Tyne. Acca was Wilfrid's secretary, then his deacon, then his chief presbyter, then in 709 his successor as bishop of Hexham. He held the see for twenty-four years.

What he gave the Northumbrian church in those years, by Bede's grateful accounting, was a school and a library. The cathedral library at Hexham under Acca was the best in northern England — the great Roman manuscripts Wilfrid had brought back from his pilgrimage journeys, the Greek codices Theodore of Tarsus had passed to him through Canterbury, the patristic and historical works Acca had himself copied or imported from the continental abbeys. Bede, who lived twelve miles down the road at Jarrow, regarded Acca as a personal friend and dedicated five of his works to him: the commentaries on Mark, on Luke, on the *Hexaemeron,* on the *Song of Songs,* on the *Acts of the Apostles.* The dedicatory letters survive. They are warm, technical, full of the precise gratitude one scholar owes another who has lent him books. *Te animarum medicum nominari mihi licet,* Bede writes in the prologue to *In Marcum* — *I may be permitted to call you the physician of souls.*

Acca was also the last bishop of Hexham to govern it as a free see. In 732 the Northumbrian succession-fight that had brought King Ceolwulf to the throne caught up with him; he was deposed, perhaps for political reasons. He spent the last eight years of his life in some kind of internal exile — Bede says only that he left Hexham. He died on the twentieth of October, 740, and was brought back home for burial. The cathedral he had loved kept his bones in two carved stone crosses set up at the head and foot of his grave outside the south wall — the *crosses of Acca,* the antiquarians called them; one survives in the Hexham museum, half its eight feet of carved interlace and acanthus still legible after thirteen centuries.

The library he assembled was scattered when the Vikings burned Hexham in 875, and his books were lost; the cathedral he had governed survived the burning and was rebuilt. The man who lent Bede the books has not had to lend the church any more — what Bede made of them is what survives.
