## February 14

# Cyril

*Apostle to the Slavs, 827–869*

He was born Constantine, the youngest of seven children of a Greek naval officer at Thessalonica. The family spoke Greek at home and Slavic with the neighbors — Thessalonica in the ninth century was half-Slavic and increasingly so — and the boy picked up both with the easy ear of a child raised in a frontier town. He was brilliant. The Byzantine court took notice; by his early twenties he was teaching philosophy at the imperial academy in Constantinople and being sent on diplomatic missions to the Arabs and the Khazars. His elder brother Methodius, a more reserved man, was already a monk.

In 862 the Slavic prince Rastislav of Moravia asked the emperor Michael III for missionaries who could teach his people the faith in their own language. The brothers were sent. The first thing they did, before they left, was design an alphabet — the Glagolitic, the parent of Cyrillic — to put Slavic into writing for the first time. They translated the gospels, the psalter, and the liturgy. They preached and baptized in Moravia for four years. They ran into Frankish missionaries who insisted that the Latin Mass alone was valid, and Cyril took the question to Rome.

Pope Hadrian II received them with honor and approved the Slavonic liturgy outright. Cyril, exhausted by the journey and by the years in Moravia, did not see Moravia again. He fell ill in Rome, took the monastic habit and a new name — *Cyril* — fifty days before his death, and was buried in the basilica of San Clemente, where his tomb still is. Methodius would carry on the work for sixteen more years, imprisoned twice by Frankish bishops for his trouble.

A third of European Christianity prays in a language Cyril wrote down by hand.

> *"O Lord, who hast given me grace to preach thy gospel in the tongue of the Slavs, gather them now into thy holy Church."* — Cyril, dying in Rome
