## April 16

# Magnus of Orkney

*Earl, martyr, c. 1075–1118*

He was a Norse-Scottish earl of the Orkney islands, co-ruler with his cousin Haakon Paulsson, in the years when the islands were still Viking polity and the Christian faith was still recent enough that adult conversion was the norm. Magnus had been baptized in his teens; he had taken the gospel seriously. His Christianity made him an embarrassment to his fellow Norsemen almost from the start. On a Viking raid in Wales in 1098, while the longships were closing on a Welsh village, Magnus refused to take up arms; he sat on the deck of his own ship and sang psalms aloud while the killing was done on shore. The skalds laughed. The chroniclers kept the story.

He and Haakon governed Orkney jointly for some years, in the inherited Norse arrangement. The arrangement frayed. Haakon's faction, ambitious for sole rule, grew restless. The two earls agreed to meet on the small island of Egilsay at Easter 1118 to negotiate peace, each bringing two ships and a small escort. Magnus came with two ships. Haakon arrived with eight.

Magnus understood at once what the meeting had become. He spent the night in the small church of Egilsay in prayer. In the morning he walked out and offered Haakon three alternatives: exile in pilgrimage, imprisonment for life, or mutilation. Haakon would have accepted any of them, but his nobles, who wanted blood, demanded the killing. Haakon's standard-bearer refused to swing the axe. Haakon ordered his cook, a man named Lifolf, to do it. Magnus took Lifolf by the shoulders, told him no innocent man should kill another, asked him not to be afraid, and gave him a blessing. *Strike hard on the crown*, he said, *for it is no death for a chieftain to be cut down like a thief.* Lifolf wept and struck.

His tomb at Kirkwall on Orkney is in the great cathedral that bears his name — the largest medieval cathedral in Scotland, raised by his nephew Rögnvald a generation later in penance.
