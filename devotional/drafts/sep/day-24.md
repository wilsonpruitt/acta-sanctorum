## September 24

# Gerard of Csanád

*Venetian monk, missionary bishop, martyr of the Magyars, c. 980–1046*

He was born Gerardo Sagredo to a noble Venetian family, entered the Benedictine abbey of San Giorgio Maggiore as a boy, was elected abbot in his early thirties, and resigned the office to set out for the Holy Land. He never got there. Stopped in Hungary on the way through, he was recognized by King Stephen — who had been gathering missionary monks for the new Christian kingdom for twenty years — and persuaded to stay. Stephen made him tutor to the prince Imre, then sent him into the southern marches of the kingdom, the wild country between the Danube and the Tisza where the Magyar tribes were still half-pagan and the priests were few. Gerard fixed his see at Csanád on the Maros river, the first bishopric of southeastern Hungary, and stayed there for fifteen years.

He was a Benedictine of the strict observance: rose at midnight to chant the office, kept perpetual silence except for preaching and confession, ate one meal a day. His preaching was famous — he learned enough Magyar to preach in it — and he wrote, while bishop, a biblical commentary called the *Deliberatio supra hymnum trium puerorum,* one of the earliest surviving works of theology composed in central Europe, in which he meditated on Daniel 3 and the witness of those who would not bow to the king's image. The Bollandists print the older eleventh-century *Vita,* careful to distinguish it from a fifteenth-century interpolation that mixes legend into the simpler narrative.

When King Stephen died in 1038 the Christian settlement of Hungary nearly came apart. The pagan party rose under a chieftain named Vata; the next two kings were assassinated within four years; the missionary bishops were marked. In September 1046 Gerard was traveling toward Buda to meet the new king when his party was attacked at the ford of the Danube below Pest. He was sixty-six. The pagan warriors stoned him, broke his cross over his head, and threw him over the cliff into the river, on what is still called Gellért Hill in Budapest — the *montis Gerardi.* The body was recovered that night and taken back to Csanád, where Stephen's grandnephew Ladislaus elevated his relics in 1083 alongside Stephen and Imre. The Magyar nation he had helped baptize keeps his memory still: *Szent Gellért,* the bishop of the bridge.

> *"Suscipiam coronam, quam mihi Dominus paravit."* — *Gerard, when warned of the ambush at the Danube ford*
