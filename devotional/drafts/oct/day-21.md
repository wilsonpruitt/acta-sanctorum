## October 21

# Hilarion of Gaza

*Father of Palestinian monasticism, the Western pupil of Anthony, c. 291–371*

He was born to pagan parents in the village of Thabatha, five miles south of Gaza in the Roman province of Palestine, and was sent as a teenager to the Christian schools of Alexandria. He was fifteen when he made his way out into the desert beyond Memphis to find Anthony — already, by 306, the most famous monk in the world — and stayed with him for two months. He came home to find his parents dead. He sold the small inheritance, distributed it to the poor and to his married brother, and walked seven miles down to the wilderness behind Maiuma, the port of Gaza, on the Mediterranean shore. He was sixteen. He stayed in that wilderness, in increasing isolation, for the next sixty-five years.

The *Vita Hilarionis* by Jerome — written around 390, less than twenty years after Hilarion's death, drawn directly from the recollections of his companion Hesychius — is one of the most loved of all the early monastic biographies. It is also one of the funniest. Jerome paints a young man who lived for thirty years on twelve dried figs a day, who slept on a reed mat in a hut made of mud and straw four feet high, who broke a vase of incense over a band of bandits who had come to steal his hut and persuaded them to repent and become monks themselves, who refused every visitor who came for healing and healed them anyway, who once raced a chariot in the games at Gaza on the prayer of a Christian charioteer and beat the pagan champion with the *signum crucis* drawn over the wheels.

He was driven out of Palestine in his sixties by the crowds — he could not get any solitude — and spent the last fifteen years of his life on the run from his own fame. He went to Egypt; he was found out. He went to Sicily; he was found out. He went to Dalmatia; the demoniacs queued at the harbor with their relatives. He went finally to Cyprus. The brothers at Paphos hid him in a small cave above a stream three days' ride into the mountains. He stayed for five years, surrounded only by his old companion Hesychius, and died there at eighty years old, on the twenty-first of October, 371.

The boy who had walked seven miles down the Mediterranean coast at sixteen had founded, by living a particular sort of small life there, the entire Palestinian and Syrian monastic tradition. Sabas, Euthymius, Theodosius — the great fathers of the next century — all looked back to Hilarion as their *abba.* The fame he ran from for fifteen years has been, for sixteen hundred years, the only thing about him most readers know.

> *"O Anima mea! Egredere; quid times? Egredere, quid dubitas?"* — Hilarion's death-prayer in Jerome: *Out, my soul; why do you fear? Out, why do you hesitate?*
