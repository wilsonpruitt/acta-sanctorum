## March 24

# Catherine of Sweden

*Abbess, daughter of Bridget, 1331–1381*

She was the fourth child of Bridget of Sweden, the great Swedish mystic and prophetess, and she was raised in a household where it was assumed that piety was the family business. Bridget had borne eight children; Catherine was the daughter who would inherit her mother's mantle. She was placed at the Cistercian convent at Riseberga to be educated, and at thirteen, as her parents arranged, she was given in marriage to a German nobleman named Eggart von Kürnen who happened to be both pious and chronically ill. Catherine and Eggart agreed at the wedding to live as brother and sister. The arrangement held for seven years until Eggart's death.

In 1349 Catherine went to Rome to find her mother, who had moved there to await an audience with the pope. She found instead a city in plague, a mother she barely recognized for severity, and the rest of her own future. She stayed. For twenty-four years she travelled with Bridget — to Naples, to Cyprus, to Jerusalem, to every Italian shrine her mother's revelations sent them to — serving as the older woman's secretary, daughter, and protector. The Romans who saw them together said Catherine was as gentle as her mother was fierce.

When Bridget died at Rome in 1373, Catherine carried her body back to Sweden in a small reliquary and laid her at Vadstena, the abbey her mother had founded but had never seen completed. Catherine became abbess. For eight years she ran the new community, the first Bridgettine house, and worked through the papal courts for her mother's canonization. She lived to see the cause moving forward; she did not live to see it close. She died at Vadstena at fifty.

Bridget was canonized ten years after Catherine's death. Catherine herself was beatified by popular acclaim; the formal cause closed only in the seventeenth century. She would have minded neither delay.
