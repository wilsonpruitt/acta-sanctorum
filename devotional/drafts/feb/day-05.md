## February 5

# Agatha of Sicily

*Virgin and martyr, c. 231–251*

She was a Sicilian girl of a noble Catanian family, perhaps fifteen, when the Roman governor Quintianus took notice of her. He proposed marriage, then position, then his protection, then his anger. Agatha — already consecrated to Christ in her own intention, if not yet by veil — refused each in turn. He had her arrested under the lapsed but still-usable Decian edict against Christians, and turned her over to a woman named Aphrodisia who ran a house of ill repute. Aphrodisia tried for thirty days to break the girl by every means at her disposal. She did not break.

Quintianus reclaimed her for the tribunal and ordered a public torture. The details — given in the Acts read at her tomb a generation later — are graphic enough that the medieval iconographers would never settle for a single image: she is most often shown carrying her own severed breasts on a tray. In the cell that night, the chronicler says, an old man entered. He told her he was Peter, the apostle of Christ, and he healed the wounds with his touch. The next morning Quintianus had her rolled over hot coals. She died in the cell that evening, asking Christ to receive her.

The local memory was stubborn. Within a generation her tomb was a pilgrimage site; within a century her name was in the Roman Canon, between Lucy and Cecilia. The Catanians believe she still protects them from Etna: when the volcano rumbles, her veil — the small surviving relic of the saint — is brought out and carried in procession against the lava, and the eruptions have always stopped before reaching the city.

Whatever the Acts have added to her story over the centuries, the Catanians who keep her name on February 5 are not honoring a fiction. They are honoring a girl whose tomb their great-grandparents knelt at.

> *"Lord, who hast preserved me from infancy, take now my soul to thyself."* — Agatha, in her cell
