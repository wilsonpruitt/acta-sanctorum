## October 12

# Edwin of Northumbria

*King and martyr, the convert Paulinus baptized, c. 586–633*

He was the son of Ælle, king of Deira, the southern half of Northumbria, and grew up in exile after his father's defeat by Æthelfrith of Bernicia in the late 590s — first in the kingdom of Gwynedd among the British, then at the court of Rædwald of East Anglia, where the young exile spent his late twenties waiting for the chance to claim his inheritance. The chance came in 616 when Æthelfrith, alarmed by Rædwald's protection of his rival, marched south to demand Edwin be handed over for execution. Rædwald refused, met Æthelfrith at the river Idle in Nottinghamshire, killed him in battle, and put Edwin on the throne of a reunited Northumbria — Bernicia and Deira together, the largest English kingdom, stretching from the Humber to the Forth.

He governed for seventeen years and brought the kingdom to a peace it had not known in a generation. Bede preserves the famous detail: Edwin set up brass drinking-cups at the wayside springs along the Roman roads of Northumbria, *and no one,* the historian writes, *would dare touch them but for the use intended,* such was the order of the kingdom. In 625 he asked for the hand of Æthelburh, the Christian princess of Kent. Her brother sent her north on condition that she could keep her religion and bring her own bishop, and the bishop they sent with her was Paulinus.

It took Paulinus two years. The famous council of 627 — the great hall at Goodmanham, the speech of the unnamed counselor about the sparrow flying through the lighted hall in winter, the chief priest Coifi's own dramatic ride out to throw a spear into the pagan temple — closes the conversation. Edwin was baptized at York the next Easter, with his daughter Eanflæd and most of his court. The decade that followed was the high point of early-medieval English Christianity: a baptized king, a missionary bishop, six years of travelling the Northumbrian rivers baptizing crowds.

It ended at Hatfield Chase in October 633. Cadwallon of Gwynedd, the British king, allied with the pagan Penda of Mercia and marched against Northumbria. They met Edwin and his sons at the marsh-edge of Hatfield south of the Don. Edwin was killed in the line; his elder son Osfrid was killed beside him; the younger son was captured and murdered later by Penda. The body was decapitated, the head taken back as a trophy. A monk of Whitby — perhaps a man Paulinus had baptized — recovered the head some time later and brought it to York, where it was buried in the porch of the cathedral Edwin had begun. The relics survive nowhere now; the cult of the king who had been baptized and martyred for his baptism survived him by a thousand years.
