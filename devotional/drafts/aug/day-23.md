## August 23

# Sidonius Apollinaris

*Bishop-poet of Clermont, witness of the fall of Roman Gaul, c. 430–488*

He was born about 430 at Lyon, in the great senatorial family of the Apollinares — his grandfather had been Praetorian Prefect of Gaul, his father held the same office under Petronius Maximus. He married Papianilla, the daughter of the future Western emperor Avitus. He composed panegyrics in elegant hexameters for three successive emperors — Avitus his father-in-law, Majorian who unseated him, and Anthemius — and was made Prefect of the City of Rome in 468. He served the empire while there was an empire to serve.

He returned to Gaul in 469. The province by then was collapsing. The Visigothic king Euric had begun the systematic conquest of Aquitaine and Auvergne. The bishopric of Clermont — the great walled hill-city of central Gaul — fell vacant in 470, and the local clergy and people, in a moment of necessity, asked Sidonius to take it. He had not been ordained. He had served as a senator and a court poet, no more. He accepted on a single condition: that he be allowed to keep faith with the imperial cause. He was ordained priest, then bishop, in a single rapid succession of liturgies. He was forty.

He led the defense of Clermont against Euric's siege for four years — 471 to 475 — with his brother-in-law Ecdicius, who commanded a small private cavalry from his own estates. The bishop fasted, preached, organized supplies, set the night watches on the walls. His letters of these years to the bishops of southern Gaul are among the most remarkable in the patristic corpus — half pastoral consultation, half dispatches from the front. In 475 the emperor Julius Nepos surrendered the Auvergne to Euric by treaty without consulting Clermont. Sidonius wrote to Bishop Graecus of Marseille, who had brokered it, the line the AASS preserves: *Pugnavimus pro vobis; deserti sumus a vobis* — we fought for you, and we have been abandoned by you.

Euric had him exiled briefly, then released him after a careful Latin panegyric — the price of his return — and he resumed the bishopric. He governed twelve more years under Visigothic suzerainty, kept his Latin and his hexameters and his prayer, gave away the family treasures in the famines of the early 480s, and died at Clermont, probably on the twenty-first of August, 488. He was fifty-eight. He prayed earnestly. He studied the Scriptures with the same care he had once given Vergil. He gave away his wealth as freely as he had once given his verses.
