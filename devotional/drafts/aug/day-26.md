## August 26

# Bregwine of Canterbury

*Archbishop, eighth-century continental Saxon, d. 762*

The AASS draws his entry chiefly from the Vita written by Eadmer of Canterbury in the eleventh century — the same Eadmer who wrote the life of Anselm — about a Canterbury archbishop whom Eadmer remembered through three centuries of cathedral tradition. The historical Bregwine had been born in old continental Saxony, in the country now lying between the Weser and the Elbe, of a noble Saxon family. He crossed to England as a young man.

The crossing is itself remarkable. Bregwine moved in the opposite direction of the prevailing eighth-century missionary current. Boniface, Willibald, Lull, Wynnebald — almost every name of the missions to the continent came from the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms across the Channel into the still-pagan Saxon north. Bregwine was a young continental Saxon whose own country had not yet been Christianized, traveling the other way to be educated and trained in the English church that the earlier missionary generation had built. Eadmer's Vita preserves his arrival in unembroidered prose: *among them Bregwine, coming there, soon attaining a position, was dear to all, and made a citizen of the country.*

He took the monastic habit and served as a priest of the cathedral chapter at Canterbury under Archbishop Cuthbert through the 750s. When Cuthbert died in October of 758, the Canterbury clergy elected Bregwine to succeed him. He was consecrated on the feast of Saint Michael in 759 or 760. He governed Canterbury only three years. The chief act of his pontificate was the conclusion of a long jurisdictional dispute with the abbots of the great independent monastery of Saint Augustine, just outside the Canterbury wall, over the burial of the archbishops of Canterbury. The abbey had claimed since Augustine's foundation that every archbishop should be buried at Saint Augustine's. Bregwine ruled that the cathedral itself — Christ Church Canterbury — was the rightful resting place of the metropolitans, and arranged accordingly.

The first archbishop actually buried under that ruling was himself. He died on the 26th of August, 762, and was buried in the cathedral he had defended. He was, the chronicler adds, deeply mourned. Three years had been short for a metropolitan; the Saxon convert had been just long enough at Canterbury to make himself indispensable, and was gone before anyone could prepare for the loss.
