## October 17

# John Colobus

*John the Dwarf, abba of Scetis, c. 339–c. 409*

He was a peasant boy from the Egyptian Thebaid, four feet tall by the most generous estimate — *colobos* in the Greek, *parvus* in the Latin, the *dwarf,* the *little one,* a name that stuck for the rest of his life and a thousand years afterwards. He went out into the desert of Scetis as a teenager, around 357, and put himself under the discipline of the great old Pambo, who took him as a novice in the same little circle that included Macarius the Egyptian and Moses the Ethiopian — the founders of the form of monasticism that would shape the desert tradition for the next sixteen centuries.

The story is the famous one — repeated in the *Apophthegmata,* repeated in Cassian, repeated by every spiritual master from Benedict to Dorotheus of Gaza. Pambo took the new novice out into the dry valley below the cell, planted a dead walnut staff in the sand, and said *water this every day until it fruits.* The nearest well was three miles away. John walked with the bucket every morning before the office and every evening before sleep. He kept walking for three years. The branch budded, leafed, and fruited. The old men of Scetis, when they heard, came out and ate the walnuts and called the tree *the tree of obedience.*

He stayed in Scetis for fifty years. He was famous in the desert for the smallness of his body and for the precision of his answers in the *Apophthegmata patrum.* *Abba,* a young brother once asked him, *what should I do with this temptation?* John told him: *sit in your cell, and your cell will teach you everything.* Another brother: *what is the most necessary virtue?* He answered: *humility, because where humility is, there is no temptation that can stand against it.* Another: *I have many evil thoughts.* John told him: *the wind blows where it will; you cannot stop it; but you can keep your sails reefed.* When the desert-raiding Mazikes broke up the Scetis communities in 408, John fled to Suez with the surviving brothers and died there, perhaps in his seventy-first year, longing to return to the cell he had not been allowed to keep.

He had founded no monastery, written no book, governed no community larger than the brothers in his immediate cell. The dead branch he had watered for three years outlived him by twelve hundred years.

> *"Sit in your cell, and your cell will teach you everything."* — John Colobus, in the *Apophthegmata patrum*
