## October 28

# Simon and Jude

*Apostles, the Zealot and the brother of James, fl. c. 30–c. 70*

The synoptic gospels list them together at the end of the Twelve, the last two before Judas Iscariot. The Simon of October 28 is *Simon the Cananaean,* called *the Zealot* in Luke and Acts — the surname probably reflecting an earlier political affiliation with the militant Galilean nationalist movement that would, three decades after the Lord's death, lead the great Jewish revolt against Rome. The Jude is *Judas of James* in Luke's list, *Thaddaeus* in Mark and Matthew — a younger brother of the apostle James the Less, perhaps a cousin of the Lord through the family of Mary of Clopas, perhaps the author of the short canonical letter that bears his name.

The gospels say almost nothing of either of them apart from the name in the list. John preserves one line of Jude in the long discourse at the Last Supper: *Lord, how is it that thou wilt manifest thyself unto us, and not unto the world?* — a question that draws from the Lord one of the most searching answers of the upper room. *If a man love me, he will keep my words: and my Father will love him, and we will come unto him, and make our abode with him.* Of Simon the Cananaean John records nothing. They are, in the gospel narrative, members of a quiet majority — apostles who walked with the Lord for three years, ate with him at the supper, ran from the garden, gathered in the upper room, received the Spirit at Pentecost, scattered into the world to preach.

The early traditions have them work first separately and then together — Simon in Egypt and the Maghreb, Jude in Edessa and Mesopotamia, both of them eventually in Persia, where they joined forces and converted the eastern Persian *magi* before being killed together at the city of Suanir, perhaps in the early 70s. Jude was clubbed; Simon was sawn in two. The legend gives them their iconographic attributes — the saw and the club, on every Western altarpiece since the Middle Ages — but their joint feast on the twenty-eighth of October goes back to the seventh century in Rome and the fourth in the East, and rests on the older and more durable tradition that they died together for the gospel they had together believed.

The two apostles whose names are barely on the page held to that gospel through a generation longer than most. The Lord's quiet promise to Jude in the upper room — *we will come unto him and make our abode with him* — was the promise he is now remembered to have lived inside.

> *"Lord, how is it that thou wilt manifest thyself unto us, and not unto the world?"* — Jude, in the upper room, *John* 14:22
