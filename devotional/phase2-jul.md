## July 1

# Julius of Caerleon

*British martyr, c. 304*

The AASS Julii I lists him at the head of its day-one calendar: *S. Julius, Martyr in Britannia.* The Bollandists accepted Julius as a substantive entry but deferred the often-paired Aaron of Caerleon to other treatment. This entry follows their judgment and gives Julius alone, with the testimony of Gildas and Bede — the two oldest extant British sources — on which the AASS itself relies.

He was a Roman citizen of *Isca Silurum*, the walled administrative center of Roman Britain west of the Severn, the place now called Caerleon on the river Usk in south Wales. He was a Christian of standing in the town — not a soldier of the legion (which had been moved out a generation before), but a citizen of the *colonia*, with the property and legal protections that name implied. He had been baptized for some years by the time the Diocletianic edict of 303 reached the western provinces.

The edict ordered the Christians of the empire to offer the imperial sacrifice. The western Caesar Constantius Chlorus enforced it with notable mildness — he confiscated church buildings; he is not known to have demanded a single death — but any local magistrate who chose to enforce it could. The magistrate at *Isca* did. Gildas, writing in the sixth century, preserves the single sentence: Alban at Verulamium two hundred miles east, Aaron and Julius at Caerleon. The three names are the entire memory British history kept of the persecution. All three were beheaded outside the town walls, on the slope above the river, in the year 304 or thereabouts.

Bede copies Gildas in the first book of his *Ecclesiastical History*, and adds nothing the British chronicler could not see. The British church kept Julius's memory through the Saxon conquest, the Welsh withdrawal into the western country, and the centuries of broken communion with Rome. When Augustine of Canterbury arrived in 597, the British bishops he met at *Augustine's Oak* on the Welsh marches were Julius's far heirs. A small parish church on the slope above the Usk at Caerleon still bears his name. The AASS, having so little to print, gave him the chair-place in the calendar of July.

> *"Aaron and Julius, citizens of the City of the Legions, who in this same persecution endured most noble martyrdom."* — Bede, *Ecclesiastical History* I.7, AASS Julii I

## July 2

# Otto of Bamberg

*Apostle of Pomerania, c. 1060–1139*

He was born in Swabia of a minor noble family, educated at a cathedral school of the upper Rhine, and went to Poland in his twenties as chaplain in the household of the Duchess Judith of Swabia. He spent a decade at Krakow, learned Slavic well enough to preach in it, and built such a reputation for administrative competence and pastoral gentleness that Henry IV of Germany asked for him as chancellor. He came to the imperial court in 1101, served through the worst years of the Investiture Controversy, refused to side openly with either his emperor or his pope, and was nominated to the see of Bamberg in 1102. He was reluctant — he held that the king should not nominate bishops — and accepted only after going to Rome on foot, submitting himself to Pope Paschal II, and receiving his pallium directly from the apostolic see.

He governed Bamberg thirty-six years. He rebuilt the cathedral after a fire, founded twenty monasteries, and hosted Bernard of Clairvaux during the schismatic conflict of 1135. The work for which the AASS remembers him on July 2 was different. In 1124, when he was sixty-four, the Polish duke Bolesław III asked him to lead a mission to the still-pagan Pomeranians of the Baltic coast, whom an earlier Spanish missionary had failed to convert. Otto agreed.

He went twice, the second time in 1128. He went in episcopal vestments, with a train of seventy persons, carrying gold plate and rich vestments — having concluded, from the failure of his predecessor, that the Pomeranians would not respect a missionary who arrived as a beggar. The strategy worked. He baptized perhaps twenty thousand persons in two summers, founded eleven churches between the Oder and the Persante, and trained a body of native Pomeranian-speaking priests before he left. He returned to Bamberg and governed eleven more years.

He died at Bamberg, June 30, 1139, in his eightieth year, and was buried in the cathedral he had rebuilt. The AASS day moves him to July 2 to keep clear of the Visitation in the late medieval calendar. The Pomeranian church he founded was destroyed by the Reformation. The Polish coast still calls him *Apostol Pomorza* — the apostle of Pomerania.

> *"It is not the bare foot but the present heart that converts a pagan."* — Otto, on his mission strategy

## July 3

# Leo II, Pope

*Sicilian-born pontiff and ratifier of Constantinople III, d. 683*

He was born in Sicily, of a notary family of Aidone in the Greek-speaking interior of the island, in the years when Sicily was the breadbasket of the Eastern Empire and the great refuge of Greek monks fleeing the Arab conquests of the East. He was bilingual in Latin and Greek. He was, the chronicler in the *Liber Pontificalis* says, *eruditus in cantilena*, deeply learned in chant — a singer of remarkable skill who could improvise the modes of the Roman office and the Eastern liturgies alike. He was elected pope on the death of Agatho in January 681, but the imperial confirmation from Constantinople took eighteen months to arrive, and he was not consecrated until August 682. He governed only eleven months before he died.

His pontificate was short and substantive. He ratified the acts of the Sixth Ecumenical Council, the Council of Constantinople III, which had condemned the Monothelite heresy at the end of 681 — the heresy that Christ had only one will. The Latin reception of those acts was Leo's principal work. He sent the council's decrees to the bishops of Spain, to the king of the Visigoths, to the patriarchs of Antioch and Alexandria. He had to deal personally with the difficult question of his predecessor Honorius I, who had been anathematized at the council for negligently entertaining the Monothelite formula in a private letter. Leo's letter to the Spanish bishops on the matter is careful, learned, and pastoral: Honorius is condemned not for heresy but *quia non, ut decuit, apostolicam praedicationem confirmavit* — because he did not, as he should have, confirm the apostolic preaching with his own teaching. The distinction has lasted.

He composed several hymns for the Roman Office. He revised the chant of certain antiphons of the Holy Week. He kept up the Sicilian habit of personal almsgiving — the Lateran register notes weekly distributions to the Greek monasteries of Rome that he had instituted in his short tenure. He died June 28, 683, having held the see less than a year. The Roman calendar moved his feast to July 3 — the AASS day — to leave clear the apostles' feast days of late June. He had been pope long enough to settle the Latin reception of an ecumenical council, and not long enough for anything else.

> *"Not for heresy, but because he did not confirm the apostolic preaching."* — Leo, of his predecessor Honorius

## July 4

# Elizabeth of Portugal

*Queen and peacemaker, 1271–1336*

She was a daughter of Peter III of Aragon and Constance of Sicily, and great-niece of Elizabeth of Hungary — for whom she was named — whose habit of giving away her dinner she imitated from the age of five. She was married at twelve to King Denis of Portugal in a match arranged five years earlier. She bore two children. Denis was a competent king and a notoriously unfaithful husband, fathering perhaps nine illegitimate sons during their marriage. She raised them, all of them, in her household alongside her own two, on the theory that they were not their fathers' faults.

She kept three rules. She fasted on every Wednesday and Friday for fifty years. She rose at dawn for Mass and then walked, in plain clothes, to visit the sick — of Lisbon, and after the court moved, of Coimbra — for an hour each morning. And she made it her work to keep the peace of the Iberian peninsula. The chroniclers preserve the famous moment of 1323, when her own son, the Infante Afonso, raised a rebel army against his father Denis on the plain of Alvalade. The two armies stood three hundred yards apart in battle order. Elizabeth, in her late fifties, rode out alone on a mule between the lines and stood there until both her husband and her son dismounted and walked to her. The peace was signed at her feet.

She intervened similarly between Castile and Aragon, between Aragon and Portugal, between Granada and Castile — eight times in fifty years, by the chronicler's count. She is the patron of mediators for that reason. Denis died in 1325 and she put on the habit of the Third Order of Saint Francis the following day, retired to a small house she had built beside the convent of the Poor Clares at Coimbra, and lived eleven more years as a Franciscan tertiary in everything but final vows.

She made one last journey at sixty-five — to Estremoz, to negotiate again between her son Afonso IV of Portugal and her grandson Alfonso XI of Castile. She arrived in the high heat of July, prevailed on the truce, and collapsed of fever. She died at Estremoz on the Fourth of July, 1336, the truce holding.

> *"If I am the mother of one and the grandmother of the other, between which of them shall I stand if not in the middle?"* — Elizabeth, before riding out at Alvalade

## July 5

# Athanasius of Athos

*Founder of the Great Lavra, c. 925–1003*

He was born at Trebizond on the Black Sea, baptized Abraamios, and orphaned in his early childhood. He was raised by a kinswoman who was a nun, sent to Constantinople in his late teens, and rose through the imperial schools to the rank of teacher of grammar. He was, the chronicler says, an unusually large and physically strong man of conspicuous gentleness, sought out by more students than he could accommodate. He was thirty when he renounced the academic career, fled an imperial summons that would have made him a court official, and made his way to the hermit Michael Maleinos on Mount Kyminas in Bithynia. He took the habit and the name Athanasius.

He spent four years there. He met on Kyminas the young general Nikephoros Phokas — Michael's nephew — who was about to become emperor and who took a liking to him. When Athanasius, fleeing the visibility of the connection, slipped away to Mount Athos in 957, the imperial money followed. Nikephoros, on becoming emperor in 963, sent him a substantial grant of gold for the foundation of a monastery on the holy mountain. Athanasius, who had wanted to be a hermit, accepted reluctantly and built. The *Megisti Lavra* — the Great Lavra — went up over the next decade on a wooded headland at the south-eastern tip of the peninsula. It was the first formally organized coenobitic monastery on Athos; the older monks, accustomed to a looser eremitic pattern, fought him over its rule for years.

He outlasted the fight. He gave Athos a typikon — based on the cenobitic tradition of Theodore Stoudite — that the other houses gradually adopted. The Great Lavra grew to seven hundred monks in his lifetime. He had been abbot a quarter-century when, in 1003, he climbed the scaffolding of a new church his community was building, to inspect the apse vaulting himself. The vault collapsed under him and six masons. Five of the masons died on the spot; he survived three days under the rubble while the brothers dug him out, and died in his cell with the last rites. He was seventy-eight.

Athos has been the chief school of Eastern Orthodox monasticism ever since. Every monastery on the mountain — twenty of them today — keeps his typikon in some form.

> *"What I have learned alone, I will teach in community."* — Athanasius, on accepting Nikephoros's grant

## July 6

# Sexburga of Ely

*Queen of Kent, abbess, d. 699*

She was the eldest daughter of Anna, king of the East Angles, and the older sister of Etheldreda (June 23 in this book). She was given in marriage at perhaps eighteen to King Earconberht of Kent, the great-grandson of Æthelberht whom Augustine had baptized at Thanet a century before. The marriage was happy. Earconberht was the first English king to issue a written edict ordering the destruction of pagan idols and the keeping of the Lenten fast throughout his kingdom; Sexburga, the chronicler says, was the partner in every part of that policy. They reigned twenty-four years together. She bore four children: two kings (Egbert and Hlothere), and two queens (Eormenhild of Mercia, and Earcongota who became a nun at Faremoutiers in Gaul).

Earconberht died in the plague of 664. She governed Kent for her young son Egbert as regent for the next eight years, and during the regency founded the abbey of Minster-in-Sheppey on the island of Sheppey in the Thames estuary — a double monastery on the Faremoutiers pattern, with seventy nuns. When her son came of age in 672 she resigned the regency on the spot, walked out of the palace at Canterbury, and entered Minster-in-Sheppey as a simple nun. She kept the rule of her own house under the abbess she had appointed, for seven years.

Her sister Etheldreda died at Ely in 679 (June 23). The community at Ely elected Sexburga abbess in her place. She left Sheppey to her daughter Eormenhild (newly widowed of the Mercian king Wulfhere) and walked north through the fen country to Ely. She governed the double monastery there twenty years, until her own death. Her great work as abbess at Ely was the translation of her sister Etheldreda's body in 695 — sixteen years after the death — into the Roman sarcophagus that had floated up out of the river. The body, the *Vita* says, was incorrupt, the wound on the neck healed; Sexburga washed it and reclothed it with her own hands and laid it under the high altar.

She died at Ely, July 6, 699. Her daughter Eormenhild succeeded her as abbess; her granddaughter Werburga succeeded Eormenhild. Three generations of Ely abbesses came down from the same Kentish royal household — a quiet matriarchal line in the early English church.

> *"What we have done for our sister we will do for one another, and what we have done for one another we will do for the Lord."* — Sexburga, at the translation of Etheldreda

## July 7

# Willibald of Eichstätt

*West Saxon bishop and pilgrim, c. 700–787*

He was a Wessex boy of a noble family, kin to Boniface of Mainz, given as a child of three to the abbey of Bishop's Waltham in Hampshire after a near-fatal illness. He took the habit at twenty. In his early twenties he persuaded his father Richard and his brother Wynnebald to set out with him on the long pilgrimage East. They left Hamble in 720 and walked across France in winter. The father died at Lucca in northern Italy and was buried in the church there. Willibald and his brother went on to Rome, prayed at the apostles' tombs, and then — alone of the Anglo-Saxon pilgrims of that decade — kept going east.

He spent seven years in the Holy Land. He sailed from Naples to Sicily, from Sicily to Ephesus, from Cyprus to Tartus on the Syrian coast. He was arrested at Emesa as a suspected Frankish spy and held for some weeks until a Spanish Muslim merchant who had known his face in Naples vouched for him. He walked to Damascus, to Nazareth, to Capernaum, to the Mount of Olives. He drank from Jacob's well at Sychar. He bought a small amount of frankincense in the Bethlehem market and smuggled it back to Europe in a hollowed gourd through the customs at Tyre — knowing, the chronicler says, that there was no customs duty on a gourd full of nothing. The *Hodoeporicon* in which his cousin Hugeburc of Heidenheim recorded these journeys is the first travel narrative by an Englishman to survive.

He returned through Constantinople and paused two years at Monte Cassino before Boniface called him on to Germany. Boniface ordained him priest in 740 and consecrated him bishop of the new see of Eichstätt in Bavaria the following year. He governed Eichstätt forty-five years. He founded the double monastery of Heidenheim, where his brother Wynnebald and his sister Walpurga ran it as abbot and abbess; he organized the parish system of his territory; he survived the death of Boniface in 754 and the upheavals of Pippin's accession.

He died at Eichstätt, July 7, 787, having outlived almost everyone he had set out with. The frankincense, the chronicler says, lasted his whole life.

> *"It is light to carry what is bought for thanksgiving."* — Willibald, on his gourd

## July 8

# Kilian of Würzburg

*Irish missionary bishop, d. 689*

He was an Irish monk of Mullagh in the kingdom of Connacht, of a noble family of the Uí Maine. Ordained by the local bishop in his thirties, he felt himself called — like Columbanus before him — to the missionary work the Irish church had made its peculiar vocation in the seventh century. He left Ireland with eleven companions about 686, walked across Britain to the Channel, sailed to the Frankish coast, and at last arrived at the duchy of the East Franks in what is now northern Bavaria. Eight of his companions had stayed behind along the way to found small churches; he came to the ducal seat at Würzburg with three: the priests Colman and Totnan, and one unnamed deacon lost to the record.

The duke Gozbert was a pagan, but tolerant. He let them preach. They preached for two years. Gozbert was eventually baptized with his household, and a sizable portion of the East Franks of Würzburg with him. Kilian then went south to Rome on foot, was confirmed by Pope Conon, was consecrated bishop of Würzburg with apostolic authority, and walked back. The mission seemed to have succeeded.

It collapsed over a marriage. Gozbert had married, while still pagan, his brother's widow Geilana — a permitted union under Frankish custom but forbidden under canon law. Kilian, as the new bishop, told the duke that the marriage would have to be regularized: Geilana was to retire to a convent and the duke was to take a lawful wife. Gozbert agreed. He was away on a campaign when Geilana found out. She bribed two of the duke's retainers. They came to the missionaries' lodging at night, on July 8 of 689, found Kilian, Colman, and Totnan at the Office at midnight, killed all three with swords, and buried the bodies in the dunghill behind the house with the chronicles, the books, and the sacred vessels. The duke returned. Geilana, the *Vita* says, went mad within months and could not eat. The bodies were found within the year, exhumed, and translated to the cathedral. Kilian's skull, still bearing the sword-cut, is in the Würzburg cathedral treasury.

The Franks of Würzburg held to their baptism. The diocese has kept his feast on July 8 since the eighth century.

> *"What is binding before God, the bishop must say, whether the duke hears or not."* — Kilian, to Gozbert

## July 9

# Anatolia and Audax

*Roman virgin and her converter, c. 250*

She was a young Roman of senatorial family, with a sister named Victoria, both betrothed in late adolescence to pagan suitors in the years of the Decian persecution. The two girls had been baptized as children — their father, the chronicler says, was a quiet Christian sympathizer of the household of the Anicii — but had until then lived as conforming members of pagan Roman society. The Decian edict of 250 brought the question to a point. They could not marry the men they were promised to without offering the imperial sacrifice, which the edict now required. They could not refuse the sacrifice without breaking the marriages and exposing themselves to denunciation. They took counsel together and refused.

The two suitors went different ways. Victoria's was, in time, persuaded by her arguments and her example; he repudiated the marriage gently and walked away. Anatolia's, named Aurelius, went the other way. He had her arrested. He denounced her to the imperial vicar of Picenum, where the family had estates, on the charges of *vesania* — irrational impiety — and of disturbing the household gods. She was held under house arrest at the family villa in the hills above Thora — modern Tora in the Lazio Apennines — for some months, and visited there by the local pagan priests and physicians, who pronounced her insane. Aurelius eventually had her transferred to the custody of a soldier named Audax, with instructions to break her down or kill her.

Audax was an officer of mixed pagan-Mithraic background, the second-grade *Miles* of the Mithras initiations. He arrived at the villa, the *Vita* says, intending to do the job. He spent three days alone with her in the locked room. He came out a Christian. He had asked for baptism from her own hand and she had baptized him with water from the household basin. He went straight to the vicar and reported that he had been converted by his prisoner and could not carry out the order. The vicar had them both beheaded in the courtyard of the villa on the morning of July 9, perhaps 250 or 251. Local Christians, the chronicler says, buried them under the floor of the villa chapel that night. The shrine at Thora kept their bones; the AASS Julii II preserves their *Acts* in the careful sixth-century recension that has them.

> *"He came to break me; I broke nothing in him but his old self."* — Anatolia, in the recension of her *Acts*

## July 10

# Rufina and Secunda

*Roman martyr-sisters, c. 257*

They were the daughters of a senator named Asterius and his wife Aurelia, raised on a family villa on the Via Cornelia ten miles north-west of the city of Rome — the same road, the *Acts* note in passing, on which an emperor would later build the basilica of Saint Peter. Their parents were Christians. The girls were baptized as infants and catechized by a priest named Eugenius who came out from the city every fortnight. Rufina was the elder; she was perhaps eighteen and Secunda perhaps sixteen when their father betrothed them, in a single double-engagement, to two young Roman noblemen named Armentarius and Verinus.

The persecution of Valerian opened in the summer of 257. The fiancés, after a few weeks of weighing the change in their fortunes, apostatized. They came to the villa one afternoon and asked the girls — first quietly, then less quietly — to follow them in the apostasy. Both girls refused. The two men went straight to the imperial prefect Junius Donatus and denounced them. Soldiers came that night to the villa, arrested both girls, and brought them under guard back into the city.

The chronicler's *Acts* preserve the hearing in the prefect's court. Rufina answered the first questions. The prefect, taking her for the bolder, ordered her flogged. Secunda, watching, interrupted: *Why dost thou honor my sister and dishonor me? Are we not children of the same Father? Did we not drink the same milk?* The prefect, the *Acts* say, was nonplussed; he ordered both girls flogged together. They were then locked in the prison of the Mamertine for some days, brought out for a second hearing, threatened with the brothel and with the wild beasts in turn. Both refused both. They were taken in a cart on the Via Cornelia, beyond the ninth mile, into the forest called *Silva Nigra*. The soldiers beheaded them at a clearing later called *ad Lucum* — *at the wood*. A Christian noblewoman named Plautilla, with property nearby, buried them on her own land the next morning.

The shrine at *ad Lucum* — later the Casale del Sasso — was a stop on the pilgrim itineraries through the eighth century. Pope Anastasius IV moved their bones to the basilica of Santa Rufina in Trastevere in the twelfth. They are still there.

> *"Are we not children of the same Father? Did we not drink the same milk?"* — Secunda, in the prefect's court

## July 11

# Pius I, Pope

*Roman pontiff of the second century, d. c. 155*

He was the brother of Hermas — the same Hermas who wrote the *Shepherd*, the long allegorical work that nearly entered the New Testament canon and was read aloud in many churches for the next three centuries as if it had. The *Muratorian Fragment*, the oldest surviving list of accepted Christian writings, gives the relation plainly: the *Shepherd* was recently written in our times in the city of Rome by Hermas, while his brother Pius held the chair of the Roman church. The two brothers, the same fragment says, had been slaves of a Christian household and had been freed.

He governed the Roman church for perhaps fifteen years, from about 140 to 155, in the long peace of the reigns of Antoninus Pius and the early years of Marcus Aurelius. He had to deal with the heretic Marcion, who arrived in Rome from Sinope in Pontus during his pontificate, made a substantial donation to the Roman church, taught his pruned gospel of an unknown alien God for several years in the city, and was at last excommunicated by Pius in 144. The donation was returned. He had also to deal with the gnostic Valentinus, then teaching in Rome and gathering a following that would not formally break with the church for another fifty years. He kept them just within the bounds in his lifetime.

Of his own writing little survives. A single pastoral letter, on the keeping of Easter, addressed to the bishop of Vienne in Gaul, is preserved in the *Liber Pontificalis*. A baptismal formula, perhaps composed under him, may underlie the Apostles' Creed in its earliest Roman form. He founded, the chronicler says, two of the *tituli* of the city: *titulus Pudentis* on the Esquiline (later Santa Pudenziana) and *titulus Praxedis* (later Santa Prassede), both on land donated by Christian families — the Pudens family whose name Paul mentions in second Timothy, and the family of Praxedes (July 21 in this book).

He died, the tradition says, a martyr, about 155, though the manner is not preserved. He had governed the Roman church through the years its earliest creeds and its earliest property-bases took shape. He was the brother who had stayed at the chair while Hermas had written.

> *"What we have received, the same we hand on; nothing of our own; nothing of any man's; only of the Lord."* — Pius, in his letter to Vienne

## July 12

# John Gualbert

*Founder of the Vallombrosans, c. 985–1073*

He was the eldest son of a Florentine knight named Gualbertus, of the small *cattani* nobility of the contado, raised in the usual way of his class to ride, fight, and revenge. His younger brother Hugh was murdered, probably about 1010, in a street feud with another Florentine knight named Anselm. John spent the next several years looking for Anselm. He found him at last, alone and on horseback, on a narrow road outside Florence on a Good Friday afternoon. He drew his sword. Anselm, who had no escape, dismounted and knelt in the road. He spread his arms, the chronicler says, in the form of a cross, and asked John in the name of the day to spare him.

John sat his horse a long moment. He sheathed the sword, dismounted, embraced Anselm in the dust of the road, and rode on to the abbey of San Miniato al Monte on the hill above Florence. He tied his horse at the gate, walked in alone, and prostrated himself before the great Romanesque crucifix that still hangs there. The crucifix, the chronicler says, inclined its head to him in answer. He never went home. He cut off his own long noble's hair with a knife at the steps of the altar, asked for the habit, and was received as a novice.

He spent seven years at San Miniato. He left when the abbot was succeeded by a simoniac whose appointment had been bought, and he refused to accept the new abbot's rule. He went north with two companions, into the wooded valley of *Vallis Umbrosa* — the shadowed valley — in the Apennines above Reggello, and founded a hermitage there about 1036. Disciples came. The Vallombrosan order grew, with him as its first abbot, into a dozen houses across Tuscany in his lifetime. He kept the Benedictine rule but pushed it toward stricter observance: longer night office, more silence, no meat, no eggs.

His public work was the war against simony — the buying and selling of church offices that the Gregorian reform was just beginning to confront. He preached against it through northern Italy for four decades. He encouraged his monks, in 1063, to put the Florentine bishop Peter Mezzabarba to the *ordeal of fire*: the monk Peter Igneus walked, the chronicler says, unharmed through a fire to demonstrate that the bishop's office had been bought. The bishop was deposed. John died at his abbey of San Salvi outside Florence, July 12, 1073.

> *"I forgave him in the road; I will forgive no buyer of the church in the cathedral."* — John, on simony

## July 13

# Henry II, Emperor

*Saxon king, restorer of Bamberg, 973–1024*

He was the great-grandson of Henry the Fowler and the son of Henry the Quarrelsome, duke of Bavaria — a man who had nearly been emperor himself and had taught the boy the duchy's politics with a hard hand. The boy was given to the cathedral school of Hildesheim at seven, then to Regensburg under Bishop Wolfgang, who saw the makings of a churchman in him. Henry would have preferred the church. His father's death in 995 made him duke of Bavaria at twenty-two, against his will. The early death of the emperor Otto III in 1002 made him king of the Germans, against most expectations, after a hard election against three other claimants.

He married, the same year, Cunigunde of Luxembourg, by every account a marriage of mutual affection that produced no children. They were buried side by side at Bamberg eighty years later. Later medieval hagiography embellished the childlessness into a deliberate vow of celibacy; the historical truth is probably more modest, and the couple themselves never said.

He was crowned emperor by Pope Benedict VIII in 1014. He governed twenty-two years. He founded the see of Bamberg in 1007 on land of his own ducal patrimony, as a missionary base for the Slavs of the upper Main and a model of reformed canon-law observance for the German episcopate. He convened the Synod of Pavia in 1022, which legislated against simony and clerical marriage thirty years before the Gregorian reform got going. He paid for the rebuilding of the cathedrals of Bamberg, Strasbourg, Magdeburg, and Merseburg out of imperial revenue. He fought three campaigns against Boleslaw I of Poland and one against the Byzantines in southern Italy, with mixed military success. He failed to reconcile the Cluniac and Gorzean reform parties in the empire's monasteries, but he kept both in funds.

He died, slowly of stones in the kidneys, at his palace at Grone near Göttingen, on July 13, 1024, leaving Cunigunde to retire to a convent. The Bavarian and Swabian dukes elected Conrad II to succeed him. Bamberg held his bones; the chapel he had founded over them, with the high relief of the imperial pair, is the centerpiece of the cathedral he had built.

> *"What I give to Bamberg, I do not lose; I send ahead."* — Henry, on the foundation of Bamberg

## July 14

# Bonaventure

*Franciscan doctor, minister general, c. 1217–1274*

He was born Giovanni di Fidanza at Bagnoregio in the southern Tuscan hills, near Viterbo. He was four years old, lying nearly dead of fever in his mother's arms, when she carried him through the mountains to Assisi to ask the blessing of the old Francis. Francis, the chronicler says, laid hands on the boy and pronounced him healed and said *O buona ventura* — *what good fortune.* The mother, on the way home, began calling him *Bonaventura*, and the new name stuck.

He went to Paris to study at fourteen and never returned to live in Italy for any length of time. He took the Franciscan habit at the Paris convent in 1243, at twenty-six, against the express preference of his family, who had been hoping the *good fortune* of his health would carry into a more secular career. He studied under Alexander of Hales, who is said to have remarked that in Bonaventure *it seemed Adam had not sinned.* He was made master of theology at the University of Paris in 1257 on the same day as his friend Thomas Aquinas. He had already been elected, three months earlier and without his knowledge, minister general of the Franciscans, at thirty-nine.

He governed the order seventeen years through the worst of its first generational crisis — the long fight between the Conventuals, who wanted regular convents and stable libraries, and the Spirituals, who wanted to keep Francis's literal poverty. He took a middle path neither party loved at the time and both grew to defend afterwards. He rewrote the official *Legenda* of Francis. He kept the order out of schism. He wrote the *Itinerarium Mentis in Deum* — the *Journey of the Mind to God* — on Monte La Verna in 1259, on the spot where Francis had received the stigmata, in six days and six chapters that have not stopped being read.

He was made cardinal bishop of Albano by Gregory X in 1273 and took a substantive role in preparing the Second Council of Lyons of 1274, which briefly reunited the Greek and Latin churches. He died at Lyons during the council, July 15, 1274, of an illness contracted on the journey. The Greek and Latin patriarchs concelebrated his Requiem. The AASS day was set on July 14 to leave the council's commemoration clear.

> *"What I have I owe to a woman walking a sick child to Assisi."* — Bonaventure, of his mother

## July 15

# David of Munktorp

*English Cluniac, missionary to Sweden, c. 1020–1082*

He was an English monk of the abbey of Cluny's daughter house at Lewes in Sussex, of a Saxon family in the South Downs displaced (by the chronicler's hint) in the Norman settlement after 1066. He had taken the habit at Cluny itself in his twenties, returned to England to help found Lewes, and was nearly fifty when the call came that he had been quietly waiting for. The kingdom of Sweden, the youngest Christian kingdom of Western Europe, had been formally baptized for a generation but remained spiritually thin: the old gods kept their temples in the back country, the clergy were too few, and the martyr-bishop Sigfrid had recently died with much of the mission still uncompleted. King Inge the Elder of Sweden sent a request to Cluny for English-speaking monks who could preach in a Scandinavian dialect close enough to their own. The Anglo-Saxon north and the Scandinavian south were still mutually intelligible at the parish level.

David volunteered. He was perhaps fifty. He sailed in 1070 with three brother monks for the Swedish coast, landed at Sigtuna near the present Stockholm, and walked sixty miles inland into the Bergslagen country — the wooded mining region of central Sweden — where the conversion was thinnest. He founded a small monastic chapel at Munktorp on the lake of Mälaren and spent the next twelve years preaching out from it through the lake villages and the miners' camps.

He gathered a circle of native Swedish priests whom he trained himself, in Latin he made them learn slowly. He baptized adults at the lake in summer and in the heated meeting-houses in winter. He refused, as his rule, ever to baptize a child whose parents did not catechumenate first themselves; the Scandinavian custom of infant baptism following the head-of-house had, he believed, half-produced the present thinness of the faith. He buried his three companions one by one as the years went on. He outlived them by a decade.

He died at Munktorp, July 15, 1082, having governed his small Swedish mission twelve years and seen — in his own count, made in a letter back to Lewes — perhaps three thousand adults baptized as catechumens. The Cluniac chronicle remembers his name; the parish at Munktorp still does. He is the chief western-monastic figure of the founding generation of Christianity in Sweden, less famous than Sigfrid but at least as historical.

> *"The font is patient. Let the family come together to it."* — David, on his baptismal rule

## July 16

# Our Lady of Mount Carmel

*The hermits on Elijah's mountain, twelfth century*

The mountain stands at the northern edge of the Mediterranean coast of Palestine, a ridge of green wooded limestone running fifteen miles into the sea at Haifa, with a long spine of caves and springs along its western face. Elijah had hidden in its caves nine centuries before Christ during the famine of King Ahab, and at its summit had held the contest with the prophets of Baal. The chronicler of the Wadi 'ain-es-Siah, the central canyon of the ridge, says that from the third century onward Greek hermits had inhabited the caves intermittently, taking the prophet for their patron, and that there was a small chapel by a spring deep in the wadi from at least the fifth century — Greek-speaking, irregular, never quite extinct.

The Latin Carmelites trace their order to a recovery of that hermitage in the years just after the First Crusade. Western pilgrims who had stayed on after the conquest of Jerusalem in 1099 began to gather at the caves about 1150 under the guidance of a Calabrian Greek pilgrim named Berthold. They lived in separate cells around the spring of Elijah, came together for the Office and the Eucharist at the chapel, and took as their rule a short formula of nine articles which the patriarch of Jerusalem, Albert of Vercelli, codified for them in 1209 — the *Rule of Saint Albert*, still the basic text of the Carmelite order. They wore a striped mantle of brown and white. They took as their patron the *Mother of God of Mount Carmel*, the small icon of the Virgin and Child that the chapel by the spring had held for centuries.

The Saracen reconquest of the coast in 1291 drove the Western Carmelites off the mountain. They scattered to Cyprus, Sicily, Provence, England, and the Low Countries — small communities of refugees, each carrying a copy of the rule and an image of Our Lady of the mountain. The order, in exile, transformed itself in three generations from an eremitic into a mendicant order on the Dominican-Franciscan pattern. It kept the patroness.

The feast on July 16 is the day, by tradition, when Simon Stock — English general of the order in 1251, at a moment of nearly fatal crisis — received the *Brown Scapular* from the hands of the Virgin in a vision, with a promise of her protection of all who wear it in faith. The mountain itself is held today by a re-established Carmel: the *Stella Maris* monastery, built in 1836, looks out over Haifa harbor toward the sea Elijah saw the small cloud rising from.

> *"And he said to his servant, Go up now, look toward the sea."* — *I Kings* 18:43, on Mount Carmel

## July 17

# Marcellina

*Consecrated virgin, sister of Ambrose, c. 327–398*

She was the eldest child of Aurelius Ambrosius, prefect of the Gauls, born at Trier where her father held office. Her mother — a Christian Roman noblewoman whose name is not preserved — bore three children in five years: Marcellina, then Satyrus, then Ambrose, who would become bishop of Milan and doctor of the Latin church. The father died in Gaul when Marcellina was perhaps seven or eight; the mother brought the three children back to Rome and raised them in the family's house near the Lateran. Marcellina was the eldest, and by her brother's own later testimony the spiritual parent of the household: it was her piety, her quiet practice of the Hours, her almsgiving to the Roman poor, that her two younger brothers learned the faith from.

She made her own decision early. On Christmas of 353 — she was perhaps twenty-five — she received the veil of a consecrated virgin from Pope Liberius at Saint Peter's, in a public ceremony that included a sermon by the pope on the dignity of the virginal life. Ambrose, then a boy of about thirteen, was present in the basilica. He would remember the day fifteen years later, when as bishop of Milan he wrote his treatise *De Virginibus* and dedicated it to Marcellina — a four-book work in epistolary form, addressed to her as the sister he had first learned the gospel from. The treatise contained the famous narrative of the martyr Agnes, which Ambrose had heard repeated in their childhood from their mother.

Marcellina lived the rest of her life in the family house, with one other consecrated virgin for companion, on a strict rule of her own composition: one meal a day, the seven offices, manual work at flax, no visits beyond the church and the houses of the poor. She kept this for forty-five years. When Ambrose was made bishop of Milan in 374 she did not move; her mother was already dead, and she would not, she said, displace the prayer-life she had built. She corresponded with him and visited rarely. He came to her at her last illness in Rome in 398 and was present at her death, July 17 of that year, less than six months before his own. She had been his first teacher and was, by his own count, the longer-faithful Christian of the two.

> *"What I am, I learned from her; what she was, she learned in silence."* — Ambrose, of Marcellina

## July 18

# Frederick of Utrecht

*Bishop of the Frisians, d. 838*

He was a Frisian by birth, born in the country between the Rhine and the Ems, of a noble household that had been Christian for two generations since the work of Willibrord. He went to the cathedral school of Utrecht as a boy, was ordained priest in his twenties, and served in the diocese under Bishop Ricfried for a decade before being elected to succeed him in 825. The diocese was a difficult one. The northern coast of Frisia — the islands and the salt-marshes between the Zuiderzee and the Weser — was nominally Christian and substantively pagan. The Norse raids on the coast had begun in the previous decade and were rising in frequency. The emperor Louis the Pious, son of Charlemagne, was an actively pious man whose personal life was in turmoil and whose household contained, by Frederick's own difficult judgment, an irregular marriage.

He worked steadily at the pagan north. He sent priests into the islands of Walcheren and Wieringen, where the Norse cult of Wodan still had open temples, with instructions to live in the villages and learn the dialect before preaching. He kept a careful catechumenate at Utrecht for adult converts from the back country. He wrote to Hrabanus Maurus at Fulda asking for copies of the *De Institutione Clericorum*, which he used himself for the formation of his Frisian clergy.

His difficulty was with the imperial household. The emperor's second marriage — to the Bavarian noblewoman Judith — was canonically irregular by Frederick's reading of the western canon law (Judith was related to Louis within the prohibited degrees). The empress, it was said, also kept lovers. Frederick wrote a private letter to the emperor, then a public remonstrance. The emperor responded mildly; the empress less so. The chronicler of Utrecht, writing within a generation, says plainly that Judith ordered Frederick's death.

He was struck down at the altar of the cathedral of Saint Martin at Maastricht on July 18, 838, just after concluding the prayer after communion in the dawn Mass. Two soldiers approached him from the side of the altar, drew swords, and cut him down. He had begun to sing the psalm-verse *Laudabo Dominum in vita mea* — *I will praise the Lord in my life* — and finished it on the floor, the chronicler says, *psallam Deo meo quamdiu fuero* — *I will sing to my God as long as I am.* The two soldiers escaped. Judith was never charged. The Frisian church buried him at Utrecht.

> *"I will praise the Lord in my life; I will sing to my God as long as I am."* — Frederick, dying, *Psalm* 145:2

## July 19

# Macrina the Younger

*Ascetic, sister of Basil and Gregory, c. 327–379*

She was the eldest of the ten children of Basil the Elder and Emmelia of Caesarea — a Cappadocian Christian household of the second rank of the senatorial aristocracy, with estates at Annisa in Pontus and connections through both grandmothers to the confessors of the Diocletianic persecution. She was named Macrina after her own grandmother, who had been a pupil of Gregory the Wonderworker and who had taught her the *Symbol* of the Cappadocian church before she could read. She was betrothed at twelve to a young man of the family circle who died before the marriage. She refused, at thirteen, to be betrothed again. She told her mother, the chronicler her brother Gregory of Nyssa says in the *Life of Macrina* he wrote about her, that the first betrothed was *not dead but present with the Lord*, and that a second engagement would be a kind of infidelity.

She kept the household. Her father died about 341. Her mother was left with ten children, four of them still small. Macrina, who was perhaps fourteen, took over the practical management of the estates while her mother retained the legal headship. She raised her own brothers and sisters: Basil (later the Great), Naucratius (a hunter and ascetic who died young), Peter (later bishop of Sebaste), Gregory (later bishop of Nyssa), and the others. She taught them the psalter and the New Testament before they were sent out to the city schools. When Basil came back from Athens at twenty-six full of his own brilliance, Gregory says, it was Macrina who took him down from his self-importance in a single afternoon and pointed him toward the monastic life.

She made the family estate at Annisa into a coenobitic community of about forty women — most of them former slaves and freedwomen of the household, freed by Macrina under a rule of common life. She herself worked at the same labors. She was, by Gregory's testimony, the chief theological mind of her own household, more decisive than Basil and more contemplative than Gregory; the dialogue Gregory wrote at her deathbed, *On the Soul and the Resurrection*, gives her speeches that he heard from her with his own ears.

She died at Annisa on July 19, 379, of a long wasting illness. Gregory had come from Nyssa to find her on a wooden plank in the small cell she had built for herself in the women's house. He buried her, the *Life* ends, in the grave her parents had been buried in, and her bones lie still in the Pontic earth.

> *"O Lord, thou hast loosed the dread of death from me, and made the end of this life the beginning of life."* — Macrina, dying, recorded by Gregory of Nyssa

## July 20

# Elijah the Prophet

*Tishbite of Gilead, c. 870 B.C.*

He came up out of the eastern hill country, out of Gilead beyond Jordan, with no genealogy and no priestly office, and stood unannounced before Ahab king of Israel in the third year of his reign and said, *As the Lord God of Israel liveth, before whom I stand, there shall be neither dew nor rain these years but according to my word.* The drought lasted three and a half years. He spent it hidden, first at the brook Cherith where ravens fed him, then with the widow at Zarephath whose handful of meal in the barrel and little oil in the cruse did not fail through the famine. He raised her son from death in the upper chamber. He went down to Mount Carmel in the third year and called the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal to meet him there before all Israel.

He had two bullocks brought, gave them their choice, and let them prepare an altar. They cut their own flesh from morning to noon, calling on Baal in vain. He mocked them: *Cry aloud, for he is a god — peradventure he sleepeth, and must be awaked.* In the evening he rebuilt the broken altar of the Lord, dug a trench around it, drenched the sacrifice and the wood with twelve jars of water, and called: *Lord God of Abraham, Isaac, and of Israel, let it be known this day that thou art God, and that I am thy servant.* The fire of the Lord fell, and consumed the sacrifice, and the wood, and the stones, and the dust, and licked up the water that was in the trench. The people fell on their faces, and said, *The Lord, he is the God.*

He fled afterward into the wilderness, sat down under a juniper tree, asked to die. The angel fed him bread baked on coals and water in a cruse, and he walked forty days and forty nights on the strength of that meal to Horeb the mount of God. He stood in the cave there while a great wind broke the mountain — but the Lord was not in the wind; and after the wind an earthquake — but the Lord was not in the earthquake; and after the earthquake a fire — but the Lord was not in the fire; and after the fire *a still small voice*, and Elijah wrapped his face in his mantle, and the voice asked, *What doest thou here, Elijah?*

He passed his mantle to Elisha at the plowing. He went up by a whirlwind into heaven from the far side of Jordan with horses and a chariot of fire. The mantle fell. The Carmelites have him for their father. The fire on Carmel still falls, somewhere, on every soul that has stopped halting between two opinions.

> *"How long halt ye between two opinions? If the Lord be God, follow him."* — Elijah, *I Kings* 18:21

## July 21

# Praxedes of Rome

*Roman virgin and gatherer of martyrs, second century*

She was a daughter of the senator Pudens, of the family the apostle Paul names in the closing chapter of the second letter to Timothy: *Eubulus greeteth thee, and Pudens, and Linus, and Claudia, and all the brethren.* Pudens, by the early Roman tradition, was a Christian senator baptized by Peter in his own house on the Vicus Patricius on the Esquiline hill. He had two daughters, Pudentiana and Praxedes, both consecrated virgins, both eponyms of two of the oldest Roman *tituli* — the small house-churches that were the earliest organized places of Christian worship in the city. The two basilicas — Santa Pudenziana and Santa Prassede — still stand on the Esquiline a quarter-mile apart.

She lived through the persecutions of the second century. The chronicler's *Acts*, in the careful Bollandist recension at AASS Julii V, give the work that her name was remembered for. With her elder sister Pudentiana while the sister lived, and alone after Pudentiana's early death, Praxedes used the family house on the Vicus Patricius as a refuge for the persecuted, a place of secret Eucharist, and a hospice for the bodies of the martyrs. When a Christian was killed in the games or in the prisons or on the Via Cornelia, she and her household servants would by night recover the body, wash it, anoint it, and lay it in a private cemetery she had opened on the family land outside the Salarian gate. She is said by the *Acts* to have gathered, in this way, the bodies of twenty-three named martyrs and many more unnamed.

She kept also a particular relic in the chapel of the house: a marble basin into which she would sponge, after each of these night-recoveries, the blood from the linens used to wash the bodies. The basin survives. It is set in the floor of the side chapel of the *Cappella di San Zenone* at Santa Prassede on the Esquiline, behind a small bronze grate, and the inscription beside it gives Praxedes's name and the second-century date.

She died in her old age, in the family house, of natural causes, sometime in the reign of Antoninus Pius, perhaps about 160. She had outlived the persecutions of her early womanhood and the kinder middle decades both. They buried her with her father and her sister in the same family vault. Pope Paschal I, in 822, brought her bones into the new basilica on the Esquiline that bears her name. The two thousand three hundred martyrs whose names the basilica's slab inscription preserves are, by Paschal's record, the count of those she had once gathered.

> *"What you do to the least of these, you do to me — even after they are dead."* — Praxedes, written on the wall of her chapel

## July 22

# Mary Magdalene

*Apostle to the apostles, first century*

She came from the lakeside fishing town of Magdala on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee, twelve miles north of Tiberias. Luke says she had been delivered, at some point before her appearance in the Gospel narrative, of seven demons. She had means; she traveled in the company of Jesus and the Twelve and helped support them, with Joanna the wife of Chuza Herod's steward, and Susanna, and several other women, out of their own resources. She is named, in this group, before the others — the way the Twelve are named with Peter first. She had a place she had earned.

She was at the cross when nearly all of the Twelve had fled. John was there; the mother of Jesus was there; Mary the wife of Cleopas was there; Mary Magdalene was there. She watched the dying. She watched the lifting down. She watched, with the other Mary, where the body was laid. She came back at first light on the first day of the week — earlier than first light, John says, while it was yet dark — to anoint the body for a proper burial that the Sabbath had cut short. She found the stone rolled away.

She ran back to Peter and John. She came back with them. They saw the linens; they went home. She stayed in the garden. She was weeping at the empty tomb when she turned and saw a man she took for the gardener, and asked him where they had laid the body. He said her name. *Mary.* She said the one word back: *Rabbouni* — *my master.* He told her he had not yet ascended to the Father, and sent her to the eleven with the message. She went. She told them. They did not, at first, believe her. The Greek of John has her speak the words that the church for two thousand years has heard her say in the dawn of every Easter morning: *I have seen the Lord.*

She is, in the early Latin tradition that Gregory the Great followed and the AASS Julii V keeps, the same woman as the sister of Lazarus and Martha, the same woman as the unnamed sinner who anointed Jesus' feet. The Greek tradition holds these three distinct. The point of her feast does not turn on the question. The point is that the resurrection was first announced not by an apostle but by a woman whom the apostles had not yet learned to believe; and that the western church gave her at last the title that the Greeks had given her first: *Apostola Apostolorum* — apostle to the apostles.

> *"I have seen the Lord."* — Mary Magdalene, in the garden, *John* 20:18

## July 23

# Bridget of Sweden

*Visionary and founder, 1303–1373*

She was Birgitta Birgersdotter, of the great Swedish noble house of Folkunga, daughter of a *lagman* of Uppland who governed his province with the law-codes the Folkungas had drafted. She was given in marriage at thirteen to the noble Ulf Gudmarsson and bore him eight children over twenty-two years. Two of those children — Karl and Catherine — would become important saints in their own right. The marriage was, by every account, deeply affectionate. She had begun receiving visions of Christ on the cross from the age of seven; she told only her confessor; she ran the great Folkunga estate, governed her household, raised her children, and kept the visions to herself for forty years.

She was lady-in-waiting at the Swedish court under King Magnus Eriksson and his wife Blanche of Namur for several years in her thirties. The court was, by the *Vita* her confessor wrote, careless; she withdrew when she could. Her husband Ulf was struck down by a fever in 1344 returning from a pilgrimage they had made together to Compostella, and she was widowed at forty-one. She put on the Cistercian habit of a tertiary, gave away her wardrobe and her jewels, retired to a small house at Alvastra near the lake of Vättern, and for two years sat in the chapel of the Cistercian abbey there transcribing the visions that had now begun to come almost daily.

The Christ of her visions told her to found a new order — for women primarily, with a small attached community of priests for the women's spiritual care — and to take the rule to Rome. She walked to Rome in 1349 by way of Cologne and the Alps and never returned. She lived in Rome for the remaining twenty-four years of her life, in a small house near the Campo dei Fiori that still stands. She wrote, through her two Swedish chaplains who took the dictation in Latin from her Swedish, the eight books of the *Revelations* — visions of Christ's nativity, his passion, his bride the church, the popes of her century at Avignon (whom she scolded for not returning to Rome), the kings of Europe (whom she scolded for the war between England and France), and the angels and saints of her own household. She founded the *Order of the Most Holy Savior* — the *Bridgettines* — and saw, before she died, her first daughter Catherine professed in it.

She died at her house in Rome, July 23, 1373, at seventy. Her daughter Catherine carried her body home to Sweden by sea and overland; the journey took a year. They buried her at Vadstena where the mother-house of the order had been chartered. The order is still there.

> *"My daughter, the world is not deceived by the great; it is deceived by the small that the great refuse to see."* — Bridget, *Revelations* IV.49

## July 24

# Christina the Astonishing

*Beguine ascetic of Brabant, 1150–1224*

She was born of a peasant family in the small Brabantine village of Brustem near Sint-Truiden, in the Low Country borderlands between French and Flemish speech. She was orphaned of both parents in her early teens and raised by her two older sisters. She was twenty-two when, during a long fever, she fell into a coma so deep that her sisters and the parish priest declared her dead, and she was carried in an open coffin to the parish church for the Requiem Mass. At the *Agnus Dei*, the chronicler Thomas of Cantimpré reports, she sat upright in the coffin, then rose, walked to the rafters of the church on a current of air the gathered crowd could see, and perched there until the priest persuaded her to come down.

She lived another fifty-two years. The astonishing things began at once. She could not bear the smell of human bodies — not from disgust, she told her confessor, but from a sense of their mortality so acute that ordinary proximity to the unbaptized hurt her. She fled crowds into the wilderness for years at a time, lived in trees, slept in the icy waters of the river Meuse in midwinter, climbed into the ovens of the village bakers, threw herself into great wheels of running mills (the bones, the chronicler says, would dislocate but never break). She begged her food at peasant doors. She wore the same single coarse habit until it rotted off her.

She was, by Thomas's account, in the right mind throughout. Her conversations with him in her old age were lucid, theologically careful, and orthodox in every particular. She gave him an account of what had happened at the moment of her coma-death twenty-five years before: she had been carried by angels to a glimpse of purgatory, then of hell, then of the threshold of paradise, where Christ had given her the choice of remaining or returning. She had returned, she said, by her own choice, to bear in her body the suffering that would relieve souls in purgatory — and to be a sign, to the living, of what the dead were undergoing. The Beguines of the Low Countries — the loose semi-monastic communities of laywomen who clustered along the Meuse and the Rhine in this same generation — took her as one of their patron-figures, although she herself never formally joined any community.

She died at the priory of Saint Catherine outside Sint-Truiden, July 24, 1224, in her cell. The local Cistercians and Beguines together buried her. She is one of the strangest figures in the AASS. Thomas of Cantimpré, who was no fool, vouches for everything he reports.

> *"The dead do not lack our love; they lack our visibility."* — Christina, to Thomas of Cantimpré

## July 25

# James the Greater

*Apostle, son of Zebedee, d. c. 44*

He was the older of the two sons of Zebedee the Galilean fisherman, brother of John the beloved disciple, partner with Peter and Andrew in the fishing-boats of Capernaum. The four had a shared business on the lake — Mark calls them *hired servants* of Zebedee, which implies a household of some local prosperity, not the wealth of Magdala but enough to own boats and nets and to employ outside labor. James and John were called by Jesus together, leaving the father in the boat with the hired men. Their mother — Salome by the Gospel tradition, sometimes identified by the early Fathers as a sister of the Virgin — followed Jesus also, and stood by the cross. James and John were nicknamed by Jesus *Boanerges*, *the sons of thunder*, for their evident temperaments.

He was one of the three. Peter, James, and John were taken aside at the raising of Jairus's daughter, at the transfiguration on the mountain, at the agony in Gethsemane. He had asked, with his brother, for the seats on the right and the left of the kingdom and had been gently corrected: *Are ye able to drink of the cup that I shall drink of?* He had said *We are able*. He was the first of the Twelve to drink it. Herod Agrippa I, the grandson of Herod the Great, ruling the reconstituted kingdom of Judaea under imperial favor from 41 to 44, killed James with the sword in the Jerusalem persecution of 43 or 44 — the persecution Luke records in the twelfth chapter of Acts in a single sentence: *He killed James the brother of John with the sword.*

He was the first of the apostles to die. The Greek tradition records that the soldier whose duty it was to execute him, watching his composure on the way to the block, was himself converted on the spot, declared his faith in Christ before the court, and was beheaded with him in the same hour.

The Spanish tradition — preserved in the seventh-century *Breviarium Apostolorum* and elaborated through the centuries — places his earlier apostolic mission in Spain, and the translation of his body, by sea after his Jerusalem execution, to the western coast of Galicia, where it was eventually rediscovered in the ninth century by the hermit Pelayo near a place called Libredon. The shrine there became Santiago de Compostela. Whether or not the journey to Spain is historical, the pilgrimage to his shrine is — by the late medieval centuries one of the three great pilgrimages of Western Christendom, along with Rome and Jerusalem, and walked still, by perhaps three hundred thousand a year on the *Camino*.

> *"We are able."* — James and John, *Mark* 10:39

## July 26

# Anne

*Mother of the Virgin, first century B.C.*

She is not in the canonical Gospels. Her name appears first in the *Protoevangelium of James* — a Greek text of the mid-second century, of unknown provenance, which the church afterward read as a useful supplement to the Gospels for what it told of Mary's parentage and childhood, but which it never received into the New Testament canon. The *Protoevangelium* names her Anna (Hebrew *Hannah*, the same as the mother of Samuel, *grace*), and gives her husband as Joachim. They were, the text says, of the line of David, lived in Jerusalem, and were childless after twenty years of marriage. Joachim's offering at the Temple was once refused by the priest Issachar on the grounds that he had not raised children for Israel. He went into the wilderness for forty days in protest and prayer; she stayed in the garden of their house, also fasting. An angel came to each of them with the same message at the same hour: that Anna would conceive and bear a daughter who would be holy to the Lord.

She conceived. She bore a daughter and named her Miriam — Mary. She kept her at home for three years, then by an old Israelite custom of the temple-girls she presented the child at the sanctuary, where Mary remained, in the formula of the text, *until she was twelve*. Anne and Joachim, then in their late middle age, lived to see her betrothed to Joseph the carpenter. The *Protoevangelium* does not record their deaths. The tradition that grew up around their cult gives them respectful old age and burial in Jerusalem; their tomb was venerated in the church of Saint Anne in the Bezetha quarter from the fourth century, and the Crusader-period basilica of Saint Anne still stands there, perhaps the most acoustically perfect church in Jerusalem.

Whether or not the *Protoevangelium*'s narrative is historical — and the AASS, with the careful Bollandist instinct, treats it as pious supplement rather than testimony — the cult of Anne is one of the oldest in the church. Constantinople kept her feast from the sixth century; Naples from the eighth; the Latin West from the twelfth. The grandmother of Christ was, in the popular medieval imagination, the patroness of mothers, of housewives, of childbirth, of the teaching of girls to read. The standard medieval icon shows her teaching the young Mary the alphabet from a book of psalms — the psalter, by the chronicler's gentle joke, that her namesake Hannah composed.

> *"And Mary grew, and her parents adored the Lord; and they brought her to the temple of the Lord, that she might be brought up there in holiness."* — *Protoevangelium of James* 6

## July 27

# Pantaleon

*Court physician, healer, martyr at Nicomedia, c. 275–305*

The Greeks called him *Panteleemon,* the *all-merciful* — a name, the AASS preserves from the older *acta,* given him in a vision shortly before his death, replacing the older *Pantoleon,* the *all-lion,* under which his early life is recorded. He was born at Nicomedia, the Bithynian capital that had become Diocletian's eastern court-city, perhaps around 275. His father Eustorgius was a wealthy pagan senator; his mother Eubula was a secret Christian who died when he was young. The father, careful for his only son's career, had him trained from boyhood in medicine under the most distinguished court physician in the Diocletianic establishment. By his early twenties Pantaleon was a successful physician with a growing private practice and a court appointment as one of the personal medical attendants of the emperor.

His conversion came through a presbyter named Hermolaus, an old Christian whom he met by chance in a colonnade near his lodgings. Hermolaus took him through the gospels over a series of meetings; Pantaleon was baptized. The first miracle the *acta* preserve is the small one that confirmed his vocation. He was walking home through the city one afternoon when he came upon a child who had been bitten by a viper and was already dead; he stopped, knelt, prayed in the name of Christ, and the child rose. His medical practice took on, after that, what the *acta* call *the second medicine* — the curing by prayer of conditions the medical art could not reach. He kept treating his pagan patients with the standard art; the Christian patients who came to him he treated with prayer; both kinds were healed. He refused payment for the prayers, distributed the regular fees to the poor, and used his court connections to intercede for arrested Christians in the prison of the imperial quarter.

The other court physicians, jealous of his practice and frightened by the boldness of his Christian profession, denounced him to Maximian Galerius in 304. Galerius, who knew him personally, tried at first to talk him out of it. Pantaleon refused. The interrogation grew formal; the standard sequence of tortures followed. The *acta* preserve the long catalogue: he was scourged, sewn into a sack with stones and thrown into the sea (he floated; the sailors fished him out); thrown to wild animals (they refused him); set in a furnace (the fire would not burn him); set on a bench to be torn in half by an iron wheel (the wheel broke). Eventually the executioner was instructed to behead him with the sword. He was bound to an olive tree outside the city walls. The headsman struck. *Lac et sanguis* — milk and blood — flowed from the severed neck, and the olive tree, dry that summer in the late August heat, sprouted leaves and fruit overnight.

He is among the *megalo-martyrs* of the East — paired with George, Demetrius, and Theodore in the iconography of the great soldier-saints — and is the patron of physicians, midwives, and the helping professions across both Eastern and Western Christianity.

> *"Lac et sanguis."* — *the* acta *of the morning at the olive tree: from the headless body, milk and blood*

## July 28

# Samson of Dol

*Welsh-British abbot, apostle of Brittany, c. 485–565*

He was born in south Wales, of a noble Demetian family of the kingdom of Dyfed, son of a man named Amwn Ddu and a woman named Anna. He was given as a boy of five to the great monastic school of Illtud at Llantwit Major on the Vale of Glamorgan — the school that in his generation educated nearly every famous British saint of the sixth century: Gildas, David of Wales, Paul Aurelian, Magloire. He stayed twenty-five years. He took the habit in his teens and was eventually ordained deacon and then priest by Bishop Dubricius of Llandaff.

He left Llantwit in his thirties under circumstances the *Vita* records honestly: his sanctity had been so noticeable that his fellow monks had begun pressing for his election as abbot in place of Illtud, and Samson, refusing the place and the rivalry it would create, slipped out one night with three companions and walked west into the Pembrokeshire coast country. He spent some years at a cave hermitage on Caldy Island; he was ordained bishop, unconsecrated to any particular see but for missionary use, by Bishop Dubricius about 521. He crossed to Ireland for a year of further study with the Irish anchorites. He came back through Cornwall and stopped a while at the monastery of Saint Petroc at Padstow, where the two abbots — Petroc the older, Samson the younger — kept the cell together for some months.

He crossed to Brittany about 545. He landed at Saint-Malo-de-Beignon on the south coast, walked inland through the country of the Bretons, and founded the great monastery of Dol on the north coast, on land donated by a Frankish noble named Pro. Dol became the chief Breton see. He governed it twenty years. He sent missions into the still-pagan country of the Léon and into the Channel Islands. He attended the Council of Paris of 557 as the Breton episcopal representative and signed the acts in Latin under his own name. His was, for the next four centuries, the great founding signature of the Breton church.

He died at Dol, July 28, 565. He had founded perhaps a dozen monastic houses in Wales, Cornwall, the Channel Islands, and Brittany. The seven *founding saints of Brittany* — Samson, Paul Aurelian, Brieuc, Tugdual, Malo, Patern, and Corentin — are kept together in the Breton liturgical calendar with him at their head.

> *"What I have not done in my country, I will do in another country; for the gospel does not stop at the water."* — Samson, on crossing to Brittany

## July 29

# Martha of Bethany

*Sister of Lazarus, hostess of Christ, first century*

She kept house. She had a sister Mary and a brother Lazarus and a house in the village of Bethany on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives, fifteen furlongs from Jerusalem — a comfortable walk in the cool of the evening, far enough out of the city to be private but close enough to be present whenever the pilgrim feasts brought Galilean rabbis to the capital. The Gospel of John says Jesus loved them — *Iesus autem diligebat Martham et sororem eius Mariam et Lazarum* — and the village house in Bethany seems to have been, in the last year of his earthly ministry, the closest thing he had to a household of his own near Jerusalem.

Luke gives the famous afternoon. He came in with the disciples. Mary sat at his feet to listen. Martha was preparing the meal — *anxious and troubled about many things*, the Greek has — and finally went into the room and said it: *Lord, dost thou not care that my sister hath left me to serve alone?* He answered her gently. *Martha, Martha, thou art careful and troubled about many things; but one thing is needful, and Mary hath chosen that good part.* The exchange is sometimes read as a rebuke. The early commentators read it as a kindness: he had named her twice, by her own name, and had not told her to stop cooking, only to remember that the cooking was not the one thing. She did not, by the chronicler's careful inference, take it as rebuke. She did not stop serving him afterward.

John gives the harder afternoon. Their brother Lazarus had died, and the sisters had sent for Jesus, and Jesus had delayed two days. When he at last came up the road to Bethany, Martha went out to meet him — Mary stayed in the house — and said, *Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died.* She did not stop there. *But I know, that even now, whatsoever thou wilt ask of God, God will give it thee.* He told her he was the resurrection and the life. He asked her, *Believest thou this?* She gave the confession that Peter had given at Caesarea Philippi, in almost the same words: *Yea, Lord; I have believed that thou art the Christ, the Son of God, which should come into the world.* It is the only Johannine confession of the divinity of Christ delivered by a woman, and it is delivered before the raising of Lazarus, not after — by faith, not by sight.

She kept the house. She fed him the meal six days before the Passion when Mary anointed his feet with the spikenard. The Provençal tradition records that after the resurrection the three siblings landed by boat on the southern coast of Gaul, and that Martha founded a Christian community at Tarascon on the Rhône and died there in old age. The AASS prints both the *Vita* and the cautious Bollandist note that the journey is not historically attested.

> *"Yea, Lord, I have believed that thou art the Christ."* — Martha, *John* 11:27

## July 30

# Peter Chrysologus

*Archbishop of Ravenna, the *Doctor of Homilies*, c. 380–450*

He was born at Imola — *Forum Cornelii* — in the Romagna, of a Roman family of modest senatorial standing, perhaps about 380. He was educated locally, ordained deacon by Bishop Cornelius, and was serving as Cornelius's archdeacon when, in 433, on the death of the archbishop of Ravenna, the local clergy elected a candidate of their own. The bishops of the province appealed to Rome. Pope Sixtus III, the *Liber Pontificalis* says, had a dream the night before the candidate was presented: the apostle Peter and the martyr Apollinaris stood on either side of a young deacon from Imola, indicating him as the man. Sixtus refused the Ravennese choice and consecrated Peter archbishop in his place. He was about fifty.

He governed seventeen years. Ravenna was then the imperial capital of the West. The empress Galla Placidia kept her court there, the boy emperor Valentinian III was raised there, Honorius died there. The court attended his cathedral. He preached at the imperial Masses, the imperial baptisms, the imperial Christmases, with sermons short, vivid, packed with rapid imagery, and tightly biblical, that earned him within his lifetime the name *Chrysologus* — *the golden-worded* — paired with the Eastern *Chrysostom*. About 180 of his sermons survive, never more than ten minutes of speaking each: the conviction of a teacher who had measured how long a tired Roman court will listen and decided to take only the time he could hold.

He wrote the famous letter to Eutyches in 449, when the heresiarch had appealed to him from Constantinople for support against Flavian. Peter answered with a single sentence: *In all things, brother, we exhort you reverently to attend to the things written by the most blessed pope of the city of Rome; for blessed Peter, who lives and presides in his own see, gives the truth of faith to those who seek it.* It is the firmest fifth-century Western statement of the apostolic authority of the Roman see, written by a Western archbishop to an Eastern heretic. Eutyches did not take the counsel.

He returned, in old age, to Imola for what he sensed would be his last journey. He died there at the cathedral of his early diaconate, July 30, 450, just before the *Tome* of Leo and the convening of Chalcedon — neither of which he lived to see, but which his own letter had already, in substance, prepared.

> *"Blessed Peter, who lives and presides in his own see, gives the truth of faith to those who seek it."* — Peter Chrysologus, to Eutyches, 449

## July 31

# Ignatius of Loyola

*Soldier, founder of the Society of Jesus, 1491–1556*

He was Íñigo López de Loyola, youngest of thirteen children of a minor Basque noble family, raised on a small castle-tower above Azpeitia. He was a page at the court of the treasurer of Castile, then a soldier in his twenties, ambitious, vain about his appearance and his swordsmanship, with two arrests for street brawling and an open litigation against an ecclesiastical court for tonsure-clipping a rival. He was thirty when, defending Pamplona against a French army in May 1521, a cannonball passed between his legs and shattered the right one. He was carried home to Loyola on a stretcher.

He spent six months in bed with the leg, then six more after the bone-setters re-broke it because it had healed crooked. He asked for books. The castle had two: the *Legenda Aurea* lives of the saints and a Spanish translation of Ludolph of Saxony's *Life of Christ*. He read both. He noticed that when he daydreamed about returning to the wars and his lady, he was elated while daydreaming and dry afterward; when he daydreamed about walking barefoot to Jerusalem like the saints he had been reading, he was elated while daydreaming and sweet afterward. He paid careful attention to the difference. It is the seed of the *Discernment of Spirits* he would later codify.

He left Loyola in spring 1522, hung his sword at the Black Madonna of Montserrat, and spent a year in a cave above Manresa in vigils, hunger, and the working-out of what would become the *Spiritual Exercises*. He walked to Jerusalem and back. He went to school in Spain at thirty-three to learn the Latin he had skipped as a boy, was twice arrested by the Inquisition for preaching without orders, and at last took his MA at Paris at forty-three. He gathered six companions there — Francis Xavier, Peter Faber, Diego Laínez, and three others. They walked together to Rome. Pope Paul III approved the Society of Jesus in 1540.

He governed the Society sixteen years from a small room in Rome on the Via degli Astalli — three windows, a writing table, a kneeling-stool. He sent Xavier to India and Japan, Faber to Germany, missionaries to Ethiopia, Brazil, the Philippines. He left, at his death, a Society of more than a thousand in twelve provinces across four continents. He died at Rome, July 31, 1556, of a long stomach illness; the brothers had not expected the death that morning, and he received no last rites — only a single word repeated as he could, *Jesús*.

> *"Take, Lord, and receive all my liberty, my memory, my understanding, and my entire will."* — Ignatius, *Suscipe* prayer, *Spiritual Exercises*
