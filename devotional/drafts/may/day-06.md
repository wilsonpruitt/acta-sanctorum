## May 6

# Petronax of Monte Cassino

*Abbot, c. 670–c. 747*

He was a pilgrim from Brescia, in his forties, on his way through Rome to visit the great Italian shrines. Pope Gregory II received him in audience and asked him, somewhat off-handedly, whether he had ever thought of going on to Monte Cassino. The pope's question was almost a joke. Monte Cassino had been destroyed by Lombard raiders in 590, abandoned ever since, the body of its founder Benedict left in the ruins, the buildings collapsed under one hundred and thirty years of weather. There was nothing at Cassino to visit — and that, the pope suggested, was rather the point.

Petronax went. He climbed the road up the rock — the Lombards held the surrounding country, the path had not been kept up for a century, the chapel of Benedict's tomb was a roofless ruin — and found that he was not alone. A handful of hermits were living in the caves around the mountain, scattered, with no Rule, knowing only that the place had once been holy. Petronax gathered them. He patched the chapel roof. He brought up other men over the next several years. By 720 there was a working monastery on the rock again.

Pope Gregory II, when the news came down to him, gave Petronax the title *Father of the Monastery* in a formal letter. The Rule of Saint Benedict had begun to be read again at the place where Benedict had written it. Sturm, the future founder of Fulda, came to study under Petronax. Willibald, returning from the Holy Land, stayed years. Carloman, brother of Pepin the Short, abdicated the Frankish throne and entered the abbey as a novice under Petronax in his last years.

Petronax died at perhaps seventy-seven, having restored not a building but a tradition. The mountain has been a working monastery, with interruptions, ever since.

> *He was admonished to seek out the fortress of Cassino, and the monastery of Benedict, which for so many years had remained destroyed, by his own zeal to strive to restore.* — Leo of Ostia, on Petronax's charge from Gregory II
