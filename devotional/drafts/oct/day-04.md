## October 4

# Francis of Assisi

*Founder of the Friars Minor, the Poor One, 1181–1226*

He was the son of a cloth merchant in Assisi, a vain and charming youth who spent his teens in extravagant suppers and his early twenties at war — first against Perugia, where he was captured and held for a year, then in arms again, then a long illness, then the sudden mid-riding return from a campaign he could no longer make himself care about. He was twenty-three when he kissed the leper outside the gates of Assisi and felt, he would say afterwards, *what had been bitter become sweet.* He was twenty-four when he heard the painted crucifix at San Damiano speak: *Francis, repair my house, which as you see is falling into ruins.* He took the words at their plainest, sold his father's bolts of cloth, and started to rebuild the chapel with his own hands. His father took him to court; he stripped naked in the bishop's presence and gave back the clothes; the bishop covered him with his own cloak.

The first companions came in 1208. By 1209 there were eleven of them, and Innocent III approved their first rule. The order grew with a speed neither Francis nor anyone else had foreseen. By 1217 there were five thousand brothers; by 1219 Francis was in Egypt with the Fifth Crusade, walking unarmed across no man's land to preach to the Sultan al-Kamil, who heard him for several days, refused to convert, but gave him safe passage out. He returned to find an order he could no longer govern, resigned the leadership, withdrew into the Umbrian hills.

In September 1224 on Mount La Verna, after forty days of fasting, he received in his own hands and feet and side the wounds of the Christ he had been preaching for sixteen years — the first stigmatic in church history, attested by the brothers who washed and dressed him in his last two years. He died at the Portiuncula on the evening of the third of October, 1226, lying naked on the bare ground at his own request, singing the *Canticle of the Sun,* in his forty-fifth year.

Three biographies were written within a generation. Most of what is in them, Thomas of Celano says, *I heard from his own mouth, or from faithful and tested witnesses.* The witness is what the church has loved. A man who took a verse at its plainest, who kissed what he had recoiled from, who heard a painted Christ ask for his house and gave it.
