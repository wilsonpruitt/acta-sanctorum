## March 14

# Matilda of Saxony

*Queen, c. 895–968*

She was raised at the Saxon abbey of Herford, where her grandmother had placed her, and where she learned to read and to keep her hours and to expect a quiet life. The arrangement was disrupted when Henry the Fowler — Duke of Saxony, soon to be king of East Francia — saw her and asked for her hand. She was sixteen. The marriage was happy. She bore five children, the eldest of whom would become Otto I, the second of whom would be the duke of Bavaria, the third of whom — Bruno — would be archbishop of Cologne.

She gave away too much. Henry tolerated her almsgiving with affection; her sons, after Henry's death in 936, did not. Otto and Henry decided their mother had emptied the royal treasury for the poor and accused her of mismanagement. They stripped her of her dower lands and confined her, for a time, in a country house. She did not protest. She wept in private and gave the courtiers no satisfaction. Within a year the brothers had reconciled with her, weeping themselves; their wives had refused to entertain them so long as their mother was disgraced.

She lived another thirty-two years as queen dowager. She founded the abbeys at Quedlinburg, Nordhausen, and Pöhlde — Quedlinburg, where she retired and where her granddaughter Mathilde would later be abbess, is the one that survived the centuries. She died there at seventy-three, having outlived two of her five children, three of her sisters, and most of the courtiers who had known her as a young queen.

She had governed without title, by being unmovable. Her household had been the Saxon court, the Saxon poor, and the Saxon convents in equal share.

> *"What I have given is not lost; only what I have kept is in danger."* — Matilda, on her almsgiving
