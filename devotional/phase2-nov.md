## November 1

# Austremonius of Clermont

*Apostle of the Auvergne, first bishop of Clermont, traditionally fl. c. 250*

He was one of the seven bishops the Roman church sent into Gaul in the middle of the third century — Saturninus to Toulouse, Trophimus to Arles, Paul to Narbonne, Martial to Limoges, Gatian to Tours, Denis to Paris, and Austremonius (in the Auvergnat speech that grew up around his shrine, *Stremoine*) to the great volcanic plateau in the heart of Roman Gaul where the Arverni had once held against Caesar. He arrived at *Augustonemetum* — the Roman name for what would become Clermont — perhaps around 250, and walked into a city of perhaps thirty thousand inhabitants where the Christian community he found was, at most, a handful.

He built a small chapel inside the city walls, the *acta* preserve, *non palam sed occulte* — not openly, but in secret. He preached. He baptized. He ordained, from the small group of converts who collected around him, the first generation of Auvergnat presbyters and deacons. The community grew. Within a few years he was running the catechumenate openly; within a generation the diocese he had founded had spread up the Allier and out across the high pasturelands of the central massif. He was perhaps the first martyr of the see — the older *acta* have him beheaded by the local pagans, having converted the children of a leading family and aroused the father to fury — though whether his life ended in martyrdom or in peaceful old age the Bollandists declined to settle.

The cult was immediate and unbroken. Gregory of Tours — who grew up in the Auvergne in the sixth century and who knew the Clermont cathedral inside and out — preserves the eucharistic miracle the *acta* report at length: a peasant who had been given consecrated bread by Austremonius after Mass, having stowed it in his satchel for the road home, was caught by a sudden flood at the crossing of the Allier and would have drowned but for the satchel, which floated him to shore as if it were a boat. Gregory adds that this is why, in the Auvergne in his day, the country people kept the practice of eating the *eulogia* — the blessed bread distributed at the end of Sunday Mass — before any other food on the Lord's day.

He was buried at first inside the city walls and later translated to the great abbey of Issoire, twenty miles south of Clermont, where his bones still lie. The Clermont he founded crowned its kings of France there and held its first crusade-council there in 1095 and rebuilt its black-stone cathedral over his crypt in the thirteenth century. The first bishop of the chair has been buried for seventeen hundred years. The chair has not been empty since.

> *"Eulogias ante alium cibum:"* — *the Auvergnat custom Gregory of Tours preserves: the blessed bread, before any other food*

## November 2

# Ambrose of Agaune

*Burgundian abbot, second father of Saint-Maurice, c. 470–c. 521*

He had been abbot of the small monastery of Île-Barbe — *the Barbarian Island,* the wooded outcrop in the middle of the Saône just upriver from Lyon — when the Burgundian king Sigismund, recently converted from Arianism, decided in 515 to refound the great Frankish monastery at Agaunum in the alpine Valais. Agaunum was the site of the martyrdom of Maurice and the Theban Legion — the Egyptian legion who had refused to draw their swords against fellow Christians, executed in the gorge of the Rhone perhaps in 287 — and the small chapel that had grown up over their grave had become, by the early sixth century, run-down, half-secularized, and inhabited largely by laymen who had drifted into it for the patronage. Sigismund wanted a real monastic community. The Burgundian bishops, gathered for the dedication on September 22, 515, had recommended Hymnemodus of Grigny for the office. He took it; he died within five years; the brothers asked for Ambrose to succeed him.

Ambrose came up from the Saône with a few of his Île-Barbe brothers in the autumn of 520. The reform he installed at Agaune would shape the great Frankish monastic foundations of the next two centuries: the *laus perennis,* the perpetual praise, by which the brothers were divided into nine choirs that succeeded each other through the twenty-four hours of the day so that the office of the Mass and the singing of the psalms continued unbroken at the tomb of the martyrs from year's end to year's end. The custom had been imported from the East — from the *acoemetae* of Constantinople, the *sleepless* monks — and the Burgundian foundation became its first Western installation. The form spread. By the eighth century the *laus perennis* would be observed at Saint-Denis, at Saint-Riquier, at Reichenau, at Lorsch, at every great Carolingian house of the Frankish heartland.

Ambrose served as abbot for perhaps a year. He died on the second of November, 521. The *Vita sanctorum abbatum Agaunensium* — written within a generation of his death by an Agaune monk who had known him personally — gives him a quiet portrait: a small, careful, deeply learned man who had spent his short rule holding the brothers to the rule and the silence, who had asked to be buried not under his own name but at the foot of the martyrs' altar, *quia non est dignus servus super dominum suum,* because the servant is not worthy to be over his lord.

The abbey he reformed has not stopped chanting. *Saint-Maurice d'Agaune* is the oldest continuously functioning monastery in the West — fifteen hundred years on the same foundation, fifty years older than Monte Cassino — and the office Ambrose installed has never been entirely silenced. The Augustinian canons who govern the abbey today still keep the vigil at the martyrs' tomb. The servant who would not be over his lord has been at his lord's feet for fifteen hundred years.

> *"Quia non est dignus servus super dominum suum."* — Ambrose, on his refusal to be buried under his own name

## November 3

# Hubert of Liège

*Bishop of the Ardennes, the stag in the forest, c. 656–727*

He was a Frankish nobleman of the Aquitanian royal house — perhaps the eldest son of Bertrand of Toulouse, the last duke of Aquitaine before the Carolingian absorption — born around 656 to the world of court intrigue and forest hunting that the Frankish *grandes* lived in the long century of decline before Pepin and Charles Martel. He was raised at the court of King Theuderic III. He married a Bavarian princess named Floribana, by whom he had a son. He served, in his early thirties, as a count of the palace under Pepin of Herstal. He was a famous hunter — Bishop Jonas of Orléans's ninth-century *Vita* preserves the family memory of him in this period: red-haired, light-eyed, expert with hawk and hound, fond of his own horses, occasionally given to anger.

The conversion came on a Good Friday in his late thirties. The court had assembled for the great spring stag-hunt in the forest of the Ardennes; Hubert had ridden out alone before dawn, against his confessor's advice, to course a great white stag he had been tracking for weeks. The chase carried him deep into the forest. The stag, when at last he closed on it in a clearing high above the Lesse river, turned and faced him; between its antlers, the *Vita* says, was a luminous cross. The stag spoke: *Hubert, why are you hunting me, when you should be hunting your own salvation?* Hubert dismounted. The cross was still there. He went home that evening, gave away his hounds and his horses, told his wife and his son, and walked east into the wilderness with no possessions and no plan.

He took shelter at the small monastic foundation of Lambert at Maastricht. Lambert was the bishop of the Tongres-Liège diocese — a missionary see that ran from the upper Meuse north into Frisia and east into the German forest country — and Lambert took the new convert in, taught him the office, ordained him priest, and over the course of a dozen years made him the indispensable second-in-command of the Maastricht church. When Lambert was assassinated in 705, Hubert was elected his successor against his strenuous protest. He governed the see for twenty-two years.

What he did with the office was missionary work. The Ardennes forest he had once hunted in was still half-pagan in the early eighth century — small Frankish villages with sacred groves and standing stones, a thin overlay of Christian baptism on a mostly older religion — and Hubert spent twenty years on horseback going from clearing to clearing, baptizing, building chapels, ordaining local priests, settling disputes, healing the dying. The cathedral he built at Liège on the banks of the Meuse, where he had translated Lambert's body and where the city of Liège grew around the basilica he had raised, became the seat of a diocese that has not moved since. He died of a hunting-injury infection at Tervueren on the thirtieth of May, 727; the cult-day was moved to the third of November, the day his body was translated to Liège.

> *"Why are you hunting me, when you should be hunting your own salvation?"* — *the white stag in the Ardennes forest, on the Good Friday Hubert was converted*

## November 4

# Emeric (Imre) of Hungary

*Crown prince of the Magyars, son of Stephen, d. 1031*

He was the only surviving son of Stephen of Hungary — the king who had taken the Christian crown from Pope Sylvester II at the dawn of the new millennium — and his Bavarian wife Gisela, sister of the emperor Henry II. He was born around 1007, named for his uncle the emperor (the Hungarian *Imre* is the Magyar form of the German *Heinrich*), and raised at the new Christian court at Esztergom in the strict piety his father had imported from the Bavarian and Lotharingian missionary houses. The tutor Stephen chose for him was Gerard of Csanád, the Venetian Benedictine bishop eventually killed by the pagan revolt of 1046. The *Vita Maior Sancti Stephani* preserves the program of the boy's education: the seven liberal arts in the morning, the *Rule of Saint Benedict* in the afternoon, the *Imitatio* of the Eastern desert fathers in the evening. The prince was being raised to be the second Christian king of Hungary.

He was being raised with the assumption that the throne was his and would come to him in middle life. Stephen, by 1030, was sixty years old; the kingdom was secure; the succession was clear; the long work of converting Hungary from a pagan tribal confederation into a Christian European kingdom was at the point where it could finally be turned over to the next generation. Imre had been knighted, betrothed, and was preparing in the autumn of 1031 to assume the regency of the southern Croatian-Serbian frontier when, on the second of September, he was killed by a wild boar in a hunting accident in the forests of the Bihar mountains. He was twenty-four years old.

The shock to his father was the central wound of the rest of Stephen's life. The *Vita* preserves the king's prayer: *Lord, you have taken from me what you gave me; what shall I give you back?* The answer, as Stephen formed it over the next seven years of his life — he died in 1038 — was the dedication of the kingdom itself. With no heir, with the next of his line a cousin who could not be trusted with the crown, with the Christian settlement of Hungary still fragile, Stephen lifted the crown of his kingdom on his own deathbed and gave it to the Virgin Mary as her perpetual fief: *Regnum Marianum,* the Marian kingdom, Hungary as Mary's land. The dedication held. The Hungarian nation has called itself by that name ever since.

What survives of Imre is small. The *Vita* preserves his vow of perpetual chastity, taken on his wedding night with his bride's consent. It preserves his nightly reading of the psalter, his almsgiving to the poor of Esztergom, his unfailing presence at his father's morning Mass, the gentleness with which he treated the small Christian community that was just beginning to take root in his future kingdom. He was canonized in 1083 by Pope Gregory VII along with his father and his tutor Gerard — three saints in one family in one generation, the only such triple canonization in medieval church history. The young man who had been preparing to govern the kingdom became, in dying before he could, the cause of its dedication to Mary. The father had given the kingdom away because the son had gone before him.

> *"Lord, you have taken from me what you gave me; what shall I give you back?"* — Stephen, in the *Vita,* at the news of Imre's death

## November 5

# Zechariah and Elizabeth

*Parents of John the Baptist, fl. c. 5 B.C.*

The first chapter of Luke is what we have. He was a priest of the temple, of the *course of Abijah* — one of twenty-four rotating divisions of the Aaronic priesthood, each of which served at Jerusalem for one week twice a year — married to a woman who was herself of the priestly line and who was, in some way the gospel does not specify, kin to the mother of the Lord. They lived in the hill-country of Judah, perhaps at Ein Karem six miles west of Jerusalem where the medieval tradition has placed them. They were old. They had no children. They had given up praying for one.

Zechariah's week of service came around in the late summer or early autumn of perhaps 6 B.C. He drew the lot for the morning incense — the most prized of the daily offerings, distributed by sortition among the priests of the day's course who had not yet performed it — and went into the *hekal,* the inner sanctuary, alone. The angel was on the right side of the altar of incense. The terror was immediate. The promise — *thy prayer is heard; thy wife Elizabeth shall bear thee a son, and thou shalt call his name John* — was older than Zechariah expected, going back beyond his prayer to the long Israelite line of which he and his wife had been the apparent late dead end. The doubt was honest. *Whereby shall I know this? for I am an old man, and my wife well stricken in years.* The sign was the silence — *thou shalt be dumb, and not able to speak, until the day that these things shall be performed.* He came out of the temple and made signs to the people, and they understood that he had seen a vision. He went home dumb.

Elizabeth conceived. She kept herself hidden five months — *thus hath the Lord dealt with me in the days wherein he looked on me, to take away my reproach among men.* In the sixth month her young cousin Mary came up from Nazareth. The greeting at the door — *blessed art thou among women, and blessed is the fruit of thy womb* — is the foundation of the *Hail Mary;* the *Magnificat* that follows is one of the four canticles of the Christian liturgy.

The child was born. They circumcised him on the eighth day. They tried to call him Zacharias for his father; the mother insisted on John, the name the angel had given; the father wrote it on a tablet — *his name is John* — and his tongue was loosed and he began to prophesy. *Benedictus Dominus Deus Israel,* the third of the gospel canticles, is what came out. He blessed his son, kissed his wife, and walked back into a quiet old age. Of his death and of Elizabeth's the gospel is silent. The medieval tradition has him martyred in the temple by Herod's troops at the time of the Massacre of the Innocents, but the scripture has nothing of it. What it has is the silence of nine months and the song that broke it.

> *"His name is John."* — Zechariah, on the wax tablet, *Luke* 1:63

## November 6

# Winnoc of Wormhoudt

*Anglo-Breton monk, abbot of Wormhoudt, c. 640–c. 717*

He was Welsh-Breton by family — a member of the Cornish-Breton royal house of Domnonia, perhaps a son of King Judicael of Brittany — and was sent across the Channel as a young man to study under Bertinus at the new Benedictine foundation at Sithiu in Frankish Flanders. The monastery he came to had been started by Audomarus and Mommolinus only thirty years earlier, and Winnoc became one of the four Britons whom Bertinus accepted as novices in the 670s, perhaps the most accomplished of them. He took the habit, made profession, and stayed at Sithiu for the next twenty years.

In about 695 Bertinus, now an old man, sent Winnoc north into the partly-pagan country between the Aa and the North Sea — the Frankish *pagus Mempiscus,* a flat marshy region of small fishing-villages and isolated farms where the gospel had not yet taken hold — with three British companions to plant a daughter house. They built a small cell at Wormhoudt, a hamlet on a low rise above the marsh, and over the next two decades raised it into a working monastery: refectory, dormitory, scriptorium, an enclosed garden for the brothers, an open one for the poor. Winnoc was its first prior and eventually its abbot. The community was small — perhaps a dozen brothers — and Winnoc, though now in his sixties and the abbot, refused to be excused from any of the manual work. He took his turn at the kitchen, the laundry, the pigsty, the scriptorium. When he had grown too old to work in the fields, he asked to be assigned to the mill, where he would grind the brothers' grain.

The mill at Wormhoudt was one of the small hand-mills the monasteries used to grind a day's flour at a time. A man Winnoc's age — he was in his late seventies by then — ought not to have been able to keep up with the strength of the millstones. The brothers noticed, after a few months, that the meal-bin behind the mill was always full when they came to draw flour from it, and that the stones were turning at speeds the old man could not possibly have produced by his own arm. One of the brothers, curious, peered through the gap in the mill-shed wall one afternoon to see how he managed. The mill stopped immediately. The brother went blind on the spot. He stumbled into the chapel weeping; the brothers brought him to Winnoc's feet; Winnoc, gentle and amused, blessed his eyes in the Lord's name and the man saw again.

He died at Wormhoudt on the sixth of November, perhaps 717, eighty years old at least. The body was translated to Bergues-Saint-Winoc when the abbey there was founded under his name in the late ninth century, and it has lain there since. The Welsh-Breton prince who had been sent across the Channel as a young man and never went home founded one of the great small monasteries of Frankish Flanders by being willing, at the end of a long life, to grind the brothers' grain.

> *"Tene quod habes, ne alius accipiat coronam tuam."* — *the verse hung above Winnoc's mill at Wormhoudt: Hold fast that which you have, that no man take your crown*

## November 7

# Willibrord of Utrecht

*Apostle of the Frisians, first archbishop of Utrecht, c. 658–739*

He was a Northumbrian, born around 658 in the kingdom of Deira, the southern half of the Northumbrian heptarchy where Edwin had ruled the previous generation. His father Wilgis was a married presbyter of the Yorkshire countryside who, after the boy's birth, set himself up as a hermit on the cliffs above the Humber and lived another forty years there as a solitary. Willibrord was sent at seven to the great Northumbrian abbey of Ripon under Wilfrid and was raised there as one of Wilfrid's own children. At twenty he went to the Irish monastery of Rathmelsigi to learn Greek and patristic Latin and continental computus; he stayed for twelve years.

The proposal to plant a mission in Frisia came from his old Irish abbot Egbert in 690. The Frankish *maior domus* Pippin of Herstal, having just defeated the Frisian king Radbod in a frontier campaign that opened the Christianizable parts of the lower Rhine country to the gospel, was looking for missionary clergy. Egbert sent Willibrord with eleven companions — *erant autem numero XII,* the twelve apostles of Frisia. They landed at the mouth of the Rhine, walked east along the coast to Utrecht, and settled in the small Roman chapel that the great Frankish missionary Amandus had built sixty years earlier and that the pagan Frisians had reduced to ruins.

Willibrord went to Rome in 695 to be consecrated bishop. Pope Sergius received him in the Lateran, gave him the new name *Clemens,* put the pallium on his shoulders, and sent him back as the first archbishop of the Frisians. He took his see at Utrecht, the small Roman *castellum* on the lower Rhine that the Frankish administration had been using as a frontier customs-post. He governed the Frisian church for forty-four more years.

The work was patient and slow. He ordained a generation of Frisian-speaking presbyters; he founded the great daughter monastery of Echternach in 698 on a piece of land Pepin's mother-in-law Irmina gave him; he made repeated missionary journeys north into Denmark (the Danish king Ongendus refused him hospitality but gave him thirty Danish boys to be raised as missionaries — they were the first Christian-Scandinavian generation), into the Frankish-Saxon borderlands, into the Frisian island of Heligoland, where he baptized in the spring at the foot of the pagan idol of Fositesland, a defiant act that drew the murderous attention of King Radbod. Bede, who knew him personally, gives him the warmest portrait of any of the second-generation continental missionaries. He was, Bede writes, *moderatus, gravis, prudens, in conversatione humilis et iocundus* — measured, serious, prudent, in his conversation humble and pleasant. He died at Echternach on the seventh of November, 739, eighty-one years old, having walked into a swampy frontier country at thirty-two and walked out of it having given the country a church.

> *"Moderatus, gravis, prudens, in conversatione humilis et iocundus."* — Bede, *Historia Ecclesiastica* 5.11

## November 8

# Godfrey of Amiens

*Reform abbot, contested bishop, c. 1066–1115*

He was given to the Benedictines of Mont-Saint-Quentin near Péronne as an *oblatus* at five years old, the customary medieval offering of a younger son to the religious life. He stayed there for thirty-five years. He was a quiet, intense, careful man — *reverend, sober, pacific,* the source Nicolas of Soissons writes — and he rose through the offices of the community in the steady way the Cluniac reform had begun to make standard for the well-governed houses of the late eleventh century: novice-master at twenty, prior at twenty-eight, in 1096 elected abbot of Nogent-sous-Coucy, the half-ruined small house in the Aisne forest that had been waiting for someone who could repair it. He went, with a half-dozen brothers from Mont-Saint-Quentin. He stayed for eight years. He raised it from twelve brothers to forty, refounded the dormitory and the chapter-house, restored the conventual office to its full Benedictine observance, and made the abbey one of the model Cluniac reformed houses of Picardy. The chronicler Guibert of Nogent — who succeeded him as abbot — calls him *speculum totius religionis,* the mirror of the whole religion.

The election to the see of Amiens in 1104 came against his strongest protest. The previous bishop had been a noted simoniac whom Pope Urban II had deposed; the chapter wanted a reformer of unimpeachable life; the king and the count of Amiens both backed Godfrey. He resisted for months — he believed an abbot should not be made a bishop, and he believed himself unequal to the office — and was finally consecrated under what amounted to an ecclesiastical compulsion in November 1104.

The eleven years that followed were the most contested of any episcopate in northern France in the early twelfth century. Godfrey was a reformer of the strict observance: he disciplined the cathedral chapter, broke the long-standing simoniacal traffic in benefices, refused to consecrate any candidate who could not show the canonical age and education, and (most explosively) supported the Amiens *commune* — the elected civic government the burghers had set up against the count and the bishop's predecessors — when the count tried to have him repudiate it. *I will rather suffer exile than withdraw from the just,* he is reported to have said when the count's representatives threatened him. He was twice driven out of the city by political enemies and twice returned at the head of a peaceful procession of his own clergy. He died in 1115 at the abbey of Saint-Crispin in Soissons, returning from a long preaching tour, sixty years old. His enemies in Amiens refused to have the body brought home; the Cistercians at Soissons buried him themselves, and his bones have been there since.

What survives, between Guibert's hostile-but-respectful chapters and Nicholas of Soissons's adoring *Vita,* is a man who would not be moved: *bonum quod amplexus erat tenebat firmiter et constantissime defendebat,* Guibert writes, even when he disliked him — *the good he had embraced he held firmly and defended with the greatest constancy.*

> *"I will rather suffer exile than withdraw from the just."* — Godfrey, to the count's envoys at Amiens

## November 9

# Ursinus of Bourges

*First bishop of Bourges, apostle of the Berry, fl. c. 250*

He was one of the seven bishops the Roman church sent into Gaul in the third century — the same band that brought Denis to Paris, Saturninus to Toulouse, Trophimus to Arles, Paul to Narbonne, Martial to Limoges, Gatian to Tours, and Austremonius (just a week earlier in this calendar, on November 1) to Clermont. Ursinus drew the Berry — the high pasture-country between the Loire and the central massif, with its capital at *Avaricum,* the great Roman city Caesar had taken from Vercingetorix two and a half centuries earlier and which was, by 250, one of the major administrative seats of central Gaul. The diocese of Bourges that grew up under Ursinus has been one of the great archdioceses of France ever since.

Gregory of Tours, writing in the late sixth century, is the earliest source — and in two different works he gives two contradictory dates. In the *Historia Francorum* he places Ursinus with the seven bishops in the time of Decius. In the *De Gloria Confessorum* he calls him *a discipulis apostolorum,* of the disciples of the apostles, which would put him in the late first century. The Bollandists settle on the older third-century dating, drawn from the local memory of the Bourges Christian community; the apostolic claim is the later legend that grew up around the rediscovery of his relics.

What survives of his work, in any case, is the ground-laying. He arrived at Bourges with no community to receive him, opened a small house chapel in the home of a Gallo-Roman senator named Leocadius — an ancestor (Gregory preserves with proper family pride) of his own family, descended from the Lyon martyr Vettius Epagathus who had died in the persecution of 177 — and gathered the first generation of converts there. He ordained presbyters and deacons from among the Berry Christians as their numbers grew. He built the first cathedral of the city, almost certainly a small wooden building inside the Roman walls. He was buried, when his time came perhaps in his late sixties, in a Gallo-Roman cemetery just outside the city walls, and his grave was forgotten in the long pagan reaction that followed the Decian persecution.

The grave was rediscovered by accident in the mid-sixth century. A servant of the diocese named Augustus, who had built a small oratory in honor of Saint Martin on what turned out to be the Roman cemetery site, was excavating for an extension when his workmen came on a marble sarcophagus inscribed with the name *Ursinus.* The relics were translated, in the presence of Bishop Probian of Bourges and the visiting Saint Germanus of Paris, into the new basilica that took Ursinus's name and that has stood on the spot since. The cathedral of Bourges that grew up over the next eight hundred years — one of the masterpieces of French Gothic, the *Cathédrale Saint-Étienne* — kept his crypt below the apse. The first bishop the Roman church sent to the Berry has not had to be moved twice.

> *"De stirpe Vettii Epagathi."* — Gregory of Tours, on the family of Leocadius the senator who first opened his house to Ursinus

## November 10

# Tryphon of Lampsacus

*Boy goose-herd, healer, martyr, c. 232–c. 250*

He was a peasant child of the small Phrygian village of Kampsada, near Apamea on the high inland plateau of Asia Minor, born to a Christian family of goose-herders perhaps in the early 230s. The Greek *acta* preserve the memory of him as a small boy in a long woollen tunic, herding the family's flock of geese on the high pastures above the village, praying as he walked. He had a gift, the *acta* preserve, for the healing of small things: the lambs that had been struck by snakes, the calves that would not nurse, the vines that the locusts had begun to take. The local farmers brought him their failing crops and their sick livestock; he laid his small hands on them; the affliction lifted. By the time he was thirteen the gift had grown to humans, and the regional pagan authorities had begun to take notice.

The fame brought him, the *acta* preserve, to the imperial court itself. The emperor Gordian III's young daughter fell into a sustained possession that none of the court physicians or the priests of Apollo could relieve. The emperor's chamberlain, hearing reports of the Phrygian boy, sent for him. Tryphon walked the eight hundred miles from Phrygia to Rome and, brought before the possessed girl, prayed over her in the language of the Galilean fishermen. The girl was freed. Gordian sent him home with a small purse and a court protection. He went back to his geese.

The protection lapsed when Gordian was murdered in 244 and Decius eventually came to the throne in 249. The Decian persecution opened in early 250. Tryphon was perhaps eighteen or nineteen by then. The local *praefectus,* a man named Aquilinus, had him arrested at his village and brought to Nicaea — the imperial city on the Sea of Marmara that would later host the great council — for the standard interrogation. The *acta* preserve the trial in close detail: Aquilinus was charming, then threatening, then enraged. The boy answered with the patience of someone who had been answering this question for years. *Christianus sum. I am a Christian. The names you would have me invoke are the names of demons. The God I serve is the God who made the geese.* Aquilinus put him through the standard tortures — the rack, the sharpened iron combs, hot lead poured down the chest, finally horse-shoes nailed to his feet so they could drag him through the streets behind a chariot. The boy held. Eventually, on the tenth of November, 250, Aquilinus tired of it and ordered the head struck off.

The body was recovered by the Christian community of Nicaea and buried at his village. The relics traveled west during the Saracen disruptions of the seventh century and ended up at Rome under the high altar of the church of *Santo Spirito in Sassia* — the small Saxon church the Anglo-Saxon kings had built for English pilgrims in the eighth century — where they have stayed since. The boy who had healed his sick goslings is the patron, in the Greek tradition, of farmers and gardeners and small children sick with fever. Eight hundred years after his death the field-priests of the Aegean were still blessing the fields of new-sown wheat in his name on his feast day.

> *"Christianus sum. The God I serve is the God who made the geese."* — Tryphon, before Aquilinus at Nicaea
