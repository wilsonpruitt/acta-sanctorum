## July 21

# Praxedes of Rome

*Roman virgin and gatherer of martyrs, second century*

She was a daughter of the senator Pudens, of the family the apostle Paul names in the closing chapter of the second letter to Timothy: *Eubulus greeteth thee, and Pudens, and Linus, and Claudia, and all the brethren.* Pudens, by the early Roman tradition, was a Christian senator baptized by Peter in his own house on the Vicus Patricius on the Esquiline hill. He had two daughters, Pudentiana and Praxedes, both consecrated virgins, both eponyms of two of the oldest Roman *tituli* — the small house-churches that were the earliest organized places of Christian worship in the city. The two basilicas — Santa Pudenziana and Santa Prassede — still stand on the Esquiline a quarter-mile apart.

She lived through the persecutions of the second century. The chronicler's *Acts*, in the careful Bollandist recension at AASS Julii V, give the work that her name was remembered for. With her elder sister Pudentiana while the sister lived, and alone after Pudentiana's early death, Praxedes used the family house on the Vicus Patricius as a refuge for the persecuted, a place of secret Eucharist, and a hospice for the bodies of the martyrs. When a Christian was killed in the games or in the prisons or on the Via Cornelia, she and her household servants would by night recover the body, wash it, anoint it, and lay it in a private cemetery she had opened on the family land outside the Salarian gate. She is said by the *Acts* to have gathered, in this way, the bodies of twenty-three named martyrs and many more unnamed.

She kept also a particular relic in the chapel of the house: a marble basin into which she would sponge, after each of these night-recoveries, the blood from the linens used to wash the bodies. The basin survives. It is set in the floor of the side chapel of the *Cappella di San Zenone* at Santa Prassede on the Esquiline, behind a small bronze grate, and the inscription beside it gives Praxedes's name and the second-century date.

She died in her old age, in the family house, of natural causes, sometime in the reign of Antoninus Pius, perhaps about 160. She had outlived the persecutions of her early womanhood and the kinder middle decades both. They buried her with her father and her sister in the same family vault. Pope Paschal I, in 822, brought her bones into the new basilica on the Esquiline that bears her name. The two thousand three hundred martyrs whose names the basilica's slab inscription preserves are, by Paschal's record, the count of those she had once gathered.

> *"What you do to the least of these, you do to me — even after they are dead."* — Praxedes, written on the wall of her chapel
