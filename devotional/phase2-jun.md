## June 1

# Justin Martyr

*Philosopher and apologist, c. 100–165*

He was born at Flavia Neapolis, the Roman colony on the site of ancient Shechem in Samaria, of pagan Greek parents. He took up philosophy as a boy — first the Stoics, then a Peripatetic who, the *Dialogue with Trypho* recalls, dismissed him as soon as it became clear he could not pay the fees; then a Pythagorean who required preliminary studies in music, astronomy, and geometry; and at last the Platonists, in whose contemplation of the incorporeal he believed he had found peace. He walked one afternoon along the shore at Caesarea, alone, trying to think. An old man met him there and asked him a question he could not answer: how the soul knew God. The old man told him to read the Hebrew prophets. He did. He found, he says, the only philosophy at once safe and profitable.

He kept his philosopher's *pallium* — the traveling cloak of the schools — for the rest of his life. He opened a Christian school of philosophy in Rome above the baths of a man named Martinus, on the road to the Forum, and taught there for years. He composed two *Apologies* addressed to the emperors Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius — careful, courteous, learned, arguing that the philosophers of the Greeks had partly known the *Logos* that had now been made flesh; that Christians were not atheists or seditionists but the truest citizens; that the rites of the Eucharist were ancient and reasonable and should be examined, not suppressed. He wrote the *Dialogue with Trypho*, the first long Christian-Jewish exchange to survive.

He was denounced about 165 by a rival philosopher named Crescens, brought before the prefect Rusticus with six of his students, and questioned. The transcript of that hearing — preserved in his *Acts* — is one of the earliest court records of any kind from the Roman world. *Rusticus said: Do you suppose that you will ascend into heaven? Justin answered: I do not suppose; I know.* They were scourged and beheaded together. He had spent forty years arguing that Christ was the *Logos* of every honest philosopher. He was the first philosopher to die for the argument.

> *"I do not suppose; I know."* — Justin, before the prefect Rusticus

## June 2

# The Martyrs of Lyons

*Forty-eight Christians of Lyons and Vienne, 177*

A summer mob in the colonial Roman city of Lugdunum — the modern Lyons — in the seventeenth year of Marcus Aurelius drove the Christians of the place out of the marketplace, the baths, the public houses. They beat them in the streets. They drove them indoors. When the governor returned to the city he ordered the Christians arrested, and the prison filled with some forty-eight souls, men and women, Gallic and Greek, of every rank. The bishop Pothinus, then in his ninety-first year, was carried before the tribunal half-dead from the beatings he had already taken, and answered the governor's *Who is the God of the Christians?* with *If thou art worthy, thou shalt know.* They returned him to the prison; he died in it within two days.

The household slave Blandina was a small, frail woman whose mistress feared she would not stand the questioning. She was hung on a stake, the chronicler says, in the form of a cross, in the amphitheater, with wild beasts loosed upon her; the beasts would not touch her. She was taken down and brought back the next day. The boy Ponticus, fifteen years old, was set beside her to watch each other; she encouraged him to the end, like a mother, the chronicler says, sending her child before her to the king. When the boy was dead they put her last into a net, and a bull was loosed upon her. She had asked, as her only request, to be the last in the dying.

The community wrote the letter the church now possesses — the *Letter of the Churches of Lyons and Vienne* — to the churches of Asia and Phrygia, who had sent missionaries to Gaul a generation before. It described what had happened with extraordinary care. It refused to call the dead *martyrs*; only Christ, they said, was the true Witness, and the rest were *confessors*, those who had not yet finished their confession. It begged the Eastern churches to pray that they would. They did finish. The amphitheater is still there, on the slope above the rivers. The letter has lasted longer than the stones.

> *"If thou art worthy, thou shalt know."* — Pothinus to the governor of Lyons

## June 3

# Kevin of Glendalough

*Irish abbot, c. 498–618*

He was born of the royal house of Leinster, in the foothills west of Dublin, baptized Coemgen — *the fair-begotten* — by a passing British monk who happened to be in the village. He was sent at seven to be fostered by Petroc the Cornishman, who had crossed to Ireland for that decade, and at twelve to the monastic school of his uncle Eogain, where he learned the Latin psalter by heart. He was ordained perhaps in his twenties. He fled the offers of a comfortable house outside Dublin, walked alone into the Wicklow mountains, and at the foot of a deep glacial valley called Glendalough — *the glen of two lakes* — found a cave in the cliff above the upper lake and shut himself in it.

He lived there seven years before anyone found him. He slept on stone. He ate cresses and the trout of the lake. He prayed standing in the freezing water of the upper lake at dawn, the chronicler says, with his arms in the form of a cross, until a blackbird settled in his open palm and laid an egg there, and he remained motionless until the egg had hatched and the bird had flown. The story may be a parable. The bare devotion under it is not. Disciples found him in the eighth year, and would not leave; he gave in, came down from the cave, and founded the great monastery of Glendalough on the lower lake. It became one of the four chief monastic schools of Ireland for the next five centuries — a city of round towers, oratories, and bee-hive cells whose ruins still stand in the glen.

He governed sixty-five years. He refused episcopal consecration when it was offered. He was, the *Vita* says, simple in his diet, plain in his speech, fierce only in his guarding of the silence of the place. He died on the day Bede gives, June 3, 618 — by then a hundred and twenty years old, the chronicler says, though even the Irish chroniclers half-doubt the count. They buried him by the lower lake. The pilgrims still come, on the third of June, around the round tower he never lived to see built.

> *"Two lakes, and one silence between them."* — said of Glendalough in the old Irish

## June 4

# Petroc of Cornwall

*Welsh prince, abbot of Bodmin, sixth century*

He was a younger son of a Welsh king of Glamorgan, sent in his teens to the great monastic school of Llancarfan, and afterward — in the way of his generation — to Ireland, where the schools were finer still. He spent twenty years there. He came back to the western tip of Britain a middle-aged abbot, landed in north Cornwall at the mouth of the Camel river, and founded the monastery of Lanwethinoc on the slope above the harbor — the place now called Padstow, *Petroc's-stow*, his town. He governed it thirty years. He sent missions inland across the Bodmin moors and founded, perhaps near the end of his life, the daughter house at Bodmin itself, which would outlive Padstow as the chief seat of his cult.

The Cornish chronicler keeps three stories. Petroc was once preaching on a beach when a stag, half-spent, ran out of the trees and lay at his feet; behind it came a king's hunting party with hounds. Petroc spread his cloak over the stag and would not move. The king, the chronicler says, dismounted and asked the abbot's blessing instead. Petroc once took a sick wolf into the monastery infirmary and nursed it back to health; the wolf would not leave, and the chronicler says it learned to ring the chapel bell with its paw at the canonical hours. And Petroc once, near the end of his life, walked away from Padstow without telling anyone, took ship for the East, and was gone seven years — pilgrim through Jerusalem, hermit for a time, the *Vita* says, on a small island in the Indian sea — before returning to find his monastery wondering whether he was dead. He took up the abbacy again and ruled it three more years.

He died on a pastoral visit to the daughter house at Bodmin, June 4, perhaps 564, perhaps later. Padstow held his bones until the twelfth century; Bodmin holds them now, in an ivory casket that survived the Reformation buried in a parish wall. He had been a king's son who would not be king. He had been an abbot who could not stop walking.

> *"The stag is mine; my Lord is its Lord."* — Petroc, on the beach, to the king with the hounds

## June 5

# Boniface of Mainz

*Apostle of Germany, c. 675–754*

He was born Winfrid, of a Saxon family in the kingdom of Wessex, and given as a child of seven to the abbey of Exeter at his own asking. He went on to the abbey of Nursling near Winchester, learned the Latin Scriptures and the grammar of the schoolmen, became novice-master, and by his thirties was one of the most respected schoolmasters in the West Saxon kingdom. He could have remained. He was forty when he left. The Saxons of the Continent — his own distant kindred — were unbaptized, and he had decided he would not die in England.

He crossed to Frisia in 716, was turned back by the wars between Charles Martel and the Frisian king Radbod, returned to England, was sent again by Pope Gregory II in 719 with a new commission and a new name: *Bonifatius* — *the doer of good.* He worked his way south through Hesse and Thuringia, baptizing in rivers, ordaining priests where he could, writing back to his old students in England for books, for prayers, for more monks. At Geismar in 723, before a watching crowd, he took an axe to the great oak of Thor — the central holy tree of the Hessian pagans — and the tree, the chronicler says, split open in four directions at the first stroke and fell of itself. He built a chapel from its wood and dedicated it to Saint Peter. The Hessians, watching, came forward in their hundreds to be baptized.

He governed thirty more years as bishop, archbishop, papal legate, organizer of the Frankish church, founder of the abbey of Fulda. He crowned Pippin king of the Franks in 751 — the first papal anointing of a Frankish king, the act that would make Charlemagne possible. He was nearly eighty when he announced he would return to the Frisians of his first journey, who had never been wholly converted. He went unarmed. At Dokkum on the Frisian coast on June 5, 754, a war-band of pagans fell on his camp at dawn while he was preparing the dawn baptisms. He raised the Gospel-book over his head; they cut through it and through him together. The book — the *Ragyndrudis Codex* — is still at Fulda, the sword-cut visible across the open pages.

> *"Let us not be dumb dogs, nor silent watchers, nor mercenaries fleeing from the wolf."* — Boniface, in a letter to Cuthbert of Canterbury

## June 6

# Norbert of Xanten

*Founder of the Premonstratensians, c. 1080–1134*

He was a noble of the lower Rhineland, related distantly to the imperial house, made a subdeacon as a child for the income of a canonry at Xanten, and lived as a worldly young clerk at the court of the emperor Henry V into his middle thirties. He rode beside the emperor to Rome in 1111 — was present at the violent seizure of Pope Paschal II — and rode home shaken without having understood why. He was riding alone four years later, near Vreden in Westphalia, when a thunderstorm caught him in open country. A bolt of lightning struck the ground at the feet of his horse. The horse threw him. He lay an hour in the wet grass and got up converted.

He spent three years at the abbey of Siegburg learning the rule. He was ordained priest in 1115. He gave away his estates and his canonry, took permission of Pope Gelasius II to preach the gospel barefoot through France and the Rhineland, and walked. He preached in the open at fairs and crossroads, in three languages, often for hours, drawing the kinds of crowds Bernard would later draw. He reconciled feuding families. He was once challenged in public to drink poison as proof of his preaching; the chronicler says he drank it and did not die.

He was given a wooded valley in Picardy, called Prémontré — *the foreshown meadow* — by the bishop of Laon, and there in 1120 he founded a community on the Augustinian rule but bound by the strictest Cistercian-style poverty. The Premonstratensian order he founded would spread within twenty years to a hundred houses across Europe. He was reluctantly made archbishop of Magdeburg in 1126 and reformed his diocese with a severity that twice nearly cost him his life — a hired assassin's knife failed; a mob drove him from his own cathedral; he returned. He defended the Real Presence against the heretic Tanchelm of Antwerp by carrying the Sacrament through the streets in procession until the heresy collapsed of its own embarrassment.

He died at Magdeburg, June 6, 1134, having outlived the lightning by twenty years and put every one of them to use.

> *"What thou hast received, give. What thou hast learned, teach. What thou hast believed, do."* — Norbert, to his canons at Prémontré

## June 7

# Robert of Newminster

*Cistercian abbot of Northumberland, c. 1100–1159*

He was a Yorkshire parish priest, educated at Paris, of a settled benefice and a settled life, when the founding of the Cistercian abbey of Fountains in 1132 unsettled him. The founders were a small band of Benedictines who had walked out of Saint Mary's at York demanding a stricter rule, and had spent their first winter in the open under a great elm by the river Skell, eating boiled elm-leaves. Robert read of them in his rectory and went north to join them. He was thirty-two. He gave up his benefice and slept the rest of his life on straw.

He was at Fountains seven years — through the bitterest of the founding privations, when the brothers ate the bread of three days in five and twice nearly disbanded — and was singled out by the abbot for his patience under the discipline. In 1138 the baron Ralph de Merlay offered the order land at Morpeth in Northumberland for a daughter house, and Robert was sent to found it with twelve monks. They called it Newminster. He governed it twenty-one years. From Newminster he founded three further houses — Pipewell in Northamptonshire, Sawley in Yorkshire, Roche in the same county — so that by his death the Cistercian map of northern England carried his hand on four small abbeys.

He was a man of quiet temperament whom the chronicler remembers chiefly for two things. He prayed standing through every night of Lent for forty years, leaning at last on a wooden tau-staff that he kept by his choir-stall. And he refused, in 1147, to take the side of the see of York in its long quarrel with Rome; he walked to the Curia in person to plead the cause of his friend William FitzHerbert — who was eventually deposed, then restored — and lost the case the first time and won it the second, and did not, the chronicler notes, ever take credit for either.

He died at Newminster on the night of June 7, 1159, having celebrated the chapter Mass that morning. The abbey was suppressed in 1537. The thirteen mossed stones of the cloister are still visible in the field above the river Wansbeck.

> *"He prayed standing, that the labor might cost him something, and the gift be worth the giving."* — Cistercian chronicler, of Robert

## June 8

# Medard of Noyon

*Frankish bishop, c. 456–545*

He was born at Salency near Noyon in Picardy, of a Frankish father and a Gallo-Roman mother, in the years when the western empire had just dissolved and the Salian Franks were settling permanently among the people they had conquered. His parents, the chronicler says, were equally Christian and equally well-off, and he was the first child of either kindred to be brought up entirely within the faith. He was, by every account, a tender child. The biographer keeps two stories from his boyhood. He gave away the family colt to a poor man whose own horse had died, and stood beside the empty stall to receive the beating; his father, on hearing the reason, did not beat him. He sheltered a beggar from a thunderstorm under his own cloak in an open field, and a great eagle, the chronicler says, hovered above them both, holding off the rain.

He was made a priest at thirty-three and bishop of Vermandois — the see of Saint-Quentin, later transferred to Noyon — at fifty-three. He governed forty years. He outlived three Frankish kings, baptized the fourth (Lothar I), and helped re-found the eastern see of Tournai when the Gallo-Roman bishops had fled it. He founded the abbey of Soissons. He instituted the Salency *rosière* — the custom by which the most virtuous young woman of the village was crowned each year with a wreath of roses, given a small dowry, and married in the chapel he had built. The custom kept going at Salency for thirteen centuries, until the Revolution.

He was famous in his old age for one habit. When he sat in his chair at the cathedral door to hear cases of his episcopal court — for he was, like every Frankish bishop, magistrate as well as pastor — he always heard the case of the poorer party first. He gave reasons; the chronicler preserves several. *The wealthy man has friends to plead him; the poor man has only the bishop. He who can wait should not be made to wait.* It became proverbial in the diocese: *the poor go first to Medard's chair.*

He died, the chronicler says, on a wet June morning at Noyon in 545. The peasants of Picardy say it rains on his feast for forty days, because Medard always weeps a little when he sees the spring crops short.

> *"He who can wait should not be made to wait."* — Medard, at the cathedral door

## June 9

# Columba of Iona

*Irish abbot, apostle of the Picts, 521–597*

He was born of the Cenél Conaill in Donegal, great-great-grandson of Niall of the Nine Hostages, with a claim — had he wished to press it — on the high-kingship of Tara. He was baptized Crimthann, *the fox*, and renamed in the cloister Colum Cille, *dove of the church*. He studied with Finnian of Moville, Gemman the bard, Finnian of Clonard, and Mobhi of Glasnevin: the whole roster of sixth-century Irish learning. He founded Derry, Durrow, perhaps Kells. His Irish verse is the earliest the language can give a date to.

He was forty-one when the trouble came. He had borrowed Finnian's psalter, copied it secretly through nights at the candle, and refused to return the copy. Diarmait, called to arbitrate, gave the famous judgment *to every cow her calf; to every book its copy* against him. Within months a kinsman of his, taking sanctuary at the king's court, was killed by Diarmait's men. Columba raised the Cenél Conaill against the high king and won the battle of Cúl Dreimne. Three thousand men died for the psalter and for the kinsman. His confessor laid a penance on him: leave Ireland, convert as many souls as had fallen in the battle, and never see the Irish coast again.

He left in 563 with twelve companions in a small *curragh* and made landfall on the island of Iona off the western coast of Pictish Scotland. From the monastery he founded there he sent missions inland for thirty-four years — to the Pictish high king Brude at Inverness, whom he converted in 564 after a contest of psalms and signs; to the Hebrides, to Argyll, to Northumbria — and trained the missionaries from whom Aidan of Lindisfarne would receive the Irish gospel that converted the English north.

He died at the altar of Iona at midnight of June 9, 597, having spent the day copying psalm 33. He had reached the verse *those who seek the Lord shall not be in want of any good thing*, laid down the pen, and added in the margin: *Let Baithene write the rest.* He went into the church for the night Office and fell at the altar steps. He had not seen Ireland in thirty-four years. The wind that night, the chronicler says, blew steadily from the west.

> *"Let Baithene write the rest."* — Columba, last words of his copy of psalm 33

## June 10

# Margaret of Scotland

*Queen and reformer, c. 1045–1093*

She was born in exile in Hungary, where her father Edward — the Anglo-Saxon prince called *the Exile* — had fled from the Danish conquest of 1016. She was raised at the court of King Andrew I, learned five languages, read the Latin Fathers, and was returned to England at twelve when her father was called home as Edward the Confessor's heir. He died on the journey. She was twenty-one and a refugee for the second time when, after the Conquest of 1066, her family fled north into a storm in the German Sea, were blown ashore at the Firth of Forth, and were taken into the household of the Scottish king Malcolm III at his court at Dunfermline. He married her three years later. She bore him eight children.

She ruled Scotland through him and around him. She read to him at table — he was illiterate but devout, the chronicler says, and would kiss the books she read from. She brought English chaplains and Benedictine monks to a court that had been culturally Gaelic and ecclesiastically Celtic since the days of Columba. She convened church councils at Dunfermline that aligned Scottish observance — Lenten dating, communion frequency, marriage law — with the broader Western pattern. She founded the abbey of Dunfermline, restored the abbey of Iona that had been wrecked by Norse raids, established the ferry across the Firth of Forth for pilgrims to Saint Andrews (a service still called *Queensferry*), and rebuilt the chapel at Edinburgh Castle that is now the oldest building in the city.

She was the household manager of Scotland. She kept three hundred orphans clothed at court expense. She washed the feet of six poor every morning in Advent and Lent. She kept the long fasts so strictly that her health failed in her late forties. In November 1093 her husband Malcolm and her eldest son Edward rode south on a raid into Northumberland; both were killed at Alnwick. The news reached her at Edinburgh Castle on November 16. She had been bedridden for weeks. She received the last rites, asked for her *Black Rood* — the small reliquary cross she had brought from Hungary — and died holding it within four days of the news. She was forty-eight. The Scots translated her relics later to the abbey of Dunfermline, on the AASS-traditional day, June 10.

> *"Let nothing seem to me sweet besides my Lord Jesus Christ."* — Margaret, to her confessor Turgot, near the end

## June 11

# Barnabas the Apostle

*Cypriot Levite, companion of Paul, first century*

He appears first in the Acts of the Apostles, in chapter four, as Joseph, a Levite of Cyprus, who sold a field he owned and laid the price at the apostles' feet. The Twelve gave him another name. *Barnabas*, Luke explains, means *son of consolation* — *bar nehama*, in the Aramaic. He had a gift, and they had noticed it. When Saul of Tarsus arrived in Jerusalem some years later, freshly converted, and the disciples were afraid of him, it was Barnabas who took him by the hand and brought him in to the apostles, and explained what had happened on the road to Damascus. He stood, as the chronicler says, for the man no one else would yet stand for.

He was sent up to Antioch to investigate the rumor that Greeks were being baptized without circumcision. He arrived, saw, and approved. Then he walked to Tarsus, found Saul, and brought him back to Antioch with him to teach. It was there, under their joint year's teaching, that the disciples were first called Christians. From Antioch the church sent the two of them — Barnabas first, Paul second, in Luke's careful order — on the missionary journey through Cyprus and southern Asia Minor that opened the gentile mission of the church. They were taken for gods at Lystra after healing a man lame from birth, and only the speech Barnabas made — tearing his clothes, refusing the wreaths the priests of Zeus had brought — kept the crowd from sacrificing oxen to them.

He quarreled with Paul afterward over the boy John Mark, whom Paul refused to take on the second journey and Barnabas insisted on. They parted. Barnabas took Mark and sailed to his native Cyprus; Paul took Silas overland through Asia Minor; the world has had two streams of mission ever since, and neither was wrong. Mark wrote the Gospel under Barnabas's roof at Salamis. The tradition, preserved in Tertullian and in the AASS, says Barnabas was stoned to death by Jews at Salamis around 61, his body buried by Mark with a copy of Matthew's Gospel laid on his breast. Four centuries later the body, the chronicler says, was found by another bishop of Salamis in exactly that condition, the Gospel intact, and Cyprus has held its bones since.

> *"He was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith."* — Luke, *Acts* 11:24

## June 12

# Leo III, Pope

*Roman pontiff, crowner of Charlemagne, c. 750–816*

He was a Roman of modest birth — the son of an apparitor named Atyuppius — who rose through the Lateran chancery on competence rather than family. He had served thirty years in the curia, as treasurer and then cardinal-priest of Santa Susanna, when on the death of Hadrian I in December 795 he was elected pope by acclamation and consecrated the next day, the swiftest succession in centuries. He sent the keys of the confession of Saint Peter and the standard of the city of Rome to Charlemagne, king of the Franks, as a sign that the Roman pontiff acknowledged the Frankish king as protector of the Holy See. Charlemagne returned a courteous letter and a large gift of plate from the Avar treasure. The two never met.

Four years later the Roman nobility — relatives of his predecessor, who had been frozen out of the chancery — ambushed Leo during a procession on the feast of Saint Mark, 799. They dragged him from his horse, tried to cut out his tongue and gouge out his eyes, left him for dead in the street. Romans of the city rescued him in the dark and hid him in the monastery of Saint Erasmus on the Caelian. He recovered, miraculously, the chronicler says, with sight and speech intact, and slipped north over the Alps with a small Frankish escort to the king's court at Paderborn. Charlemagne received him with extraordinary honor and sent him home with an escort and an investigation commission. Leo was cleared by an oath of self-purgation at Saint Peter's in December 800. Two days later, on Christmas, as he rose from the *Te Deum* of the third Mass of the feast, he set the imperial crown of the West upon Charlemagne's head and acclaimed him *Augustus*.

He governed twenty-one years. He defended the *Filioque* clause in the creed against Greek protest but refused to insert it in the Roman liturgy in his lifetime, on the principle that the council of Nicaea had spoken. He sheltered Anglo-Saxon refugees from the Viking raids on Northumbria. He rebuilt the Lateran *triclinium* with the famous apse mosaic that survives in fragment today — Christ commissioning the apostles, Saint Peter handing the *pallium* to Leo and the standard to Charlemagne — a programmatic image of the bond he had made on Christmas Day. He died at the Lateran, June 12, 816.

> *"From this day there is no more Augustus but Christ, and no Caesar but he whom Christ anoints."* — Leo, on the Christmas of 800

## June 13

# Anthony of Padua

*Franciscan preacher and doctor, 1195–1231*

He was born Fernando at Lisbon, son of a knight of King Sancho I of Portugal. He entered the canons regular of Saint Augustine at fifteen and was sent at nineteen to the abbey of Santa Cruz at Coimbra, where he spent nine years reading. He read the Latin Bible until he had memorized the whole. He could have been a doctor of the schools. He never wrote a treatise. The moment of his conversion came in 1220, when the bodies of five Franciscans, killed by Moors in Morocco, were brought back to Coimbra in their habits. He looked at them, went home, and asked that day for the Franciscan habit himself. He took the name Anthony, after the Egyptian father, and set sail to follow the five.

He never reached it. A fever drove him back; storms blew the ship to Sicily; he made his way to Assisi in May 1221 for the great chapter and was assigned, as a man of no apparent use, to the kitchen of the hermitage of Monte Paolo above Forlì. He swept and washed pots for a year. The accident of an unprepared preacher at an ordination Mass in 1222 produced the discovery: he was made to preach extempore and was found to be the most learned scriptural preacher in Italy. Francis gave him reluctant permission to teach theology to the brothers — the first such permission within the order — and sent him out.

He spent the last nine years of his life preaching. He preached in Lombardy, in Provence, in the Romagna, sometimes to thirty thousand in the open piazzas of Padua and Rimini, who came barefoot from the surrounding hills. He preached on the prophets, on the parables, on usury, on reconciliation, on what conscience could be argued back from. The chronicler reports the famous moment at Rimini — when the heretics would not listen, he turned to the river and preached to the fish, and they rose in their thousands with their heads above the surface — as Franciscan parable, not zoology.

He died of edema at thirty-six, near Padua, on June 13, 1231, his last words a vision of Christ in the hands of a friar holding a candle. He was canonized inside the year — the fastest in church history.

> *"Lord, if you have anything to say to me, my heart is here."* — Anthony, in the kitchen at Monte Paolo

## June 14

# Methodius I of Constantinople

*Patriarch and confessor of the icons, c. 788–847*

He was born at Syracuse in Sicily, of a wealthy Greek family long settled on the island, and went to Constantinople in his twenties expecting a court career. The court rejected him. He turned aside, took the monastic habit at Chenolakkos in Bithynia, and was made abbot before he was thirty. He was sent to Rome in 815 as the patriarch Nikephoros's legate in the protest against Leo V's renewal of iconoclasm. The mission failed. He was in Rome eight years, learning Latin, copying Greek manuscripts on the Aventine, and waiting for an empire that did not want him back.

He returned to Constantinople in 821, when Leo V was dead, expecting a reconciled church. He found instead the new emperor Michael II refusing to reopen the question. Methodius — a private monk, of no rank — wrote and circulated a defense of the icons addressed to the emperor. He was scourged, branded on the face, and thrown into a dungeon on the island of Saint Andrew in the Sea of Marmara, where he was kept seven years with two thieves. One of the thieves died; Methodius buried him with his own hands inside the cell and chanted the funeral office alone. When at last he was let out in 829, the gangrene of seven years in the dark had eaten away half of his lower jaw, and he wore a black cloth tied around his head for the rest of his life to hide it.

He was made patriarch in March 843 by the empress Theodora, regent for the boy emperor Michael III, on the condition that he restore the icons. He convened a synod within days. He led the procession from the Blachernae church to Hagia Sophia on the first Sunday of Lent, 843 — the *Sunday of Orthodoxy* the Greek church has kept every year since — carrying icons through immense crowds who had not seen them in public for nearly thirty years. He governed four years more, dealing severely with the most rigorist of the iconodules as well as with the residual iconoclasts. He died at the patriarchal palace, June 14, 847, with the cloth still over his jaw and the synodikon of Orthodoxy he had drafted on the table.

> *"What I confessed with the half of my mouth that is left me, I will confess now with the whole of my soul."* — Methodius, on his consecration as patriarch

## June 15

# Landelinus of Crespin

*Merovingian robber-baron turned abbot, c. 625–686*

He was born of a Frankish landowning family in the country of Hainault, given as a boy to the school of Saint Aubert of Cambrai, and ran away from it in his teens. He fell in with a band of brigands in the forests between Cambrai and the Sambre, and for some years — the chronicler does not say how many, but no fewer than six, perhaps ten — he led them. He robbed travelers on the Roman roads. He robbed merchants of the Sambre wool trade. He raided villages. He had killed men, the *Vita* says plainly, though it does not number them; the number, by his own later confession, mattered less than that he could no longer count it.

He met Aubert again on the road by accident, or perhaps not by accident — the chronicler suggests the bishop had ridden out specifically to find him. They spoke through a long afternoon at the edge of a wood. Landelinus dismissed the band that evening. He walked to Rome on foot as the first penance. He stopped at every shrine on the road and sometimes slept in the porches for days. He came back to Hainault a different man, was ordained at Cambrai by Aubert's successor Vindician, and was given title to a small clearing in the wood at Lobbes on the Sambre.

He built a monastery there with his own hands in 654, then a second at Aulne in 657, then a third at Wallers, then a fourth — the great house — at Crespin in 670, on land donated by a converted noble. He governed Crespin for sixteen years. He kept the rule of Columbanus, sang the night office of three hundred and fifty psalms a week, and required of his monks only this: that they live by the work of their hands and refuse no traveler at the gate. He had been a robber on these roads; he made his houses into the first thing a stranger met on them. The men of his old band, the *Vita* says, came to him one by one over the years, several to die as monks under his rule. He died at Crespin, June 15, 686, having outlived his crimes by forty years and lost count, again, of the days he had spent in penance.

> *"What I took on these roads, may my houses on these roads return."* — Landelinus, founding Crespin

## June 16

# Lutgardis of Aywières

*Cistercian mystic of Brabant, 1182–1246*

She was born at Tongeren in the duchy of Brabant, to a merchant father whose dowry investment for her — a sum of silver kept in the family chest for the day she would marry — was lost in a single trading venture when she was twelve. The father, in some embarrassment, deposited her instead at the Benedictine convent of Saint Catherine outside Saint-Trond. She did not at first take to it. She was lively, conversational, well-liked among the visitors who came to the parlor, and went on for some years receiving the discreet attentions of a young soldier of the town through the speaking-grille. The change came at sixteen, when, the chronicler says, she saw a vision of Christ showing her his wounded side and asking her to attend to him with the attention she had been giving the world.

She gave it. She took final vows. She was elected prioress at twenty-four. Almost immediately she fled the office — too prominent, too administrative, too unsuited to the contemplation she had only just learned to hold — and transferred at twenty-four to the stricter Cistercian house at Aywières in the French-speaking Walloon country south of Brussels, where she lived the remaining forty years of her life. She refused to learn French. She had vowed, she said, never to speak again about anything that did not matter for salvation, and the unfamiliar grammar would make her speak slowly and weigh each word.

She spent three twenty-year periods of her life under three different fasts. The first twenty years she took only bread and beer. The second twenty years she ate fish and vegetables, having been told in vision that the Church needed her strength for prayer against the Cathar heresy then breaking out across the Lowlands. The third twenty years — when the heresy had subsided and she was old — she returned to bread and water. She went blind in her last eleven years. She was visited in her cell by her old confessor Thomas of Cantimpré, who wrote her *Vita*, and by Hadewijch the Beguine poet, who took some of her phrases home for her own *Visions*. She died at Aywières, June 16, 1246, asking for nothing at the end except that they bury her under the choir step, where the sisters would walk over her.

> *"Let me lie where you will walk; the dead also keep watch."* — Lutgardis, requesting her grave

## June 17

# Botulph of Icanho

*East Anglian abbot, d. 680*

He was a Saxon of East Anglia, sent for his education with his brother Adulph to the Frankish monastery of Faremoutiers-en-Brie in the 630s, when the sons and daughters of the Anglo-Saxon nobility were crossing the Channel to learn the rule. He came back to England about 654, was ordained, and was offered land for a foundation by Anna, king of the East Angles. He asked for a place no one wanted. Anna gave him a small bare promontory in the marshes called *Icanho* — *ox-island*, a high-tide islet at the mouth of what is now the Alde. The chronicler says the place was haunted; the country people would not pasture even their oxen on it after sunset.

He built his oratory there in 654 and slept in it the first night, the chronicler says, while the demons of the place wailed around the thatch until dawn. He stayed. Within a generation he had drained the salt-marshes into tillable ground. He grew a community of perhaps thirty monks who kept the rule he had learned at Faremoutiers — Columbanian with a Benedictine softening, severe in the night office, generous in hospitality. He was sought out as a teacher by every house that could spare a brother to send him; Ceolfrith of Wearmouth, who would later send the *Codex Amiatinus* to the pope, walked the length of England as a young man to learn the Office from Botulph.

He was famous in his own century for one thing: hospitality at the rim of the sea. Icanho stood on the road by which every traveler from the Frankish kingdoms to East Anglia made landfall. He gave every arrival a meal, a bed, and a blessing for the journey inland. Travelers built small shrines to him at the gates of their towns as a thanksgiving for safe arrival. By the high middle ages those gate-shrines numbered seventy-one across England, and the City of London held four of them; the parish of *Saint Botolph without Aldgate* survives today.

He died at Icanho, June 17, 680, having ruled there twenty-six years. The Danes burned the monastery in 870. The bones, hidden the night of the raid, were translated three times in three centuries and finally divided among Bury, Ely, and Westminster.

> *"Every gate is a sea-gate, and every traveler comes off the sea."* — Botulph, on hospitality

## June 18

# Elizabeth of Schönau

*Benedictine visionary of the Rhineland, 1129–1164*

She was a Rhineland girl from a modest free family, given to the Benedictine double monastery of Schönau in the Nassau hills at the age of twelve, and never left. She took her vows at eighteen. She became prioress of the women's community at twenty-eight. She was a small, quiet, sickly woman, the chronicler says, of unremarkable conversation, who served the meals and copied the choir books and was assumed by everyone in the cloister to be exactly what she appeared to be.

She began to receive visions at twenty-three. They came at first only at the Office, only on the great feasts, lasting a few minutes, and she told no one. By her late twenties they had spread through every Hour and to ordinary days; she fell into trance in choir, in the cloister, at table. She resisted writing them down for years until her brother Egbert, a Benedictine monk at the same house and later abbot of it, insisted. He took her dictation. The three *Books of Visions* that resulted, together with the *Book of the Ways of God* and the visionary expansion of the *Acts of Saint Ursula and the Eleven Thousand Virgins*, made her, within her own lifetime, the second most-read woman writer in twelfth-century Latin Europe, after her older friend and correspondent Hildegard of Bingen.

She and Hildegard exchanged ten years of letters that survive. They were strikingly different visionaries. Hildegard saw cosmic structures, the wheels of creation, the proportions of grace. Elizabeth saw scenes — the Virgin at the cradle, the angel at the empty tomb, the saints in their proper feasts — and her letters back to Hildegard treat her own gift with a kind of apologetic wonder, as if she could not understand why she of all people should be made to see these things. She was bedridden the last seven years of her life from the toll of the trances. She died at Schönau, June 18, 1164, at thirty-six. Egbert, who outlived her by twenty years, edited her writings and never claimed for them what she would not claim. The texts she had thought too small for the cloister are still in print eight hundred years later.

> *"What I see, I see; what I am, I am not."* — Elizabeth, to her brother Egbert at dictation

## June 19

# Bruno of Querfurt

*Camaldolese missionary-bishop, protomartyr of the order, c. 974–1008*

The AASS Junii III calls him *Bonifacius* — the name he took in religion in honor of the apostle of Germany, and the name he carried through the brief, intense, eight years of his apostolic mission to the pagan north. He had been born about 974 at Querfurt in Saxony of a noble family, raised at the cathedral school of Magdeburg, where the boy Adalbert of Prague was a fellow pupil. He had served as imperial chaplain at the court of Otto III, traveled to Italy in the emperor's retinue, met Romuald at the Camaldolese hermitage outside Ravenna, and there took the habit and asked the Pope's permission to follow Adalbert's road north.

He went, under the commission of Pope Gregory V, to the pagan country east of Prussia, where Adalbert had been martyred in 997. He traveled with eighteen companions. He baptized in rivers, taught through interpreters, lived rough. The natives, the chronicler notes, feared that if they killed him as they had killed Adalbert, the miracles afterward would convert their people; so by a cunning malice they held back their hands a long time, unwilling to give him the death he desired.

The chronicler keeps one miracle. The pagan king of the region, refusing baptism, had a great pyre built before his hall and challenged Bruno to walk through it as proof of his faith. He walked through it unharmed, *per ignem transisset illæsus*. The king and his household were baptized in the river the same evening. The king's brother, pagan, could not accept what his brother had just done, raised a war-band, fell on the new converts at dawn, killed the king, killed Bruno, killed the eighteen. The chronicler dates the death to 1008, March 1009 by modern reckoning. The AASS keeps his feast on June 19.

He had wanted, the *Vita* by Peter Damian says, to spill blood for Christ from the first day he had taken the Camaldolese habit. The natives had refused him a long time. They had refused him their conversion as long as they could. They gave him both in the same hour.

> *"He passed through the fire unharmed, baptized the king and the people, and was killed by the king's raging brother."* — Baronius's *Roman Martyrology*, quoted at AASS Junii III, on Bruno

## June 20

# Silverius, Pope

*Roman pontiff and martyr, d. 537*

He was the legitimate son, born before his father's ordination, of Pope Hormisdas (514–523), and was raised at the Lateran in the years his father was reconciling the Latin and Greek churches after the long Acacian schism. He took orders, served as a subdeacon, then a deacon. He was a quiet man, of moderate parts, of his father's blood and his father's instincts: hold what has been received. He was forty-something when the Ostrogothic king Theodahad — at war with the emperor Justinian and the general Belisarius then advancing through Italy — looked for a Roman to elevate to the chair of Peter who would be loyal to Gothic Italy. He picked Silverius and pressed the Roman clergy hard. They consecrated him under protest in June 536.

Belisarius took Rome by negotiation six months later. The empress Theodora at Constantinople, who had her own plans for the Roman see, sent letters to Belisarius asking him to depose Silverius and replace him with the deacon Vigilius, then in the imperial capital, who had privately promised to overturn the Council of Chalcedon and rehabilitate the Monophysite Anthimus, Theodora's protégé. Silverius refused on every point. He would not condemn Chalcedon. He would not seat Anthimus. He told the empress's messenger, the chronicler says, that he would rather descend from the chair than ascend to it on those terms.

He was summoned to Belisarius's headquarters in the Pincian palace on March 20, 537. The general's wife Antonina received him on a couch, with Belisarius at her feet. She read out forged letters purporting to show Silverius's correspondence with the Goths against the empire. He was stripped of his vestments on the spot, dressed in a monk's tunic, and exiled the next day to the island of Patara on the Lycian coast. Vigilius was consecrated pope in his place. The bishop of Patara, on receiving the exile, went to Constantinople himself to protest; Justinian, embarrassed, ordered the trial reopened. Silverius was returned to Italy and the matter remitted for investigation. He never reached Rome. Antonina intercepted him on the road, had him deported to the small malarial island of Palmarola off Naples, and left him there. He died of starvation in a cave on the island, June 20, 537.

> *"I had rather descend from the chair than ascend to it on these terms."* — Silverius, to Theodora's messenger

## June 21

# Alban of Mainz

*Greek martyr in Germany, c. 404*

He was, by the elogium that Rabanus Maurus preserved in his ninth-century *Martyrologium*, a Greek of the Cyclades — most likely Naxos, the AASS reasons. He was a younger cleric in the church there, traveling under the older Theonestus and a third companion named Ursus. They sailed west to Milan in the late fourth century, came under the influence of Ambrose — then fighting the Arian dukes for control of the basilicas of the city — and were dispatched by the archbishop north into Gaul.

Ursus did not survive the journey. He was martyred at Aosta — *Augusta Prætoria in Salassiis* — and is commemorated on February 1. Theonestus and Alban kept walking, took the Rhine road, and reached Mainz. The Rhineland was a difficult mission. Bishop Aureus of Mainz had been deposed and exiled seven times by Arian factions in his decades on the chair; Theonestus arrived to a bishop in office only by intervals.

Alban was the younger of the two, the AASS says plainly: *junior, sic et ad lacessendos hæreticos animosior quam Theonestus, magistro suo præripuerit coronam martyrii* — younger, and more spirited in confronting the heretics than his master Theonestus, so that he ran ahead of his master to the crown of martyrdom. He preached in the streets of Mainz against the Arian faction. An *infida turba* — a faithless crowd — caught him in the open and beheaded him on the spot. The local Christians took his body and buried him in a small chapel they built on the place. The Romanesque verses still inscribed at the chapel in the Petersplatz date the death precisely: *Orbe quater centum numerante et quattuor annos, dum tenet Imperii moderamina Honorius* — when the world counted four hundred years and four, under the rule of Honorius. The year 404.

The Mainz tradition gave him afterward the cephalophore's iconography, pictured carrying his own severed head to the place of burial — a standard convention for beheaded martyrs, the AASS gently notes, and not a separate miracle. What was real was simpler: a Greek-speaking priest from a small island, sent west by Ambrose, killed on a Rhineland street for refusing to be quiet about the consubstantial Son.

> *"Albanus sanctus hic requievit, scisso cum capite agonem complevit."* — Saint Alban here rested, his contest finished with his head sundered. — verses on the Mainz chapel, AASS Junii IV

## June 22

# Paulinus of Nola

*Senator, poet, bishop of Campania, c. 354–431*

He was Meropius Pontius Paulinus, of a senatorial Gallo-Roman family with estates in Aquitaine, Italy, and Spain. He was rich beyond ordinary measure. He had been a pupil of the poet Ausonius at Bordeaux, made consul-suffect at Rome in his late twenties, married a Spanish noblewoman named Therasia, and held a string of provincial governorships. He could have lived out the imperial career arc and been forgotten by everyone but the prosopographers. He was forty when their only son died at eight days old. Husband and wife buried the child at Alcalá in Spain and walked, that summer, to the shrine of Saint Felix at Nola near Naples, where he had once as a young governor felt a pull he had not explained even to himself.

They came home and sold everything. He freed his slaves with pensions. He gave the cash to the poor and to the churches of his three provinces. He kept only one small plot of land at Nola itself, on which stood the shrine, and walked back to it on foot in 395 with Therasia. They built two small huts of clay and reeds in the orchard behind the shrine and went into them. They lived thirty-six more years. He was ordained, by force, in Spain on a Christmas Eve in 394; he was elected bishop of Nola, again with reluctance, about 410. Therasia died sometime after 415. He governed Nola through the Vandal raids on Italy, the fall of Rome in 410 (refugees from which he housed by the hundred in the abandoned senatorial villas of Campania), and the famine years that followed.

He wrote letters to everyone — to Augustine in Africa, to Jerome at Bethlehem, to Sulpicius Severus in Aquitaine, to Martin of Tours, to Ambrose at Milan — and his fifty-one surviving letters are among the most affectionate documents of the Latin patristic world. He wrote thirty-three poems for the annual feast of Saint Felix on January 14, one a year, almost without interruption, for thirty-three years. He sold himself once into Vandal slavery, the chronicler says, to ransom the only son of a widow of Nola whom the Vandals had taken; the Vandal chief, on discovering whom he had bought, sent him back. He died at Nola, June 22, 431, having outlived the imperial world he had been born into and built another one in its orchard.

> *"Felix lived a life of poverty in his own poverty, and an angel's life in others'."* — Paulinus, *Carmen* 21 on Saint Felix

## June 23

# Etheldreda of Ely

*East Anglian queen and abbess, c. 636–679*

She was a daughter of Anna, king of the East Angles, and one of four sisters who all became saints. She was given in marriage at perhaps sixteen to Tondberct, a chief of the South Gyrwe in the fenland country south of Ely. She had asked, and he had agreed, that the marriage be celibate. He kept the agreement; she was twenty-three when he died, leaving her the small island of Ely as her widow's portion. She lived alone on it five years.

She was given in marriage again, against her will, at twenty-eight, to the boy king Egfrith of Northumbria, then fifteen. She insisted again on the condition; he agreed. The agreement held twelve years. As Egfrith grew into manhood he began to want it set aside, appealed to Bishop Wilfrid of York to release her vow, and was refused. Wilfrid, the chronicler says, would not lie to the king for the king's marriage. Etheldreda finally walked out of the Northumbrian court with two attendants and made her way south to her aunt Ebba's abbey at Coldingham. The king pursued her with a war-band. She took refuge on a hill on the Northumbrian coast called *Colbert's Head*; the tide rose, the chronicler says, and held high for seven days, surrounding the hill, and the king retreated. She returned to Ely a free woman in 672.

She founded a double monastery there in 673 on the island that had been her dower, and governed it seven years. She built the first stone church on the island, the foundation of what would later be Ely Cathedral. She kept the strict rule she had longed for: one meal a day, wool habit on the skin, the night office stood through, no fire in her own cell even in the fenland winter. She bore patiently through her last year a swelling on her neck — the result, she said, of jewels of gold she had worn as a young queen, now repaid. She died of plague at Ely, June 23, 679, at perhaps forty-three. Sixteen years later her sister and successor Sexburga had her body translated to a Roman sarcophagus that had floated up out of the river; the body, the *Vita* says, was incorrupt, and the swelling on the neck had healed.

> *"I have been a queen of one realm; let me be a serving-woman of a better."* — Etheldreda, on returning to Ely

## June 24

# John the Baptist

*Forerunner of the Lord, prophet of the wilderness, c. 5 B.C.–c. A.D. 30*

The AASS opens with the formula *DE SANCTO IOANNE PRODROMO ET BAPTISTA D. N. IESU CHRISTI* — *of Saint John, Forerunner and Baptist of our Lord Jesus Christ.* The two Greek titles are the two halves of his vocation. *Prodromos* is what runs ahead, the herald who clears the road. *Baptistēs* is what plunges under, the one who lowers the body into the water and lifts it back. He did one work in two motions. He went ahead. He took down.

He had been the late-born only son of two old people in the hill country of Judah, and grew up, the gospels suggest, mostly in the Judean wilderness. *And the child grew, and waxed strong in spirit, and was in the deserts till the day of his shewing unto Israel.* He came out around the year 27 or 28, in the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar, and began to preach a baptism of repentance at the lower Jordan crossings.

The crowds were enormous. Pharisees and Sadducees, tax-collectors and soldiers came down from Jerusalem and Galilee to be dipped under in the cold green river by the man in the camel-hair tunic. The preaching was severe — *O generation of vipers, who hath warned you to flee from the wrath to come?* He preached the imminence of the kingdom; he preached the messiah whose sandals he was not worthy to unloose. When the messiah walked down to the river himself, John knew him, tried to refuse, and was insisted on. He lowered him into the water and lifted him out, and the heavens opened.

The end was political. Herod Antipas had divorced his wife to take his brother's wife Herodias; John had named the act adultery; Herodias had wanted his head ever since. Antipas imprisoned him at Machaerus. The night of the king's birthday Herodias's daughter danced for the guests; the king, drunk, swore to give her anything; the mother whispered the answer; the head came in on a silver platter. John was perhaps thirty-two.

The church keeps his nativity at midsummer, six months before Christmas — *the calendar made so,* Augustine writes, *because the light has reached its peak and from this day must lessen.* The man who said *he must increase, but I must decrease* gave the day the shape of his own teaching.

> *"He must increase, but I must decrease."* — John, of Christ, *John* 3:30

## June 25

# Prosper of Aquitaine

*Layman theologian of grace, c. 390–455*

He was a layman his whole life. He was born at Limoges of a Gallo-Roman family of moderate property, educated in the rhetorical schools of southern Gaul, and married a wife whose name has not survived. He was already in his forties when he encountered, in Provence, the writings of an obscure African bishop named Augustine of Hippo on the questions then most acutely vexing the Western church: whether grace was given according to merits, whether the will was free in the way the Pelagians said, whether a Christian could even begin to want God without God first wanting him. He read everything Augustine had written. He read it, the chronicler says, with the joy of a man who had been looking for a sentence and had found a library.

He began to write back. He composed verse paraphrases of Augustine's prose so that the doctrine could be memorized. He compiled the *Sentences from Augustine*, three hundred and ninety-two of them, on grace and predestination, that became the standard handbook of Augustinian doctrine in the West for the next eight centuries. He carried on a correspondence with Augustine himself in the last decade of the African bishop's life; his second letter to Augustine, sent in 429, is what occasioned Augustine's last great work, *De Praedestinatione Sanctorum*. When Augustine died in 430 with the Vandals at the gates of Hippo, Prosper became the principal Latin defender of his theology in the West for a quarter of a century, against the Semi-Pelagian objections of Cassian, Vincent of Lérins, and the monks of southern Gaul.

He went to Rome about 440 and entered the chancery of Pope Leo the Great as a notary, an unusual post for a layman. He helped Leo draft the *Tome* of 449 to Flavian of Constantinople — the document the Council of Chalcedon would receive as defining the two natures of Christ. He wrote the *Chronicle* that completed Jerome's, continuing world history down to 455. He may have drafted, anonymously, the *Indiculus de gratia Dei* that Pope Celestine I issued. He died at Rome about 455. He was never canonized in the formal Roman sense, but the Bollandists, recognizing the depth of his Augustinian witness and the inseparability of his name from the orthodox tradition on grace, gave him his AASS day on June 25.

> *"As we pray, so we believe."* — Prosper, *Capitula on Grace* — the source of the axiom *lex orandi, lex credendi*

## June 26

# Anthelm of Belley

*Carthusian prior and bishop, 1107–1178*

He was born of the noble house of Chignin in Savoy, and was a canon of Geneva and provost of Belley — two comfortable benefices held simultaneously — when, at thirty, on a hunting trip in the Chartreuse mountains, he stopped at the Carthusian motherhouse founded by Bruno of Cologne. He stayed three days. He went home and arranged his affairs. He returned within months, asked for the habit, and was received as a novice at the *Grande Chartreuse*. He was made prior of the house at thirty-two, the seventh in succession to Bruno, and he governed it twenty years.

He rebuilt the *Grande Chartreuse* after an avalanche in 1132 had destroyed the original foundation. He turned the loose confederation of Carthusian houses — until then each a private foundation under its prior — into a unified order with regular General Chapters, the first in 1140. He drafted what would become the *Consuetudines* of the order, the customary that gave the Carthusians a constitutional form they have kept eight hundred years. He founded the women's Carthusian branch by accepting Beatrice of Ornacieux's Prébayon community in 1145. He retired from the priorate to be a simple monk again in 1152, was forced out by the pope in 1163 to take the see of Belley as bishop, and accepted only on the condition that he keep his Carthusian habit, his Carthusian fast, and a Carthusian cell — built for him in the cathedral close — to which he could retire each evening when the pastoral hours of the day were done.

He governed Belley fifteen years. He defended the rights of the Lyonnais clergy against the imperial party in the schism between Pope Alexander III and the antipopes of Frederick Barbarossa, even refusing the emperor's safe-conduct when summoned to the imperial diet. He intervened personally in the case of Count Humbert III of Savoy when the count had unjustly imprisoned a priest, and laid the count's lands under interdict until the priest was freed. He died at Belley in his cell, June 26, 1178. The Savoyards held him long after — until the Reformation — as the bishop who had refused to leave his Carthusian habit behind.

> *"The mitre is a borrowed thing; the habit is what they will bury me in."* — Anthelm, on accepting Belley

## June 27

# Ladislaus I of Hungary

*King and lawgiver of the Magyars, 1040–1095*

He was the second son of King Béla I of Hungary, born in exile at the Polish court at Krakow, where his father had taken refuge during the long disputed succession of the Árpád dynasty. He returned to Hungary as a boy when his father took the throne in 1060, and in the bitter dynastic wars that followed his father's death he served as duke under his elder brother Géza I against their cousin Solomon. The kingdom was unstable. The Magyars were two generations Christian and not yet entirely Christian. The Cumans and Pechenegs raided the eastern marches almost every year. Ladislaus took the crown on his brother's death in 1077, at thirty-seven, and held it eighteen years.

He was, the chronicler Hartvic says, six feet four inches tall, of immense physical strength, gentle of speech, and a man whom his soldiers obeyed because he was not afraid to be among them. He fought three campaigns against the Cumans and broke the back of their raiding power; the legend of Ladislaus tearing a captured Hungarian girl out of a Cuman warrior's saddle and killing the warrior with his bare hands at the battle of Kerlés in 1068 (which took place under his brother but in which he led the Hungarian wing) was painted on the walls of half the parish churches of medieval Hungary for the next four centuries. He chartered the synod of Szabolcs in 1092, which gave Hungary its first written ecclesiastical law: severe on simony, severe on married priests, severe on the residual pagans, but compassionate on debtors and on the protection of widows.

He canonized his great-grandfather Stephen, the first Christian king of Hungary, in 1083, together with Stephen's son Emeric and the eleventh-century bishop Gerard of Csanád — the act by which he gave the Hungarian church its own native sanctoral. He prepared a crusade in 1095, having been elected commander by an alliance of Polish, Bohemian, and German princes, but died at Nyitra on July 29 — not June — of an illness on the road north. The AASS day moved his commemoration to June 27 to disentangle him from the high-summer Hungarian saints. He was canonized in 1192. He had been the king his people remembered as *Szent László*, holy László, the one who had carried both the sword and the law for them at once.

> *"Let the law be written, that even the king may be bound by it."* — Ladislaus, opening the synod of Szabolcs

## June 28

# Irenaeus of Lyons

*Bishop, theologian of the Tradition, c. 130–202*

He was born at Smyrna in Asia Minor, perhaps around 130, of a Greek-speaking Christian household, and as a boy he sat at the feet of the old bishop Polycarp — who had himself sat at the feet of the apostle John. Irenaeus would write, fifty years later, that he could still see the very chair Polycarp used, could recall the way the old man held his hands when he spoke, could repeat what Polycarp had said John had said about the Lord. The whole apostolic chain, in his own mind, was the length of two human lives.

He went west as a missionary, perhaps in his twenties, perhaps with the migration of Greek-speaking traders that established Christian communities along the Rhône valley. He was a priest of Lyons when the persecution of 177 fell, and was on a mission to Rome carrying letters from the prison cell of his bishop Pothinus when the killings happened in his absence. He returned to find his bishop dead, his community decimated, and himself elected to the empty see. He governed Lyons twenty-five years.

He wrote, in those years, the five books *Against Heresies* — the *Adversus Haereses* — which was the first great defense of the apostolic faith against the Gnostic systems then proliferating in the East. He took the Valentinians, the Marcionites, the Carpocratians, the Basilideans system by system, laid them out, refuted them not by speculation but by appeal to two things: the Scriptures, and the chain of bishops who had received the faith from the apostles. He gave the church the doctrine of *recapitulation* — that Christ had summed up all of human history in himself, undoing in his own life what Adam had done — that would run through every later Latin theology of redemption. He preserved the texts of Polycarp and of the *Letter of Clement*. He pleaded with Pope Victor in 190 not to excommunicate the Asian churches over the date of Easter, on the ground that diversity in matters not of faith was not a sin against unity.

He died at Lyons about 202, perhaps a martyr in the persecution of Septimius Severus, perhaps of natural causes — the early sources differ. He had been the first Christian to think in terms of *Tradition*, and the church has never stopped thinking that way since.

> *"The glory of God is a living man; and the life of man consists in beholding God."* — Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* IV.20.7

## June 29

# Peter and Paul

*The two apostles of Rome, d. c. 64–67*

They had quarreled in Antioch. Paul writes about it himself in the letter to the Galatians, plainly and without softening, perhaps fifteen years before either of them died. Peter had come down from Jerusalem, had eaten with the Gentile converts in the manner Paul had taught — until the men from James arrived, and Peter, the senior apostle, drew back from the Gentile table. Paul withstood him to the face. He says so. The collision was sharp. They went on. They had agreed in the end on the substance of the gospel; they had divided the territories — Peter to the circumcision, Paul to the uncircumcision — they had given each other the right hand of fellowship and not gone home enemies.

They came at last to the same city. Peter to Rome by the long way around, through Pontus and the diaspora cities, perhaps by Corinth, arriving in the capital of the empire he had been told once at Caesarea Philippi he would govern in another sense. Paul to Rome under arrest, brought from Caesarea Maritima after appealing his case to Caesar, in chains, by sea, in the winter of 60-61. They lived in the city together for what seems to have been some years before the persecution. Tradition is firm: Peter governed the church of Rome from the gentile community he had inherited from his predecessors; Paul preached from the rented house Acts ends with, the one where he received all who came. They were in Rome together when the city burned in July of 64.

Nero blamed the Christians. The persecution that followed lasted through Nero's last four years. Peter, by the ancient tradition first attested in Tertullian, was crucified at Rome — head downward, at his own request, because he would not be killed in the manner of his Lord — at the foot of the Vatican hill, in or near the circus of Caligula and Nero. They buried him on the slope above. Paul, a Roman citizen, was not crucified but beheaded — by tradition, at the third milestone on the Ostian Way, at a place called Aquae Salviae. The two basilicas at Saint Peter's and Saint Paul Outside the Walls stand over the two graves.

The church has kept their feast on one day from very early. The argument in Antioch was real; the unity in Rome was real; the church has needed both.

> *"He withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed."* — Paul of Peter, *Galatians* 2:11

## June 30

# The First Martyrs of the Holy Roman Church

*Killed under Nero, 64*

They are nameless. Tacitus the pagan Roman historian, writing fifty years after, in book fifteen of the *Annals*, gives the longest contemporary account. The great fire of July 64, he says, devastated ten of the fourteen *regiones* of the city of Rome; rumor laid the fire at Nero's door; the emperor, to deflect the rumor, fastened the guilt on the *Chrestiani*, a sect — Tacitus says — already notorious for its *odium humani generis*, its hatred of the human race. *A vast multitude* was arrested. Some were sewn into the skins of wild beasts and torn by dogs in the imperial gardens on the Vatican hill. Some were nailed to crosses. Some were set on fire and burned as living torches to light the chariot races Nero held by night in his own gardens. The emperor mingled in the crowd, Tacitus says, dressed as a charioteer, watching. Even those who hated the *Chrestiani* came at last to pity them, Tacitus writes, because they perceived that the killings were not for the public good but for the cruelty of one man.

The *Chrestiani* were the second generation of the Roman church. They had been baptized by the men whom Peter and Paul had trained. They had heard Peter preach in his last years. Some of them, perhaps, are the names Paul greets in the sixteenth chapter of Romans: Phoebe, Andronicus, Junia, Tryphaena, Tryphosa, Persis the beloved, the household of Aristobulus, the household of Narcissus. Of how many of these survived to the year 64, we do not know. We know only that the church kept a memory of them as the *protomartyres Romani* — the first martyrs of the Roman church — and that the basilica of Saint Peter, whose foundations rest on the very Vatican gardens where the killings took place, is in one sense their monument too.

The church has kept their day on the day after the two great apostles whose city they shared. Peter and Paul on the twenty-ninth, name by name; the nameless on the thirtieth, after them. The Holy Roman Church was not first the see of Peter; it was first these. *A vast multitude*, Tacitus says, and gives no number. The Lamb's book has the count.

> *"A vast multitude was convicted, not so much on the charge of arson as for hatred of the human race."* — Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44
