## May 23

# William of Rochester

*Baker and pilgrim-martyr, d. 1201*

He was a Scottish baker in the town of Perth, of middling prosperity, unmarried into his forties. He had the habit, the chronicler says, of laying aside every tenth loaf from his oven and distributing it to the poor at the door of his shop before he opened for the morning's trade. He was a daily communicant. He had taken at some point a private resolution to walk, before he died, to the holy places — to Canterbury, to Rome, to Jerusalem if he could — and he was waiting only for an occasion to set out.

The occasion came in the form of a foundling. He found a wailing infant at his door one dawn while it was still dark, wrapped in cheap and rough rags, abandoned. He took the child in, had him nursed, and raised him in the baker's art; the boy was known as "foundling David." When the boy was grown and old enough to travel, William believed the moment had come. He took his pilgrim's staff and scrip, the boy beside him, and they set out south for Canterbury.

They had crossed England and reached the outskirts of Rochester in May 1201 when the apprentice — for what cause the chronicler does not name; perhaps the few coins of the alms purse — drew William off the road into a wood, struck him on the back of the head with an axe, and fled. A passing madwoman of the town, who had been wandering the lane, found the body. She set a crown plaited from honeysuckle around the head, in mockery; and the next day, returning, she took the blood-soaked crown from the head and placed it on her own — and in that moment her reason returned. The monks of the cathedral priory, hearing the report, took William's body inside the city walls and buried him in their church.

He was canonized in 1256. He had wanted to walk to Jerusalem. He got as far as a wood outside Rochester, where the road ended and the only mourner was a madwoman who crowned him with wildflowers.
