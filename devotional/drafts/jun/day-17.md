## June 17

# Botulph of Icanho

*East Anglian abbot, d. 680*

He was a Saxon of East Anglia, sent for his education with his brother Adulph to the Frankish monastery of Faremoutiers-en-Brie in the 630s, when the sons and daughters of the Anglo-Saxon nobility were crossing the Channel to learn the rule. He came back to England about 654, was ordained, and was offered land for a foundation by Anna, king of the East Angles. He asked for a place no one wanted. Anna gave him a small bare promontory in the marshes called *Icanho* — *ox-island*, a high-tide islet at the mouth of what is now the Alde. The chronicler says the place was haunted; the country people would not pasture even their oxen on it after sunset.

He built his oratory there in 654 and slept in it the first night, the chronicler says, while the demons of the place wailed around the thatch until dawn. He stayed. Within a generation he had drained the salt-marshes into tillable ground. He grew a community of perhaps thirty monks who kept the rule he had learned at Faremoutiers — Columbanian with a Benedictine softening, severe in the night office, generous in hospitality. He was sought out as a teacher by every house that could spare a brother to send him.

He was famous in his own century for one thing: hospitality at the rim of the sea. Icanho stood on the road by which every traveler from the Frankish kingdoms to East Anglia made landfall. He gave every arrival a meal, a bed, and a blessing for the journey inland. Travelers built small shrines to him at the gates of their towns as a thanksgiving for safe arrival. By the high middle ages gate-shrines to him stood across England; several survive in the City of London, among them the parish of *Saint Botolph without Aldgate*.

He died at Icanho, June 17, 680, having ruled there twenty-six years. The Danes burned the monastery in 870. The bones, hidden the night of the raid, were translated three times in three centuries and finally divided among Bury, Ely, and Westminster.

> *"Be not, my son, disturbed: for God is able to restore all to us."* — Botulph, when his last loaves had been given to the poor
