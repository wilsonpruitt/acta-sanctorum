## October 11

# Bruno the Great

*Archbishop of Cologne, brother of an emperor, scholar-prince, 925–965*

He was the youngest son of Henry the Fowler, the Saxon duke who had been elected king of the East Franks in 919 and whose sons would build the empire of the Ottos. Otto I, who was to be crowned emperor at Rome in 962, was thirteen years older; Henry of Bavaria was nine years older; Bruno was the fourth child, the dynastic spare, and his parents — perhaps his mother Mathilda, who had her own Saxon piety — set him aside for the church when he was four years old. He went to the cathedral school at Utrecht at six. He stayed in school for the next twenty-five years.

What he became was the first great scholar-prince of the Ottonian renaissance. He read Greek — not just letters but the philosophers and the Cappadocians; he assembled the largest private library north of the Alps in his own day; he kept up a correspondence with the literary luminaries of the time. When Otto, having beaten the Hungarians at Lechfeld in 955, decided he needed a brother in the see of Cologne to anchor the Rhineland, Bruno was appointed archbishop in 953, at twenty-eight, and was simultaneously named duke of Lotharingia, the long border province from the North Sea down to the Vosges. He held both offices for twelve years and held them well.

He kept his cathedral school open seven days a week to anyone who could read; he reformed the Rhenish abbeys with the new Cluniac discipline coming up from Burgundy; he patronized the manuscript ateliers that produced the great Ottonian gospel-books of Reichenau and Echternach; he settled three civil wars in his own duchy without resort to arms. He wrote, in the long winter evenings, lives of his predecessors in the Cologne see, and at his death his secretary Ruotger — who had served him for eleven years — wrote his life back. The *Vita Brunonis* by Ruotger is one of the great documents of the tenth century, gracious and unsparing.

He died at Reims on the way back from putting down a Lotharingian rebellion, on the eleventh of October, 965, at forty years old. The fourth son the family had given to the church returned to the family the order it had needed: a Latin prince who had been first a scholar, who held both crozier and sword for twelve years and never let either eclipse the other, and whose library was the quiet thing the empire had not known it required.

> *"Sapientia ædificavit sibi domum."* — *the verse Ruotger took for the epitaph: Wisdom has built herself a house*
