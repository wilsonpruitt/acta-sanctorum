## October 6

# Bruno of Cologne

*Founder of the Carthusians, the silent reformer, c. 1030–1101*

He was born into a noble Cologne family, sent south for his schooling at Reims, and stayed for thirty years — first as student of the cathedral school, then as its master, then as its director. He taught grammar, dialectic, theology; the men he trained included the future Pope Urban II, who would never forget him. In 1075 he was elected chancellor of the see of Reims under Archbishop Manasses, and in that office he discovered, slowly and with horror, that his archbishop was openly simoniacal — selling ordinations, appointments, dispensations — and that the diocesan administration had become a market. He spent five years pressing for reform from inside, was driven out of the city when Manasses had him exiled, was vindicated when the council of Autun deposed the archbishop in 1080, was offered the see itself, and refused it.

He was past fifty by then. He had decided, while in exile, that his life would not run any longer in the cathedral world. With six companions he walked south through the autumn of 1084 to ask the bishop of Grenoble — Hugh, his old student — for a wild place in the mountains where they could live as hermits under a common rule. Hugh took them up to a high alpine valley above the Guiers Mort river, four thousand feet in the air, snowbound seven months a year, with a rim of cliffs all around it. *Cartusia,* the place was called: the Charterhouse. They built seven small cells around a central church, kept silence except in choir, ate one meal a day, took their work alone in their cells, met only for the night office and the chapter of faults. The form they invented — neither cenobitic nor purely eremitic but a middle thing — survived them. The Carthusians have kept the same rule for nine hundred years, with so few changes that the saying *Cartusia numquam reformata, quia numquam deformata* — *never reformed, because never deformed* — has been taken into proverbs.

Bruno would not have liked the proverb. He was modest under praise, humble in office. In 1090 his old student Urban II called him to Rome as papal advisor; he came reluctantly, served briefly, refused the archbishopric of Reggio Calabria, withdrew further south to a wild Calabrian valley where he founded a second Charterhouse at La Torre. He died there on the sixth of October, 1101.

He left no rule in writing, no formal life. What he left was the silence the order has kept since. The cathedral master who would not be archbishop went up the mountain and built seven cells, and nine hundred years later they are still being lived in.

> *"O bonitas!"* — *the only saying his disciples preserved of him, his frequent ejaculation in choir: O goodness!*
