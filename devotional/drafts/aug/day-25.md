## August 25

# Louis IX of France

*King and crusader, 1214–1270*

He was born in 1214 at Poissy and called himself, ever afterward, *Louis de Poissy* — Louis of the baptismal font — preferring the place where he had been made a Christian to the place where he had been born a king. His father Louis VIII died when he was eleven. His mother Blanche of Castile governed France as regent for nine years, fending off the great baronial coup of 1226 with the same firm hand she had used to teach her son the Pater Noster. She told him, the chronicler Joinville records, that she would rather see him dead before her than knowingly commit a mortal sin. The line shaped him.

He took up the cross in December 1244 after a near-fatal illness; recovered, he prepared the Seventh Crusade for three years. He landed at Damietta in June 1249 and took the city without resistance. The campaign in the Nile delta the next year went badly. He was defeated at Mansurah, then captured. He was ransomed for the city of Damietta and a sum the kingdom had to borrow against. He spent the next four years in the Holy Land at Acre, rebuilding the Latin fortifications and negotiating with the Mamluks, while his mother died in his absence — the news reached him in 1252.

He returned to France in 1254. The next sixteen years were the years that made him *Saint Louis*. He revised the kingdom's law-codes through the *Établissements*. He instituted royal investigators — *enquêteurs* — who could ride into any seneschalcy and hear complaints against the king's own officials. He gave justice in person, sitting under the famous oak of Vincennes, where every Frenchman with a grievance could come to him directly. He founded the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris to house the Crown of Thorns and the True Cross relics he had bought from the impoverished Latin Emperor of Constantinople. He kept the Friday fast, washed the feet of the poor weekly in Lent, and refused, the AASS records, to laugh at jokes told at the expense of clerics.

He took the cross again in 1267. He was fifty-three, his health was failing, and his confessors begged him not to. He went. The Eighth Crusade landed at Tunis in July 1270. Dysentery swept the camp within weeks. The king fell ill on the twenty-fourth of August. He received the last rites on a bed of ashes spread in the shape of a cross on the floor of his tent — the medieval royal practice of *cendres* — and died on the twenty-fifth. He was fifty-six. The army withdrew. His son Philip III carried the bones home to Saint-Denis. He was canonized in 1297.
