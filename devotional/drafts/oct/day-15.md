## October 15

# Teresa of Ávila

*Discalced Carmelite reformer, Doctor of the Church, 1515–1582*

She was born at Ávila to a family of *converso* descent — her grandfather had been reconciled to the Inquisition in 1485 — and grew up the most charming of nine children in a strict Castilian merchant household. Her mother died when she was twelve. She read romances of chivalry through her teens — *I have come,* she would later tell her sisters, *from many vanities* — and ran away to a Carmelite convent at twenty, partly out of vocation and partly to escape her father's plans for her marriage. The convent at the Encarnación held a hundred and eighty nuns under a relaxed observance; she stayed there for twenty-six years; she was bored for most of them. Her conversion came at thirty-nine, in front of an *Ecce Homo* in a chapel corner — *the very God of my heart,* she writes in the *Vida.* The mystical experiences began in her early forties.

She was forty-seven when she founded the first reformed Carmelite convent — San José of Ávila, four nuns in absolute enclosure under the original Carmelite rule — against the resistance of the diocese, the city council, and her own provincial. She would found sixteen more in the next twenty years. The reform was hard and meant to be hard: discalced, strict poverty, perpetual fasting, no large dowries, no relaxations. She walked or rode mule-back across Castile, La Mancha, Andalusia, Catalonia, and Aragon to plant houses in town after town, in summer heat and winter snow, sometimes sleeping in stables, often arriving at midnight to a building her advance party had not yet been able to make habitable. *Lord,* she said once on the road in the rain, *if this is how you treat your friends, no wonder you have so few.* She wrote, in the same years, the *Vida,* the *Way of Perfection,* the *Foundations,* and the *Interior Castle* — four of the great works of Spanish prose.

The *Interior Castle* — a vision of the soul as a great crystal globe with seven mansions inside it, the king dwelling in the innermost — is the work the church has loved most. She died on the road at Alba de Tormes on the fourteenth of October, 1582 (the calendar reform took effect at midnight; her death-day is dated the fifteenth in the new calendar), at sixty-seven, from exhaustion. *I am a daughter of the church,* she said when she was dying. The seventeen houses she had founded were five hundred within a century. Paul VI made her, in 1970, the first woman Doctor of the Church in fifteen hundred years.

A nun bored for twenty-six years, converted at thirty-nine in front of a painting, founded an order, wrote four masterpieces, and died on a road. The patience that came before was the soil the rest grew from.
