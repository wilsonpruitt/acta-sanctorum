## May 29

# Maximinus of Trier

*Bishop and protector of Athanasius, d. 349*

He came from Aquitaine, from a Christian family at Silly near Poitiers, and was educated in the school of his future predecessor Agroecius at Trier — then the imperial capital of the Gallic provinces, a city of perhaps eighty thousand, the residence of Constantine and afterward of Constans. He succeeded Agroecius as bishop about 332 and held the see for some seventeen years. He was a man of the imperial court without being a man of the imperial party. He was for that reason a useful refuge.

In 336 Athanasius of Alexandria, exiled by Constantine for the first time, was sent to Trier and entrusted to Maximinus. The bishop received him not as a prisoner but as a fellow patriarch, gave him quarters in his own house, walked with him in the gardens above the Moselle, and discussed the Nicene faith with him through the two years of his exile. When Athanasius was at last permitted to return to Egypt in 337, he carried with him into the East the lifelong friendship of the Gallic bishop and the confidence — confirmed everywhere in his later writings — that the Latin West stood with Nicaea. The synod of Sardica in 347, which Maximinus attended, was the public face of what his hospitality had quietly built.

He defended the Trinitarian faith at every council he attended and refused to sign the Arianizing creeds that the Eastern bishops pressed. When the emperor Constans, the western Augustus, leaned toward orthodoxy partly under Maximinus's influence, the bishop's name became a curse in the Arian camp. He died around 349, before the storm of Constantius's hegemony fully broke, and was buried in the church of Saint John outside the Trier walls.

He had no eloquence the centuries preserved, no treatise, no martyrdom. He had a guest-room. The Nicene faith of the Latin West owes more to that guest-room than to a dozen treatises.

> *Maximinus the Bishop of Trier is held famous: by whom Athanasius, Bishop of Alexandria, when sought by Constantius for punishment, was honorably received.* — Jerome, *Chronicle*
