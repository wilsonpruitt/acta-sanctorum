## April 15

# Hunna of Strasbourg

*Holy washerwoman, d. 679*

She was the wife of a Frankish nobleman named Huno of Strasbourg, a Christian lord with lands along the Rhine, and she was rich enough to live in any manner she chose. She chose laundry.

Strasbourg in the seventh century was a city the way most early medieval cities were a city: a Roman stone core surrounded by wooden houses and yards full of refugees, day laborers, soldiers' widows, unwed mothers, beggars. The poor of the city went without clean clothes for the same reason they went without clean water: it was work no one was paid to do for them, and they could not do it for themselves. Hunna decided to do it for them. She kept a wash-house at one of the family properties on the river. She sent her servants for the dirty clothes of the poor, washed them with her own hands beside her servants, dried them, and had them returned. The people of Strasbourg, who knew exactly whose hands were at the wash-tub, gave her the nickname that the Bollandists kept: *Hunna die heilige Wäscherin*, Hunna the holy washerwoman.

She took special care of the unwed mothers, whom no one else would help, and of the old men of the streets whose clothes had gone beyond what any servant would touch. She kept this up for the better part of forty years. Her husband Huno supported the work without ever joining it; their only son Deodatus eventually entered the abbey of Ebersmünster and became a monk. Hunna died in old age, around 679, and was buried at the parish church of Hunabach near Strasbourg.

Her local cult was so persistent that the bishop of Strasbourg, in 1520 — eight and a half centuries after her death — formally petitioned Rome for her canonization. Leo X granted it. She is one of the very few saints whose canonization rested on the testimony of generation after generation of women who had needed clean clothes and remembered who had brought them.

> *"What the poor cannot do for themselves, somebody must do for them."* — the chronicle on Hunna's work
