## June 23

# Etheldreda of Ely

*East Anglian queen and abbess, c. 636–679*

She was a daughter of Anna, king of the East Angles, and one of four sisters who all became saints. She was given in marriage at perhaps sixteen to Tondberct, a chief of the South Gyrwe in the fenland country south of Ely. She had asked, and he had agreed, that the marriage be celibate. He kept the agreement; she was twenty-three when he died, leaving her the small island of Ely as her widow's portion. She lived alone on it five years.

She was given in marriage again, against her will, at twenty-eight, to the boy king Egfrith of Northumbria, then fifteen. She insisted again on the condition; he agreed. The agreement held twelve years. As Egfrith grew into manhood he began to want it set aside, appealed to Bishop Wilfrid of York to release her vow, and was refused. Wilfrid, the chronicler says, would not lie to the king for the king's marriage. Etheldreda finally walked out of the Northumbrian court with two attendants and made her way south to her aunt Ebba's abbey at Coldingham. The king pursued her with a war-band. She took refuge on a hill on the Northumbrian coast called *Colbert's Head*; the tide rose, the chronicler says, and held high for seven days, surrounding the hill, and the king retreated. She returned to Ely a free woman in 672.

She founded a double monastery there in 673 on the island that had been her dower, and governed it seven years. She built the first stone church on the island, the foundation of what would later be Ely Cathedral. She kept the strict rule she had longed for: one meal a day, wool habit on the skin, the night office stood through, no fire in her own cell even in the fenland winter. She bore patiently through her last year a swelling on her neck — the result, she said, of jewels of gold she had worn as a young queen, now repaid. She died of plague at Ely, June 23, 679, at perhaps forty-three. Sixteen years later her sister and successor Sexburga had her body translated to a Roman sarcophagus that had floated up out of the river; the body, the *Vita* says, was incorrupt, and the swelling on the neck had healed.

> *"I deservedly bear in my neck the weight of an ailment, in which, as a young girl, I bore the superfluous weights of necklaces."* — Etheldreda, on her last illness
