## October 23

# Severinus of Cologne

*Gallo-Roman bishop on the Rhenish frontier, c. 340–c. 403*

He was born in Aquitaine and sent north as a young presbyter to the Rhine frontier in the 360s, when Valentinian I was rebuilding the limes and the Roman administration of the Rhineland was still standing, just barely. He succeeded Euphrates as bishop of Cologne sometime around 370, perhaps in his early thirties, and held the see for the next thirty-three years.

What he inherited was a frontier church under siege. The Cologne diocese ran from the small Roman colony at *Colonia Agrippina* north along the lower Rhine to the Frisian marshes, and east into the German forests where the Franks and the Allemanni were beginning the slow westward pressure that would, within his lifetime, push across the Rhine for good. The Christian community Severinus inherited had been founded perhaps two generations earlier, was perhaps two thousand strong in a city of forty thousand, and was hemmed in by an Arian Gothic minority on one side and a still-pagan Frankish military presence on the other. He governed it for thirty years with a steady, unspectacular, practical capacity. He built the basilica of SS. Cornelius and Cyprian on the south side of the city. He resettled the small church on the north side that the Frankish raids of 388 had burned. He buried, when no other bishop would come, the Christian dead of the great Frankish raid of 392. He kept the cathedral school open through years when the supply of papyrus from the south was unreliable.

The *Vita* preserves the small detail Gregory of Tours preserves first: that Severinus was at the office in his cathedral one Sunday morning in November of 397 when, at the moment of the *agnus Dei,* he stopped, looked up, and said audibly to his archdeacon, *Listen — do you hear the angels singing? My brother Martin of Tours is dead.* The archdeacon, who wrote the report down for the cathedral chronicle, marked the day. The news that Martin had died at Candes that morning reached Cologne by post-courier two weeks later. The dating matched.

He died perhaps in 403 and was buried in the basilica he had built. The basilica that grew out of the cult of his tomb, *Sankt Severin* in the south of the old city, is still there, a fourteenth-century Gothic church on Roman foundations on Carolingian foundations on the original tomb-shrine. The Cologne diocese he had governed survived the Roman collapse, survived Charlemagne, survived the Reformation, and is one of the oldest continuously functioning dioceses in northern Europe. The Aquitanian who had been sent up to a frontier see for thirty years had, simply, kept it.

> *"Audi: angeli canunt. Frater meus Martinus migravit ad Dominum."* — Severinus, in the cathedral at Cologne, on the morning of Martin's death
