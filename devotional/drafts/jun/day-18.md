## June 18

# Elizabeth of Schönau

*Benedictine visionary of the Rhineland, 1129–1164*

She was a Rhineland girl from a modest free family, given to the Benedictine double monastery of Schönau in the Nassau hills at the age of twelve, and never left. She took her vows at eighteen. She became prioress of the women's community at twenty-eight. She was a small, quiet, sickly woman, the chronicler says, of unremarkable conversation, who served the meals and copied the choir books and was assumed by everyone in the cloister to be exactly what she appeared to be.

She began to receive visions at twenty-three. They came at first only at the Office, only on the great feasts, lasting a few minutes, and she told no one. By her late twenties they had spread through every Hour and to ordinary days; she fell into trance in choir, in the cloister, at table. She resisted writing them down for years until her brother Egbert, a Benedictine monk at the same house and later abbot of it, insisted. He took her dictation. The three *Books of Visions* that resulted, together with the *Book of the Ways of God* and the visionary expansion of the *Acts of Saint Ursula and the Eleven Thousand Virgins*, made her, within her own lifetime, the second most-read woman writer in twelfth-century Latin Europe, after her older friend and correspondent Hildegard of Bingen.

She and Hildegard exchanged ten years of letters that survive. They were strikingly different visionaries. Hildegard saw cosmic structures, the wheels of creation, the proportions of grace. Elizabeth saw scenes — the Virgin at the cradle, the angel at the empty tomb, the saints in their proper feasts — and her letters back to Hildegard treat her own gift with a kind of apologetic wonder, as if she could not understand why she of all people should be made to see these things. She was bedridden the last seven years of her life from the toll of the trances. She died at Schönau, June 18, 1164, at thirty-six. Egbert, who outlived her by twenty years, edited her writings and never claimed for them what she would not claim. The texts she had thought too small for the cloister are still in print eight hundred years later.

> *"By the grace of God I am what I am. Let not the greatness of the revelations exalt me."* — Elizabeth, awaking from a vision on the feast of John the Baptist
