## January 29

# Gildas

*British monk and historian, c. 500–570*

He grew up in post-Roman Britain — what the English would later call Wales — at the moment when the country was forgetting it had ever been Roman. The villas had been overgrown. The legions had been gone for a century. The Saxon settlers were past the coast and beginning to push inland, and the British kings who should have been fighting them were chiefly fighting each other. Gildas, monk, took the only weapon he was trained for, which was Latin prose, and wrote *On the Ruin and Conquest of Britain*.

The book is famous and unloved. He names the British kings he holds responsible for the disaster — Constantine the tyrant whelp of unclean Damnonia, Aurelius Caninus, Vortipor the tyrant of the Demetae, Cuneglas the fawn of the rust-stained den, Maelgwn the dragon of the island — and he prints their crimes. He turns then to the clergy and prints theirs. He preaches at the entire surviving British Church for several chapters and refuses to apologize for the volume. He says, with no apparent humor, that he is writing what no one else has been willing to say.

But he had said it, and the book traveled. It crossed to Brittany with him when he founded the monastery at Rhuys, late in life, on the Atlantic. Two centuries later Bede would read it, half believe it, and quote it as the only contemporary witness to a century of British history that left no other written record. The English Reformation read it. Milton read it. It exists because one Welshman, in his last decades, was too angry to keep quiet and too disciplined to lie.

> *"I shall not, I trust, be silent about it any longer."* — Gildas, *De Excidio Britanniae* I
