## June 10

# Margaret of Scotland

*Queen and reformer, c. 1045–1093*

She was born in exile in Hungary, where her father Edward — the Anglo-Saxon prince called *the Exile* — had fled from the Danish conquest of 1016. She was raised at the court of King Andrew I, learned five languages, read the Latin Fathers, and was returned to England at twelve when her father was called home as Edward the Confessor's heir. He died on the journey. She was twenty-one and a refugee for the second time when, after the Conquest of 1066, her family fled north into a storm in the German Sea, were blown ashore at the Firth of Forth, and were taken into the household of the Scottish king Malcolm III at his court at Dunfermline. He married her three years later. She bore him eight children.

She ruled Scotland through him and around him. She read to him at table — he was illiterate but devout, the chronicler says, and would kiss the books she read from. She brought English chaplains and Benedictine monks to a court that had been culturally Gaelic and ecclesiastically Celtic since the days of Columba. She convened church councils at Dunfermline that aligned Scottish observance — Lenten dating, communion frequency, marriage law — with the broader Western pattern. She founded the abbey of Dunfermline, restored the abbey of Iona that had been wrecked by Norse raids, established the ferry across the Firth of Forth for pilgrims to Saint Andrews (a service still called *Queensferry*), and rebuilt the chapel at Edinburgh Castle that is now the oldest building in the city.

She was the household manager of Scotland. She fed nine orphaned infants from her own spoon each morning, and she and the King served three hundred poor together in the royal court. She washed the feet of six poor every morning in Advent and Lent. She kept the long fasts so strictly that her health failed in her late forties. In November 1093 her husband Malcolm and her eldest son Edward rode south on a raid into Northumberland; both were killed at Alnwick. The news reached her at Edinburgh Castle on November 16. She had been bedridden for weeks. She received the last rites, asked for her *Black Rood* — the small reliquary cross she had brought from Hungary — and died holding it within four days of the news. She was forty-eight. The Scots translated her relics later to the abbey of Dunfermline, on the AASS-traditional day, June 10.

> *"Lord Jesus Christ, who by the will of the Father, with the Holy Spirit cooperating, through your death have given life to the world: free me."* — Margaret's last words, as she received the Viaticum
