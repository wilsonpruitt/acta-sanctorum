## February 25

# Walburga of Heidenheim

*Anglo-Saxon abbess in Germany, c. 710–779*

She was a Wessex princess, niece of Boniface, sister of Willibald and Winibald — a family that gave the eighth century four of its great missionary saints. She had been educated at the great abbey of Wimborne in Dorset, taken the veil there, and lived the quiet life of an English nun for about twenty-five years when Boniface, deep into the German mission, wrote home asking for women to help train the wives and daughters of his new converts. Walburga, perhaps forty, volunteered. She sailed in 748.

The crossing nearly killed her. A storm broke over the small boat in the North Sea and the sailors were terrified; Walburga, the chronicler says, knelt on the deck and prayed, and the storm eased almost in the next moment. She came ashore at Antwerp and walked the rest of the way to Mainz. Boniface and her brother Winibald placed her at the double monastery they were building at Heidenheim — men under Winibald, women under another abbess, joint house under the same Rule. When Winibald died in 761, Walburga was made the head of both houses. She governed them for the next eighteen years.

She was literate in three languages — Anglo-Saxon, Latin, German — and the only writer in the family. She composed in Latin the Lives of her brothers Willibald (his account of his pilgrimage to the Holy Land is the earliest English travel narrative we have) and Winibald, and her own letters are among the first writings by an Englishwoman that survive. She is the first English writer of either sex whose name we know.

She died at Heidenheim at sixty-nine. Her relics were translated to Eichstätt a century later and began to exude a sweet liquid — *Walpurgis oil* — which the local Christians collected for healing.

> *"The Lord is in the storm and after the storm; he is not bound to either."* — Walburga, on her crossing
