## March 9

# Frances of Rome

*Roman matron, mystic, 1384–1440*

She was born into a wealthy Roman family at the beginning of the Great Schism and married off at twelve to Lorenzo Ponziani, son of another wealthy Roman family, who turned out to be the best gift God had given her — a husband who let her live her vocation. Frances had wanted the cloister; her parents had refused. She made her peace with the marriage by deciding that whatever her husband would let her do for the poor of Rome, she would do.

She did a great deal. Her sister-in-law Vannozza became her closest friend; the two of them visited the sick together, distributed the family's grain in famine years, walked the streets at night to bring food to the destitute, and emptied the linen presses to clothe them. When Lorenzo, suspecting nothing of the inventory, asked one winter where his winter cloaks had gone, Frances answered without looking up: *they are warmer on the poor.* Lorenzo, who loved her, said no more.

She bore six children; two died in plague. She survived the sack of Rome by the Neapolitan condottieri; the family fled, the house was looted, Lorenzo went into exile, and Frances remained in the ruined household to run it for the children. When at last he returned, broken in health, she nursed him for the last years of his life. She kept seeing — and her confessor finally believed her, after years of testing — her guardian angel: a small boy in white, walking just before her with a lamp, lighting her way through the streets of Rome at night.

After Lorenzo's death she joined the community of Oblates she had founded at Tor de' Specchi. She died at fifty-six, in the house of her son, having spent half her life leaving God at the altar to find him in the kitchen.

> *"It is most laudable in a married woman to be devout, but she must never forget that she is a housewife. Sometimes she must leave God at the altar to find him in her housekeeping."* — Frances of Rome
