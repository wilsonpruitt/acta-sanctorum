## January 1

# Odilo of Cluny

*Abbot, founder of All Souls' Day, c. 962–1049*

A pilgrim from Auvergne, shipwrecked on a small island near Sicily, was told by a local hermit that the rocks beyond their cove opened at certain hours into fissures of flame, and that voices could be heard from them — demons complaining that souls were being torn from their grip by the prayers and Masses of Odilo and the monks of Cluny. The pilgrim made his way home, and then to Souvigny, and told the abbot the story.

Odilo did not boast. He commanded. The next year, in every monastery of his order, on the day after the feast of All Saints, the whole community gathered to celebrate the Eucharist, sing the psalms, and distribute bread to the poor on behalf of all the faithful departed. He did not invent the prayers; the Church had prayed for the dead since the catacombs. He gave them a fixed day. The practice spread out of Cluny across Europe, and in time the universal Church received what Odilo had begun.

The chroniclers remembered him for other things. Riding near Paris, he once came upon two children dead of cold in the road, unburied and unmourned. He dismounted, stripped the wool from his back to cover them, dug their graves, and rode on. During the famine years he broke up the silver vessels of his abbey for bread — even melted the imperial crown that Henry had given him — judging it unworthy to deny the poor what Christ had been killed for. And when a leprous priest from Le Puy withdrew to a hut by the Loire and asked through an intermediary to see him, Odilo came at once: the brothers watched in alarm as the abbot took the man into his arms and held him there, in the long conversation of friends. He loved, the chronicler wrote, the prerogative of nature in a man more than he dreaded the approach of disease.

What Odilo gave the Church was a calendar that confessed what every grieving parishioner already half-believed: that the dead are not lost to us, and the prayers of the living still carry. Today, wherever a son or daughter kneels for the parents they have buried, may the words still rise: *Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them.*

> *"I prefer to be mercifully judged for mercy than cruelly condemned for cruelty."* — Odilo
