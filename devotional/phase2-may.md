## May 1

# Sigismund of Burgundy

*King, martyr, d. 524*

He was the son of Gundobad, king of the Burgundians, an Arian who had built the kingdom out of the wreckage of the Western Empire on the upper Rhône. Sigismund grew up at his father's court an Arian like his father. He converted to Catholic Christianity in his early manhood, under the influence of Avitus of Vienne, and brought the kingdom — gradually, against resistance from his Arian nobles — over with him. He inherited the throne in 516.

He founded the great Burgundian abbey of Saint-Maurice d'Agaune in 515, at the site of the legendary martyrdom of the Theban Legion. He endowed it with royal lands. He instituted there the *laus perennis*, the continuous chant of the divine office by relays of monks who took shifts so that the praise of God never stopped, day or night, for centuries.

He had also made one terrible mistake of his life, which the chroniclers do not soften. His second wife, jealous of his son Sigeric (born of his first marriage), accused the boy of conspiring against him. Sigismund, in a fit of trust in the wrong wife, had the young man strangled. He realized within hours what he had done. The chronicle preserves him lying on his son's body and refusing food, water, or comfort for days. The abbey of Saint-Maurice, in its way, was the rest of his penance — an attempt to build, with the prayer of generations of monks, a sound that might cover what he had done.

The Frankish king Chlodomer invaded Burgundy in 524 and took Sigismund prisoner. He had Sigismund, his second wife, and the two younger children thrown alive down a well at Coulmiers. The bodies were recovered, after Chlodomer's own death in battle three years later, by the abbot of Saint-Maurice and brought back to the abbey for burial.

> *"Forgive me, Lord, that I trusted the wrong voice."* — Sigismund, after the killing of Sigeric

## May 2

# Athanasius of Alexandria

*Patriarch, doctor, c. 296–373*

He was twenty when he attended the Council of Nicaea — a deacon, secretary to his bishop Alexander of Alexandria, the youngest serious voice in the room — and helped draft the language about the Son being *of one substance* with the Father that the council adopted. He was thirty when he succeeded Alexander as patriarch. He was thirty-one when he started being exiled.

There would be five exiles over forty-five years, totaling seventeen years of his episcopate. Constantine sent him to Trier. Constantius sent him to the Egyptian desert (where he hid for six years among the hermits and wrote, in the leisure of his hiding, the *Life of Antony* that would invent Western Christian biography and bring monasticism into the Latin Church). Julian the Apostate exiled him; Valens exiled him. In the gaps between exiles he wrote — the *Discourses Against the Arians*, the *Apology to Constantius*, the *Festal Letters* that fixed the canon of the New Testament, the dozens of sermons and tracts. The Arian party would call a synod to depose him; he would attend, defend himself, be acquitted in one place and condemned in another, exiled again, recalled again. The Latin Church coined a phrase for him: *Athanasius contra mundum*. Athanasius against the world.

He won. The Council of Constantinople in 381, eight years after his death, would ratify the Nicene Creed in its final form and put it in the mouth of every Christian liturgy ever since. The orthodox Christology of fifteen centuries — that the Son is fully God and fully man, not by adoption or appointment but by nature — rests on Athanasius's pen and Athanasius's stubbornness.

He died in his cathedral in Alexandria at seventy-seven, an old patriarch who had outlasted every emperor who had tried to break him.

> *"God became man, so that man might become God."* — Athanasius, *De Incarnatione* 54

## May 3

# Philip and James the Less

*Apostles, first century*

The Western Church keeps them on the same day, May 3, because their relics were brought to the basilica of the Holy Apostles in Rome on the same day in the year 561, and the two men have been remembered together ever since. They had not been particular friends in life; the gospels never name them in the same scene. But the Church keeps them together as it has kept many of the Twelve together: in pairs, because the apostles were sent out two by two and because no apostle can be remembered alone.

Philip was from Bethsaida, the same fishing village on the Sea of Galilee that gave us Peter and Andrew. He was the third disciple called by name in John's gospel. He went immediately to find his friend Nathanael — *we have found him of whom Moses in the Law and the prophets wrote* — and brought him along. He appears again at the feeding of the five thousand, where Jesus tests him with a question he cannot answer. He appears in the upper room at the Last Supper asking Christ to show them the Father — and is told, gently, that he has had the Father in front of him all this time. After the Resurrection he disappears from the gospel record. Tradition gives him a long mission in Phrygia, and he was likely martyred at Hierapolis.

James the Less was the son of Alphaeus and a kinsman, in some way, of the Lord. The early Church often identified him with the James who became bishop of Jerusalem and led the Church there until his martyrdom in the early sixties. He was thrown from the parapet of the Temple, stoned, and finally finished with a fuller's club. He is the author of the small fierce letter at the back of the New Testament that begins *count it all joy*.

> *"Lord, show us the Father, and we shall be satisfied."* — Philip, John 14:8

## May 4

# Monica

*Mother of Augustine, 332–387*

She was a North African Berber, born to a Christian family at Thagaste in what is now Algeria, married off in her teens — without her consent — to a Roman official named Patricius. He was pagan, hot-tempered, and unfaithful. He hit her. Most of her women friends in Thagaste were also hit; she counseled them, with the wry practical wisdom her son would later quote, to remember that the marriage contract had made them servants in their husbands' houses and not to provoke their masters with sharp words. Whatever her husband threw at her, she returned silence and patience. Patricius converted late in his life — broken by her example, the chronicle says, more than by argument — and died a baptized Christian.

She had three children. Two went the way she would have chosen. The third, the gifted one, the favorite, did not. Augustine left Thagaste for Carthage in his teens, fell into the Manichean heresy, took a mistress, fathered a son out of wedlock, and slipped through every prayer his mother could put up. She followed him to Rome. She followed him to Milan. She refused to let him be lost. She wept for him so persistently that the bishop of Carthage said the famous line to her: *the son of so many tears cannot perish*.

He didn't perish. Ambrose of Milan baptized him at the Easter Vigil in 387. Monica saw it. She had won. She told Augustine, on the road home to Africa, that she had nothing left in the world to ask for. They reached Ostia, the port of Rome; they had a long evening conversation about the eternal life of the saints, leaning together on a window looking out at the harbor; she fell ill within days. She told Augustine to bury her wherever was convenient. *Nothing is far from God; he will not have to look for me*. She died at Ostia at fifty-five.

> *"Bury this body anywhere; let nothing of it worry you."* — Monica, dying at Ostia

## May 5

# Hilary of Arles

*Bishop, 401–449*

He was a cousin of Honoratus, the founder of the great island monastery of Lérins off the southern coast of Gaul, and when Honoratus left Lérins to become bishop of Arles in 426, Hilary followed him there. He was twenty-five. He served as Honoratus's deacon for the three years of his cousin's episcopate. When Honoratus died, the Arlesian clergy elected Hilary in his place, against his own preference, and made him bishop at twenty-nine.

He governed the see for twenty years. He was a learned man — he had read the Greek Fathers as well as the Latin — and he gave Arles a serious theological identity it had not had under his predecessors. He preached well enough that copies of his sermons were circulating in Italy within his lifetime. He kept the Lérinian asceticism even as a metropolitan: he ate sparingly, slept little, walked the streets of his city in a plain wool tunic. He insisted that his clergy live more simply than the nobility of the city around them.

He could also be hard. The famous dispute of his episcopate was with Pope Leo the Great, in 444, over Hilary's exercise of metropolitan authority over the bishops of southern Gaul. Hilary had deposed and replaced a bishop in a neighboring diocese without consulting Rome; he had perhaps overstepped, perhaps merely acted as the metropolitans of Gaul had always acted. Leo, who was building the centralized papacy of the next thousand years, intervened. He issued the rescript *Quamquam Patrum traditio*, which curtailed the authority of the see of Arles. Hilary submitted without arguing. He had not wanted the office, and he refused to fight for its privileges once he had it.

He died in 449, forty-eight years old, exhausted. Honoratus's old disciples at Lérins remembered him as the cousin who had carried the work on for two decades after Honoratus could no longer carry it.

> *"What was given me, I have given back; what was taken from me, was not mine."* — Hilary, after the rescript

## May 6

# Petronax of Monte Cassino

*Abbot, c. 670–c. 747*

He was a pilgrim from Brescia, in his forties, on his way through Rome to visit the great Italian shrines. Pope Gregory II received him in audience and asked him, somewhat off-handedly, whether he had ever thought of going on to Monte Cassino. The pope's question was almost a joke. Monte Cassino had been destroyed by Lombard raiders in 581, abandoned ever since, the bones of its founder Benedict carried away to Fleury, the buildings collapsed under one hundred and forty years of weather. There was nothing at Cassino to visit. *The Lord*, the pope said, *might wish someone to live there again.*

Petronax went. He climbed the road up the rock — the Lombards held the surrounding country, the path had not been kept up for a century, the chapel of Benedict's tomb was a roofless ruin — and found that he was not alone. A handful of hermits were living in the caves around the mountain, scattered, with no Rule, knowing only that the place had once been holy. Petronax gathered them. He patched the chapel roof. He brought up other men over the next several years. By 717 there was a working monastery on the rock again.

Pope Gregory II, when the news came down to him, gave Petronax the title *Father of the Monastery* in a formal letter. The Rule of Saint Benedict had begun to be read again at the place where Benedict had written it. Sturm, the future founder of Fulda, came to study under Petronax. Willibald, returning from the Holy Land, stayed years. Carloman, brother of Pepin the Short, abdicated the Frankish throne and entered the abbey as a novice under Petronax in his last years.

Petronax died at perhaps seventy-seven, having restored not a building but a tradition. The mountain has been a working monastery, with interruptions, ever since.

> *"The Lord might wish someone to live there again."* — Gregory II to Petronax, dispatching him to Cassino

## May 7

# John of Beverley

*Bishop, c. 640–721*

He was a Northumbrian, born somewhere in the kingdom of Deira, educated at the great school Theodore of Tarsus and Adrian of Canterbury had founded at Canterbury and then under Abbess Hilda at Whitby. He was, the chronicler says, an exceptionally quiet student — Bede, who knew him in old age and recorded his life, never tires of mentioning his stillness. He was made bishop of Hexham in 687 and translated to York in 705. He governed York for twelve years and resigned at the end of them, weary, to retire to a small monastery he had founded years before at Beverley on the river Hull. He lived there four more years and died.

What Bede preserves of him is gentle. He visited his parishes constantly, on foot, in any weather. He cared particularly for the speech-impaired — *Bede records, with quiet affection*, the story of the young man with a stutter so severe that he had never spoken in his life, whom John kept at his lodgings for a Lent, teaching him to form the sounds one at a time, until on Easter morning the young man could say the alphabet, then the syllables, then his own name. The chronicler does not call it a miracle. He calls it the bishop being patient long enough.

He also ordained the boy Bede deacon, and later priest, at Hexham. Bede never quite stops writing about him with the warmth of a man writing about a teacher he had loved.

He died at Beverley in 721, ninety years old, having ordained two generations of Northumbrian clergy and been quietly responsible — by his patronage of the boy at Jarrow — for what we now read as the standard history of the English Church.

> *"He taught the boy with the stammer to speak by saying nothing himself, but waiting."* — Bede, *Ecclesiastical History* V.2

## May 8

# Peter of Tarentaise

*Cistercian archbishop, 1102–1175*

He was born to a noble family in the Dauphiné, joined the Cistercians at twenty in the fervor of the order's first generation, and was professed under Bernard of Clairvaux's direct guidance. He was made abbot of the Cistercian house at Tamié, high in the Savoyard Alps, in his early thirties. He built a hospice there for the pilgrims and merchants crossing the Little Saint Bernard pass — the hospice that still bears his name and still receives travelers in our day.

In 1142 the chapter of Tarentaise elected him archbishop. The diocese was a wreck. The previous archbishop had been deposed for embezzlement; the cathedral had been pillaged; the local lords had seized half the diocese's lands. Peter accepted reluctantly. He recovered the lands. He restored the cathedral. He founded schools, hospitals, hospices for the dying, two more abbeys. He preached against the new fashion in the Savoyard nobility of usury. He visited every parish in his vast Alpine diocese annually on foot, sleeping in the priests' houses and refusing every offer of a softer bed.

Twelve years into his episcopate he could bear the office no longer. He disappeared. The diocese, alarmed, sent searchers in every direction; he was found six months later working as a kitchen lay-brother in an obscure Swiss abbey of Bonnevaux, with no one yet having recognized him. He was brought back to Tarentaise. He continued his work as archbishop for another twenty years.

In the 1160s he served as mediator between Pope Alexander III and the Emperor Frederick Barbarossa during the schism over Anti-Pope Victor IV. He travelled relentlessly — to France, to Italy, to Switzerland — preaching reconciliation. He was perhaps the only churchman trusted by both sides. The schism eventually closed. He died on the road at Bellevaux Abbey in 1175, seventy-three years old, on yet another peace-making errand.

> *"What we have not given, we have not yet received."* — Peter of Tarentaise, in a sermon

## May 9

# Pachomius the Great

*Founder of cenobitic monasticism, c. 292–348*

He was a young Egyptian conscript in the Roman army of Constantine, taken from his village in the upper Nile in his twenties and marched downriver in chains with the other recruits. He was not a Christian. The army stopped one night at a small town called Esna, where the local Christians came out at evening with food and water for the prisoners — not knowing who they were, not asking what gods they served, only knowing that men in chains needed feeding. Pachomius, who had grown up in a religion that did not feed strangers, watched the bread come into his hand and asked the soldier next to him what the people were doing.

When the war ended and he was discharged, he kept the question. He sought out a Christian community, was baptized, attached himself to a hermit named Palamon, and learned the desert disciplines for seven years. He was thirty. He was praying alone at a place called Tabennesi on a bend of the Nile when, the chronicler says, a voice told him to stay there and build a community. He had not thought of community. The desert tradition he had inherited from Anthony was solitary. He stayed anyway.

What he founded was new. Pachomius proposed that monks live together under a written Rule — fixed hours of prayer, common meals, common work, a hierarchy of superiors. The hermits at Tabennesi were skeptical. His brother Anub, who came down to investigate, became his first disciple. Other men followed. By Pachomius's death, twenty-eight years later, the *koinonia* of Tabennesi was nine monasteries housing perhaps three thousand monks, with his sister Mary running an affiliated women's community across the river.

Athanasius came to visit him once. Cassian would later carry his Rule to the West. Benedict would build on Cassian. Every Christian community on earth that has ever lived under a written common Rule traces, ultimately, to the moment Pachomius decided to stay at Tabennesi.

> *"Stay here and build."* — the voice Pachomius heard at Tabennesi

## May 10

# Antoninus of Florence

*Dominican archbishop, 1389–1459*

He was Antonio Pierozzi, a small thin Florentine boy, sickly enough that his parents had given up hope of his living past childhood and had quietly told the cathedral chapter not to count on him as the family's contribution to the clergy. He surprised them by surviving. At fifteen he asked the reformer John Dominic, then preaching at the cathedral, for the Dominican habit. Dominic looked at the boy, told him to memorize Gratian's *Decretum* and come back when he could recite it, and was startled the next year when Antoninus could. He was admitted to the order.

He spent his early decades in the Observant reform — the strict-observance Dominican movement that had been founded to bring the order back to its first poverty. He governed reform houses at Cortona, Foligno, Naples, Rome, and finally at the great Florentine convent of San Marco, where Fra Angelico was already painting the cells when Antoninus arrived as prior. Cosimo de' Medici, the city's banker-prince, funded the convent's reconstruction. Antoninus thanked him for the building and preached against his usury in the same week.

He was made archbishop of Florence in 1446 over his strenuous objections. He served thirteen years. He walked everywhere; he ate at the same table as his servants; he sold the archbishop's silver in the plague year of 1448 and again in the famine of 1453 and gave the proceeds away. He wrote a great *Summa Moralis* — three volumes on the theology of conscience and the ethics of commerce — that became the standard manual for confessors of merchants for two centuries.

He died at San Marco in 1459, seventy years old. Florence put up a marker that called him *the Counsellor* — the man whom the city's princes, bankers, and beggars had each in their own seasons consulted at the same door.

> *"What the rich man's gold cannot buy, the poor man's prayer obtains."* — Antoninus, in the *Summa Moralis*

## May 11

# Mamertus of Vienne

*Bishop, d. c. 475*

He was a Gallo-Roman bishop in the city of Vienne, on the Rhône south of Lyon, in the years when the Western Empire was visibly disintegrating. The Burgundians had moved into the upper Rhône valley; Vienne was on the southern edge of their kingdom. The fields around Vienne in the 460s were beset by a chain of calamities: late frosts that killed the grain, wildfires in the surrounding forests, an earthquake that brought down part of the basilica, wolves coming into the streets at night from the abandoned villas of the countryside. The Christian community in the city was frightened. The pagan remnant was angry.

On Easter Eve of one of those years, a fire broke out near the basilica during the vigil. Mamertus, vesting in the sacristy, came out to find his city on the edge of panic. He led the people in prayer through the night and the fire turned. The next morning he announced that the Christian community would, beginning that week, hold a three-day public procession of penance — a great litany, sung antiphonally, through the streets and the fields outside the walls — to ask God for mercy on Vienne. The procession was to be held on the three days before the Ascension and would be repeated every year. The clergy walked; the people walked; the sick were carried; the great litany of supplication was chanted at every shrine.

The calamities eased that year. The practice took. Within a generation other Gallic dioceses had adopted it. Within a century the Roman liturgy itself had received it as the *Rogation Days* — the *rogationes*, the askings — to be observed across the Latin Church on the same days each year. It was, perhaps, the only liturgical institution of a Gallo-Roman bishop to become a universal observance of the Western Church.

He had asked his city to ask. They asked. The asking has gone on for fifteen hundred years.

> *"Let us ask. We have not asked."* — Mamertus, instituting the Rogations

## May 12

# Germanus of Constantinople

*Patriarch, iconodule confessor, c. 634–733*

He was the son of a Byzantine senator who was executed by the emperor Constantine IV on a charge of plotting against the throne. The senator's young son was, by long imperial custom, made a eunuch to prevent him ever claiming political power, and was placed in the patriarchal household at Constantinople as a cleric. Germanus grew up there. He became deacon, priest, archdeacon. He was made bishop of Cyzicus on the southern shore of the Sea of Marmara around 705. He was made patriarch of Constantinople in 715. He was eighty when Emperor Leo III the Isaurian, in 726, issued the first imperial edict against the veneration of holy images.

Germanus refused the edict. He wrote a long doctrinal letter to the pope at Rome (Gregory II) defending the practice of icon veneration as consistent with the Christian doctrine of the Incarnation: *He whose face cannot be drawn took our face, and the face that he took can now be drawn*. He wrote to bishops across the East. He preached against the imperial position from his own cathedral. He was, at eighty, the senior bishop of the empire, and the emperor could not openly silence him.

In 730 Leo summoned a *silentium* — a formal imperial council — and demanded that the patriarch sign the edict. Germanus refused. He removed the pallium from his shoulders, set it on the table before the emperor, and walked out of the room. He retired the same week to the monastery of Platanion outside the city, where he had lived as a young deacon, and refused all further public role.

He died at the monastery three years later, ninety-nine years old, having held the patriarchate of New Rome for fifteen years and resigned it for the sake of a face.

> *"He whose face cannot be drawn took our face, and the face that he took can now be drawn."* — Germanus, in a letter to Rome

## May 13

# Servatus of Tongeren

*Bishop, d. 384*

Tradition gives him an Armenian birth and a long journey west — across Syria, Asia Minor, the Balkans, the Alps — that brought him at last, perhaps in his thirties, to the obscure town of Tongeren in the northern reaches of Gaul, where he was made the first bishop of a diocese that had nothing yet but a name. The Roman authority in the region was already thinning. The Frankish settlers in the lowlands were pagan. Servatus served the small Gallo-Roman Christian population that remained in the town for forty years.

He went out twice — to Sardica in 343, and to Rimini in 359 — to defend the Nicene faith against the Arian party that had become powerful in the eastern empire. At Sardica he stood with Athanasius; at Rimini, when the emperor Constantius extorted an Arian-friendly compromise from the assembled bishops, Servatus refused to sign and walked out of the council in protest. He was one of a handful of Western bishops who held their ground that year.

He went also to Rome on pilgrimage. He prayed at the tomb of Peter and, in a vision recorded in his Life, was told that Gaul would soon be overrun by barbarians whom the kingdom could not stop. He returned home and warned the people of Tongeren to flee. They did not. He himself moved his see eastward, across the Meuse, to the safer Roman city of Maastricht. The Huns came over the Rhine fifteen years later. Tongeren was sacked. Maastricht stood.

He died at Maastricht in 384 and was buried in the small church there that has been a pilgrimage shrine ever since. The diocese of Liège, which traces its origins to him, still keeps May 13 as the day of its first bishop.

> *"What you cannot save, save what you can; what you cannot warn, warn whom you can."* — Servatus, on his return from Rome

## May 14

# Carthach the Younger of Lismore

*Irish abbot, d. 637*

He was a Munster boy named Cuthach, called Mochuda by his teachers — *my Cuda*, an affectionate Irish diminutive — and finally Carthach in the Latinized form the chroniclers used. He had been a swineherd in his early youth on his father's lands, learning by ear the psalms a passing monk had taught him while he watched the pigs. He came to a monastic vocation at perhaps twenty. He was tonsured at Killeely and then went out, like many of the Irish abbots of his generation, looking for a place to build a community of his own.

He found it at Rahan in central Ireland, in the middle of the kingdom of Mide. He built a small wooden chapel and a beehive cluster of cells; men came; the place grew. Carthach governed Rahan for the better part of forty years and wrote there the Rule that still bears his name — a brief Old Irish text in verse, simpler than the Continental Rules, focused on the gentle balancing of prayer, work, and silence.

In 635, a political quarrel between rival Irish kingdoms turned against him. The new king of Mide wanted Rahan for his own bishop. He came in person with armed retainers and ordered Carthach to leave. Carthach, who would not call down the king's army on his own monks, took his community south. They walked the hundred miles from Rahan to the kingdom of the Déisi in Munster, a long, exhausting journey for a man already in his seventies. The local Déisi king Maelochtar gave them land on a sheltered ridge above the river Blackwater. They began building again.

The monastery they founded at Lismore became the great theological school of southern Ireland and lasted nine centuries. Carthach himself died on the ridge less than two years after the displacement, still unpacking, surrounded by the men he had brought south.

> *"Wherever they would not have us, the Lord has a place for us."* — Carthach, on the road to Lismore

## May 15

# Isidore the Farmer

*Madrid farmhand, 1070–1130*

He was a poor boy of Madrid, baptized at one of the small parish churches of what was then a frontier town on the Castilian plain — Madrid had been wrested back from the Moors only a generation earlier — and at fourteen, when his parents died, he hired himself out as a farm laborer on the estate of a Madrileño nobleman named Juan de Vargas. He worked the same fields for the rest of his life. He married a girl named María de la Cabeza, a fellow servant on the estate, who would later be venerated as a saint herself. They had one son. The boy fell into a well at five and drowned. They had no more children.

The story that has come down through every Castilian generation is the story Juan de Vargas told. Isidore prayed before sunrise, prayed at the field's edge before he began his work, paused at noon to pray, prayed again at sunset. His fellow laborers grew impatient with the long pauses and began complaining to the steward that Isidore was getting paid for prayer. The steward complained to Juan de Vargas. Juan, irritated, went out at dawn to the field to confront him.

He found Isidore kneeling. He found also, the chronicle says, two strangers in plain country clothing already plowing the field, the oxen moving easily before them. By the time Juan reached the spot the strangers were gone and the field was half-plowed. He understood at once. He never again raised the matter of Isidore's prayers.

Isidore was buried at Madrid in 1130. The Madrid he had left was a thousand farmhands' town; the Madrid that canonized him in 1622 was the imperial capital of Spain. Both Madrids remember him. He had given a city that would forget where its food came from a saint who never forgot.

> *"The Lord works with the man who works for him."* — Isidore the Farmer

## May 16

# Brendan the Navigator

*Irish abbot, c. 484–577*

He was born in Munster, baptized by Bishop Erc of Slane, fostered for his early childhood with the Irish saint Ita at her hermitage in Limerick, and educated finally at the great school of Jarlath of Tuam. He was made a priest in his twenties. He founded a string of monasteries across western Ireland — Ardfert, Inisdadroum, Inchquin, eventually the great house at Clonfert in Galway. He was a successful, conventional Irish abbot, late in his middle age, when the story of the rest of his life begins.

A fellow abbot named Barinthus visited Clonfert one evening with a tale of having sailed west into the Atlantic and reached a place of unending light he could only call the *Land of Promise of the Saints*. Brendan, the chronicler says, listened all night. He could not sleep after his visitor left. He gathered seventeen monks at first light, asked them whether they would come with him, and they did. They walked west across Ireland to the Dingle peninsula, built a hide-covered boat called a *curragh* over a wooden frame, provisioned it for forty days, and set sail.

The *Navigatio Sancti Brendani* — the Latin account compiled by an Irish monk perhaps three centuries later — records what they found in the seven years they were gone. They wintered on an Island of Sheep where the rams were as large as oxen. They celebrated Easter on the back of a great whale named Jasconius, who lay still while the monks sang the Office on him and only moved when the fire to cook breakfast was lit. They passed an island of birds that sang the Latin psalter in plain chant. They sailed under a column of crystal rising from the sea (an iceberg). They watched a mountain of fire belch black rock at them (a volcano). They reached, at last, a land of unending light where every flower bore fruit and the only sound was the praise of God.

They came home. Brendan founded another monastery and died at Annaghdown in his nineties. The voyage was made in the *curragh* of his Irish faith.

> *"Christ shall be our helmsman, and the wind shall be what he wills."* — Brendan, on launching the *curragh*

## May 17

# Paschal Baylon

*Franciscan lay brother of the Eucharist, 1540–1592*

He was born to peasants in the Aragonese village of Torrehermosa and put out to herd sheep before he could read. He taught himself letters by asking the passersby on the road what was written on the prayer-cards he carried, then comparing the answers. He read by the light of the moon when the flock was bedded. When he was twenty-four he walked to a Franciscan house of the strictest Alcantarine reform in Valencia and asked to be received as a lay brother. He never sought ordination. He spent the rest of his life as a porter, a cook, a beggar at the door, an *hortulanus* among the friary cabbages.

What he was given instead of office was the Blessed Sacrament. He would kneel on the stones outside the chapel after Compline and not move until Matins. The brothers found him there in every weather. When sent on an errand across France during the Wars of Religion, he was set upon by Huguenots who demanded he deny the Real Presence. He refused so calmly and so often that they finally beat him senseless and threw him by the road; he rose and walked on. The chronicler notes only that he completed the errand.

He died at the Franciscan house in Villarreal on the feast of Pentecost, May 17, 1592, at the moment of the Elevation in the Mass being sung in the chapel above his cell. He was fifty-two. The biographers gathered the things he had owned: a breviary, a cord, a discipline, a small wooden spoon. Leo XIII, three centuries later, named him patron of eucharistic congresses and confraternities — a title that would have surprised the boy who learned to read from prayer-cards on a road.

Paschal had no chair, no pulpit, no degree. He had a knee, and a place to put it, and a hunger that did not change for fifty years.

> *"My heart is in the Sacrament; if you would find me, find me there."* — Paschal, to a visitor at the friary door

## May 18

# John I, Pope

*Roman pontiff and martyr, d. 526*

He was an old Tuscan deacon when the clergy of Rome elected him to the chair of Peter in August 523, succeeding Hormisdas at a moment when the Italian peninsula was held by an Arian king and the imperial capital had moved seven hundred miles east. Theodoric the Ostrogoth had governed Italy for thirty years with a tolerable mildness, but at the end of his reign the news from Constantinople — that the emperor Justin had begun to harass the Arians of the East — made him suddenly afraid for his own. He summoned the new pope to Ravenna and ordered him to lead a Roman embassy to the emperor: not on behalf of orthodoxy, but on behalf of the Arian heretics whom Theodoric ruled.

John went. He was the first bishop of Rome to set foot in Constantinople. He was received by Justin with extraordinary honors — the emperor prostrated himself before him in the city square — and he crowned Justin in the basilica of Hagia Sophia on Easter Day, 526, the first imperial coronation by a pope. He celebrated the Roman rite at the altar, taking precedence over the patriarch. What he did not do was the thing Theodoric had sent him to do. He returned to Italy having quietly failed the king's commission, having declined to ask the emperor to restore the seized basilicas to Arian use.

Theodoric had him met at the dock at Ravenna, stripped of his retinue, and shut in a cell. John was old and the journey had been long. He died there within weeks, on May 18, of hunger and exhaustion, never having returned to Rome. The Romans brought his body home and buried him in the portico of Saint Peter's. The Acta number him among the martyrs, though no sword fell. He had been killed by a refusal — the refusal to use the office of Peter to enlarge the reach of a heresy, even when the price of refusing was a dungeon.

> *"I am sent on the emperor's road, but I walk on Christ's."* — John, before the embassy

## May 19

# Dunstan of Canterbury

*Archbishop and reformer of English monasticism, c. 909–988*

He was a West Saxon noble's son raised in the shadow of Glastonbury, taught by Irish scholars who had drifted into the old British monastery, and brought as a boy to the court of King Æthelstan because he had a quick mind and a quicker tongue. He was nearly expelled from court on charges of practicing magic — he had a habit, his accusers said, of speaking with invisible companions. He survived the charge, took the habit at Glastonbury, and became abbot there at perhaps thirty. He rebuilt the house from a poor and shrunken community into the seedbed of the tenth-century English monastic revival, the *Regularis Concordia* that would shape every Benedictine house in England for a century.

He served four kings. Edmund nearly dismissed him for ascetic severity, then, having barely escaped death over a cliff at Cheddar Gorge, recalled him and made him abbot. Eadred trusted him with the royal treasury. Eadwig exiled him for rebuking the king's adultery on his coronation day; Dunstan fled to Ghent. Edgar recalled him, made him bishop of Worcester, then London, then in 960 archbishop of Canterbury, and entrusted him with the long peace that would be remembered as the *pax Edgari*.

The Acta preserve the workshop stories more lovingly than the political ones. Dunstan was a metalsmith of great skill — he cast bells, illuminated manuscripts, repaired church plate with his own hands. The legend of the devil and the red-hot tongs comes from a real forge at Mayfield where he is said to have worked late into the night, alone, with the door open to the dark. He composed hymns and the chant for them. He drew an image of Christ in the *Glastonbury Classbook* and signed it *Dunstanus*. He died on Ascension eve, 988, after preaching three sermons that day, and was buried at Canterbury. England had been reformed by a monk who never stopped working with his hands.

> *"Let the work of my hands rise as incense, and the cunning of my fingers be an evening sacrifice."* — Dunstan, at his forge

## May 20

# Bernardine of Siena

*Franciscan preacher of the Holy Name, 1380–1444*

He was orphaned at six, raised by an aunt in Siena, and was studying canon law at seventeen when the plague returned. The hospital of Santa Maria della Scala lost most of its staff in a fortnight. Bernardine gathered a small band of fellow students and walked into the wards. For four months they nursed the dying, washed the dead, and read the Office over the pits when there were no priests left to read it. Both of his closest friends died beside him. When the plague had passed, he gave away what he had inherited, took the Franciscan habit of the Observance, and was sent into the hills to learn silence.

He came down twenty years later as the most powerful preacher in Italy. He spoke in piazzas because no church could hold the crowds — in Padua, in Milan, in Florence, in Rome, sometimes for three and four hours together, sometimes for eight successive days from the same square. He carried with him a wooden tablet on which he had painted the letters *IHS* — the Greek monogram of the Holy Name — surrounded by golden rays, and at the close of every sermon he raised it. Men set down their daggers; rival families embraced; the bonfires of vanities he kindled with the listeners' own ornaments became proverbial. He was three times offered a bishopric — Siena, Ferrara, Urbino — and three times refused, saying he had been sent to walk.

He died on the road, as he had asked, at L'Aquila in the Abruzzi on the vigil of the Ascension, 1444, having preached fifty sermons in the city in the last three weeks of his life. He was sixty-three. The painters of the next century gave him a hollowed face, a hand raised over the tablet, the three mitres he had refused at his feet. He had spent his strength on one word.

> *"At the name of Jesus every knee shall bow — and I have only been pointing."* — Bernardine, raising the tablet

## May 21

# Godric of Finchale

*English hermit, c. 1065–1170*

He was born in Norfolk to poor parents, Ailward and Ædwen — Christians of no rank but of plain faith. The boy had no schooling. Galfridus, who wrote his Vita from the longer one by Reginald of Durham, calls him *rusticanus et idiota nihilque sciens nisi Christum Jesum, et hunc crucifixum* — a peasant and unlettered, knowing nothing save Christ Jesus and him crucified. He turned to trade, spending sixteen years as a merchant, walking the villages of eastern England with packs and working the small coastal shipping. A near-drowning at Wellestrem — wading after a beached dolphin while the tide passed his ankles, his knees, then over his head — became the moment he first applied the prophet's word *the deep encompassed me*, and began to think of another road.

He gave up trade in his late thirties for a pilgrim's life. He went to Rome and Jerusalem barefoot, in great poverty. He came back to England looking for a place to serve God alone, and was told in a dream to seek out Finchale — a wooded ground on a bend of the Wear not far from Durham. He found it after long searching. He lived there first with a sister, *cum sorore paupercula*; after she died he lived alone for the rest of his very long life.

The plain at Finchale was small. He dug with a hoe, ate what he grew, gave whatever was left to those who came. The monks of Durham sent an older brother to instruct him and bring him the Sacrament. He built a small oratory in honor of John the Baptist. He lived sixty years a hermit. Galfridus, who as a boy met the old man in the last weeks before his death, gives the only physical description AASS keeps of him: small in body, the belly thinned by fasting, the hair white, the face long, the eyes pale. *Velox ad audiendum, tardus autem ad loquendum* — quick to hear, slow to speak. He could weep with those who wept; he did not know how to laugh with those who laughed.

He died May 21, 1170, in his oratory, beside the altar where he had been used to lie prostrate. The same patch of floor took his grave.

> *"Sainte Marie virgine, moder Iesu Cristes Nazarene, onfo, schild, help thin Godric."* — Godric's own Middle English hymn, taken down by Reginald of Durham

## May 22

# Rita of Cascia

*Augustinian widow, c. 1381–1457*

She was born to elderly peasants in the Umbrian hill village of Roccaporena and married at twelve, against her wish, to a quarrelsome local named Paolo Mancini. He was, by every account, harsh — sharp-tongued in the streets and at home. She bore him twin sons. She kept the house. She prayed at dawn at the rough wooden cross on the hill behind the village. After eighteen years the husband was ambushed and killed in one of the local feuds that fed on each other for generations in those mountains. Rita's two sons, by then in their late teens, swore the vendetta in the customary form, naming the killers.

She did the thing for which Cascia remembers her. She prayed that her sons would die before they could fulfill the oath. They both fell sick within months, both of natural illness, and both — by the testimony she gave the chronicler — confessed and were reconciled before they died. She was a widow of forty, childless, alone.

She tried three times to enter the Augustinian convent of Santa Maria Maddalena in Cascia and was turned away — the house did not take widows, and the family feud still hung in the air. The biographer says her three patrons (John the Baptist, Augustine, and Nicholas of Tolentino) brought her by night through the locked gate; the nuns, finding her in the chapel at Matins, took her in. She lived there forty years. She nursed the sick of the town through the plague of 1437. Toward the end of her life, kneeling before a crucifix and asking to share even a little of Christ's suffering, she received a thorn wound in her forehead — a single point of pain that did not heal until she died, and reopened, the chronicler says, only when she heard scripture read aloud.

She died on May 22, 1457. The wound closed. The bees still come, in Cascia, to the wall of the cell.

> *"Lord, if I cannot be a mother to peace, make me at least its widow."* — Rita, after her sons died

## May 23

# William of Rochester

*Baker and pilgrim-martyr, d. 1201*

He was a Scottish baker in the town of Perth, of middling prosperity, unmarried into his forties. He had the habit, the chronicler says, of laying aside every tenth loaf from his oven and distributing it to the poor at the door of his shop before he opened for the morning's trade. He was a daily communicant. He had taken at some point a private resolution to walk, before he died, to the holy places — to Canterbury, to Rome, to Jerusalem if he could — and he was waiting only for an occasion to set out.

The occasion came in the form of a foundling. He found a boy at his door one winter morning, perhaps four or five years old, half-frozen, abandoned. He took the child in, fed him, baptized him David (so the boy seems to have been a pagan abandoned by travelers), and raised him as an apprentice. When the boy was perhaps fifteen and could keep the shop alone, William believed the moment had come. He took his pilgrim's staff and scrip, the boy beside him, and they set out south for Canterbury.

They had crossed England and reached the outskirts of Rochester in May 1201 when the apprentice — for what cause the chronicler does not name; perhaps the few coins of the alms purse — drew William off the road into a wood, struck him on the head, cut his throat, and fled. A passing madwoman of the town, who had been wandering the lane, found the body. She arranged twigs around it in the shape of a cross and crowned the head with woodbine. She was, the *Acta* note, cured of her madness in the act. The monks of the cathedral priory, hearing the report, took William's body inside the city walls and buried him in their church.

He was canonized in 1256. He had wanted to walk to Jerusalem. He got as far as a wood outside Rochester, where the road ended and the cross was made of twigs.

> *"He gave a loaf in alms each morning; he gave his life one evening."* — Rochester chronicler

## May 24

# Vincent of Lérins

*Monk and theologian of Provence, d. before 450*

He had been, by his own account in the preface of the *Commonitorium*, a man tossed in the world before he came to the island. He had served as a soldier, or perhaps in the imperial administration; he was a Gallic gentleman of education, weary, in the phrase of the chronicler, of the storms and disturbances of secular life. Around 425 he crossed to Lérins, the small Mediterranean island off Cannes, where Honoratus had recently founded a monastery on a Greek rule and which had already become the seedbed of bishops for southern Gaul. He took the name Peregrinus — pilgrim — and from a hut on that pine-scented rock he wrote, in the third year of his profession, the small book that would outlast every other thing he did.

The *Commonitorium* is a short treatise on a single question: when scripture is contested, when controversies divide the church, how is a Christian to know what is the catholic faith? Vincent's answer is one sentence, framed as a tool to be carried in the memory: *Quod ubique, quod semper, quod ab omnibus creditum est* — what has been believed everywhere, always, by all. He calls these the three notes — universality, antiquity, and consent. He does not propose them as a slogan but as a discipline. He works through them example by example, showing how each note alone is insufficient and how the three together filter every novelty against the deposit. He is careful, generous, and never harsh.

He died in his island cell sometime before 450, leaving the book and no other certain trace. His name was rarely invoked in the next twelve centuries. Then the post-Reformation centuries seized him as a lamp. Anglicans cited him; Jansenists cited him; Newman cited him; the Catechism cites him. He had been a tired Gaul who came to an island to think clearly, and what he wrote in three small chapters has been the church's pocket compass for sixteen hundred years.

> *"Hold fast what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all."* — Vincent, *Commonitorium* 2

## May 25

# Gregory VII, Pope

*Reformer of the Latin Church, c. 1015–1085*

He was born Hildebrand, of small Tuscan parents, sent as a boy to an uncle who was abbot at Santa Maria on the Aventine, and educated thence at the Lateran. He served five popes before his own election — as chaplain to Gregory VI in exile in Germany, as administrator of the patrimony under Leo IX, as legate to France for Nicholas II, as archdeacon under Alexander II. He was small, dark, intense, an indifferent Latinist but a relentless administrator. He had spent thirty years preparing the reform he believed the church needed when, at the funeral of Alexander II in April 1073, the Roman crowd carried him off and acclaimed him pope by acclamation in San Pietro in Vincoli before he could refuse.

He took the name of the great Gregory, and he laid down, in the *Dictatus Papae* of 1075, twenty-seven theses on the freedom of the apostolic see. The substance of his pontificate was the substance of those theses: that bishops should not be appointed by kings, that priests should not be married, that simony should be uprooted at every level, that the pope was the spiritual head of all baptized rulers. The conflict that followed — the Investiture Controversy — broke open the high middle ages. Henry IV of Germany, having received the bishops of Worms' deposition decree against him, met him at Canossa in January 1077, stood barefoot in the snow for three days, and was absolved. Three years later the king repudiated the absolution, marched on Rome, and installed an antipope.

Gregory was rescued from Castel Sant'Angelo in 1084 by the Normans of Robert Guiscard, who sacked Rome so brutally in the rescue that the Romans turned against the pope. He withdrew with the Normans to Salerno. He died there on May 25, 1085. The last words the chronicler preserves are these — quoted in every monastery of the reform: *Dilexi iustitiam et odivi iniquitatem, propterea morior in exilio.* I loved justice and hated iniquity; therefore I die in exile.

> *"I loved justice and hated iniquity; therefore I die in exile."* — Gregory, at Salerno

## May 26

# Augustine of Canterbury

*First archbishop of the English, d. 604*

He was prior of Gregory the Great's own monastery of Saint Andrew on the Caelian hill in Rome when, in 596, the pope picked him out of the community and sent him north with forty monks to convert the English. The mission was Gregory's long ambition. Years before, walking through the slave market in the Forum, the pope had seen flaxen-haired British boys for sale and asked their nation; told *Angli*, he is said to have answered, *Non Angli, sed angeli* — not Angles, but angels. He had wanted to go himself; called instead to be pope, he sent Augustine.

The party crossed the Alps, wintered at Lérins, and got as far as Aix before the monks lost their courage. They had heard the English were ferocious and the British Channel rough; they sent Augustine back to Rome to ask permission to abandon the mission. Gregory wrote him a letter that did not give permission. He set out again. They landed on the Isle of Thanet in the spring of 597. Æthelberht of Kent, whose Frankish queen Bertha was a Christian and had her own chapel at Canterbury, came down to meet them — outdoors, lest the strangers' magic prevail under a roof. Augustine and the monks walked toward him singing, a silver cross before them, an icon of Christ on a board.

Æthelberht did not believe at once. He gave them a house in Canterbury and let them preach. Within months the king was baptized; by Christmas, the chronicler says, ten thousand of his people followed him. Augustine consecrated Mellitus for London and Justus for Rochester. He sent the famous *Responsiones* — nine practical questions on how to govern a young English church — back to Gregory, and treasured the answers. He met the British bishops in the west, failed to bring them under his authority, and let the failure stand. He died on May 26, 604, ten years before his church had its first English-born bishop.

> *"What we have brought is not our own; receive it as you would receive its giver."* — Augustine, before Æthelberht at Thanet

## May 27

# Bede the Venerable

*Monk and historian of Jarrow, c. 673–735*

He was given to the monastery at Monkwearmouth when he was seven, transferred to its twin house at Jarrow when he was twelve, and never left. The pestilence of 686 nearly wiped out the choir; for a time only the abbot Ceolfrith and one boy could sing the Office, and the abbot, the chronicler says, ordered the chant continued without the antiphons rather than break the cycle. The boy is presumed to have been Bede. He was ordained deacon at nineteen, priest at thirty, and spent the remaining forty years of his life within the walls of Jarrow doing the three things he loved: chanting the canonical hours, teaching the younger monks, and writing.

He wrote nearly sixty books. He wrote commentaries on almost every book of the Bible, working through them slowly in Latin glossed against the Hebrew and Greek he could partly read. He wrote *On the Reckoning of Time*, which gave Christian Europe the *Anno Domini* dating it has used ever since. He wrote the lives of Cuthbert, of Felix, of the Abbots of his own house. And in his last decade he wrote the book the rest of the world remembers him by — the *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* — five books in clear Latin, drawn from letters he requested from every diocese in England, that gave a young nation its first history of itself. Without him we would not know how Christianity reached the English at all.

He dictated through the spring of 735, dying. On the eve of Ascension he was finishing a translation of the Gospel of John into English for his pupils. The boy taking down the words said, "Master, there is one sentence still." Bede dictated it, lifted his eyes, sang the *Gloria Patri*, and at the *Sancto Spiritui* breathed his last. He was sixty-two. They buried him at Jarrow; later centuries moved his bones to the Galilee chapel of Durham Cathedral, where they lie today under one stone with a single word: *Beda*.

> *"It is finished. Receive my soul into thy hands."* — Bede, on Ascension eve, 735

## May 28

# Germanus of Paris

*Merovingian bishop and almoner, c. 496–576*

He was born near Autun in Burgundy, of decent family, and so nearly killed in the womb — his mother, the *Vita* by Fortunatus says, attempted twice to miscarry him for shame at the close spacing of her pregnancies — that the chronicler returns to the detail as the first sign of God's hand on him. He was raised by a cousin who was a priest, ordained young, and made abbot of Saint-Symphorien at Autun by his fortieth year. The house under him became famous for almsgiving: he gave away grain so freely that the brothers once locked the storeroom against him, and the saint, the chronicler says, found the key by sleeping outside the door until it was returned.

Childebert I, the Frankish king of Paris, having visited Autun and recognized the abbot, recalled him to the capital in 555 and pressed him into the vacant see of Paris. Germanus accepted on the condition that the king found an abbey for the poor outside the city walls — the house that would become Saint-Germain-des-Prés. He served as bishop for twenty-one years. He spent the revenues of his see almost entirely on captives and the poor; he is said to have ransomed so many slaves from Spanish merchants on the Seine that the merchants began to avoid the Paris market. He was a constant petitioner before the four Merovingian kings who sat on the Paris throne in his lifetime, restraining (where he could) the fratricidal wars of the dynasty and excommunicating Charibert I after the king's incestuous remarriage.

He died on May 28, 576, in his eightieth year. The chronicler notes the small thing: that the kitchen, on the morning of his death, ran out of grain for the daily distribution at the gate, and the bishop ordered the granary opened entire, and there was bread that day for everyone, and none for the bishop's table. Paris buried him in the basilica he had founded for the poor; it bears his name.

> *"What the church holds is held for the captive and the poor; the bishop is only its hand."* — Germanus, to Childebert

## May 29

# Maximinus of Trier

*Bishop and protector of Athanasius, d. 346*

He came from Aquitaine, from a Christian family at Silly near Poitiers, and was educated in the school of his future predecessor Agroecius at Trier — then the imperial capital of the Gallic provinces, a city of perhaps eighty thousand, the residence of Constantine and afterward of Constans. He succeeded Agroecius as bishop in 333 and held the see for thirteen years. He was a man of the imperial court without being a man of the imperial party. He was for that reason a useful refuge.

In 336 Athanasius of Alexandria, exiled by Constantine for the first time, was sent to Trier and entrusted to Maximinus. The bishop received him not as a prisoner but as a fellow patriarch, gave him quarters in his own house, walked with him in the gardens above the Moselle, and discussed the Nicene faith with him through the two years of his exile. When Athanasius was at last permitted to return to Egypt in 337, he carried with him into the East the lifelong friendship of the Gallic bishop and the confidence — confirmed everywhere in his later writings — that the Latin West stood with Nicaea. Two years later Maximinus received also Paul of Constantinople, exiled by Constantius for the same cause. The synod of Sardica in 343, which Maximinus attended, was the public face of what his hospitality had quietly built.

He defended the Trinitarian faith at every council he attended and refused to sign the Arianizing creeds that the Eastern bishops pressed. When the emperor Constans, the western Augustus, leaned toward orthodoxy partly under Maximinus's influence, the bishop's name became a curse in the Arian camp. He died around 346, before the storm of Constantius's hegemony fully broke, and was buried in the church of Saint John outside the Trier walls.

He had no eloquence the centuries preserved, no treatise, no martyrdom. He had a guest-room. The Nicene faith of the Latin West owes more to that guest-room than to a dozen treatises.

> *"My house is the patriarch's house while the patriarch is in mine."* — Maximinus, receiving Athanasius

## May 30

# Isaac the Anchorite of Constantinople

*Confessor, abbot, d. 383*

He came from Asia Minor and had borne, the Synaxaria say, the yoke of the Lord from his boyhood. He had been an ascetic in the eastern hill country for years when, in the spring of 378, he made his way to Constantinople. The emperor Valens — a hard Arian who had filled the orthodox sees of the East with men of his own party and exiled the Nicene confessors — was at that moment leading his army westward to meet the Gothic confederation that had crossed the Danube. Isaac broke through the imperial guard, took hold of the bridle of the emperor's horse, and would not let go.

He addressed the emperor — Theodoret preserves the words at *Historia Ecclesiastica* IV.34: *Where dost thou go, Emperor, thou that hast made war on God and hast him not for thy helper? It is he who has stirred the barbarians against thee, because thou didst expel from the churches those who praised him. Restore the best Pastors to their flocks, and thou shalt take a bloodless victory. But if thou go forward, neither shalt thou return, and thou shalt lose thine army.* Valens, in anger: *I will return, and I will kill thee.* Isaac, unafraid: *Kill me, if a lie be found in my words.*

The emperor had him thrown in prison and rode west. He did not return. The army was destroyed at Adrianople on the 9th of August, 378; Valens, wounded, fled to a barn where the Gothic *foederati* did not know he was hiding, and burned alive when the barn was fired. Isaac, the AASS records from Theophanes, perceived from his cell the smell of the burning emperor's body before any messenger had arrived in the city, and announced the death to those who stood with him.

The new Augustus Theodosius freed him within months. The monastic community that gathered around him became, under his successor Dalmatus, the *Dalmatorum monasterium* — the chief monastery of Constantinople for the next four centuries. He died *plenus dierum*, old and full of days, and the Greek Synaxaria keep his name on May 30. He is in the *Acta* a *Confessor* — not for blood, but for the prison.

> *"Kill me, if a lie be found in my words."* — Isaac, holding the emperor's bridle, AASS Maii VII

## May 31

# Petronilla

*Roman virgin, daughter of Peter, d. by tradition c. 95*

She is the only saint in the early Roman martyrology that the apostolic generation gave a daughter to — *Petronilla, filia Petri Apostoli*, in the formula the Bollandists print from Bede and Ado. The acts are short and old. The principal source is a fragment of correspondence from a Roman noble named Marcellus, written to the exiled brothers Nereus and Achilleus on Ponza in perhaps the year 95, telling them what had happened to their friend while they had been away.

Marcellus's letter opens with the family scene at Peter's house in Rome. The disciple Titus had asked Peter, in the way of friends, why Petronilla — Peter's young daughter, perhaps in her late teens — was paralyzed and bedridden when her father had healed so many others. Peter answered: *It is good for her so.* Then, as if to satisfy Titus, he turned to her and said: *Get up, Petronilla, and serve us.* She got up. She served the table. He sent her back to bed.

She lived in obscurity until Peter was killed in the Neronian persecution of 64. She continued to nurse the sick of the Roman community — the gift she had been given the second time was for the others who had not yet come up from their beds. She refused all offers of marriage. Her own death came when a powerful pagan named Flaccus, who had heard of her beauty, came to the house with armed men to take her by force. She received him with a delegation of consecrated virgins, asked for three days' grace, spent them in fasting and prayer, received the eucharist from the priest Nicomedes on the third morning, lay back on her bed, and died.

Her body was buried in the catacombs of Domitilla on the Via Ardeatina, in the chamber called *cubiculum Petronillae*, still visible today. Pope Paul I translated the relics in 758 to the Vatican; they lie now in the new basilica's *Cappella di San Petronilla*.

The young woman who had been told twice by her father to get up — once for her own healing, once to receive the sacrament of her own death — has been the daughter of the apostolic church for nineteen hundred years.

> *"It is good for her so."* — Peter to Titus, on Petronilla's paralysis, in the *acta* of Marcellus
