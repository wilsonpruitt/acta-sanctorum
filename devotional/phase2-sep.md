## September 1

# Arcanus and Aegidius of Borgo San Sepolcro

*Hermit-pilgrims, founders of an Umbrian town, c. 936–c. 960*

They met, the chronicle says, at the Holy Sepulchre. Arcanus was a Greek from Arcadia; Aegidius was a Spaniard. Both had come to Jerusalem on pilgrimage, both stayed to pray at the empty tomb, both walked out one morning carrying a satchel of relics they had been given and the conviction that they were not finished travelling. They went to Rome together. They venerated the apostles' tombs. They turned north, intending to go on to Venice and from there to take the longer way home.

In Umbria, between Arezzo and the Tiber, they stepped off the road into a wood to rest at a clear spring. Arcanus fell asleep. He woke believing he had heard a voice — *this place is holy; stop here; build an altar.* He looked at the satchel of relics he had hung on a low branch. As he watched (so the *Annals of Borgo* tell it) the satchel slipped its cord and rose into the air. It would not come down until the two of them, half terrified, half laughing, vowed they would build a church on the spot. Then it descended, and they began to gather stones.

The Bollandists handle the satchel story with the polite skepticism it deserves — Leo X, when he made the place a bishopric in 1515, mentioned "a divine vision" but spoke of the flying relics not at all. What no one disputes is what came afterwards. The chapel rose; pilgrims found it; cottages went up around it; a town grew. They named it for the empty tomb the two pilgrims had left behind in Jerusalem: *Borgo del Santo Sepolcro,* the burgh of the Holy Sepulchre. Camaldulese monks took over the chapel a generation later. The town is still there. It still bears the name.

The two pilgrims kept walking until they didn't. *This place is holy; stop here.* Most lives are made by knowing when to stop.

> *"Locus hic sanctus est, hic siste gradum, et aram erige."* — *the voice Arcanus heard at the spring*

## September 2

# Stephen of Hungary

*First Christian king of the Magyars, c. 975–1038*

His father Géza had been a pagan duke who hedged his bets — he kept the old gods at one shoulder and let Adalbert of Prague baptize his household at the other. The boy who would become Stephen was given the name Vajk; he learned to ride before he could read, took the new name at the font, and grew up between two worlds. When his father died in 997 he had to fight a cousin, Koppány, who wanted Hungary back for the old religion. He won, hard. He cut Koppány into four pieces and sent them to four cities as a warning. Then he set about building a Christian kingdom on the Danubian plain.

Pope Sylvester II sent him a crown. He was anointed at Esztergom on the first day of the new millennium. Over the next thirty-eight years he carved Hungary into ten dioceses, founded the abbey of Pannonhalma, codified the laws, opened pilgrim hospices on the road to Jerusalem, and married Gisela of Bavaria, sister of the emperor Henry II. Where his father had hedged, he was decisive: the new faith would be the law of the land and the kingdom would be ruled from a font, not a battlefield. The Bollandists carry the *Vita* by Bishop Hartvik, written under Stephen's grandnephew Coloman a generation later, against several centuries of attempts to discredit it. The substance is durable: Stephen built a church-state where there had been a confederation of pagan tribes, and the church-state outlasted him.

It nearly didn't. His only son Imre, the heir he had trained and the saint he loved most, was killed in a hunting accident in 1031. Stephen lived seven more years with no successor and a circling court. On his deathbed in 1038 — the feast of the Assumption — he raised the kingdom in his hands and offered it to the Virgin: Hungary, he said, was hers from this day, *Regnum Marianum.* He died as the priests finished the office. The kingdom he gave away outlived every dynasty that came after.

> *"Receive, O Queen of Heaven, this kingdom which I commit to thy keeping."* — Stephen, on his deathbed

## September 3

# Phoebe the Deaconess

*Patron of Cenchreae, courier of the Letter to the Romans, fl. c. 56–58*

Saint Paul had finished his great letter to the church at Rome — three years' worth of theology compressed into sixteen chapters — and now he had to find someone to carry it across the Adriatic. The roads from Corinth to Rome were not safe for a woman travelling alone, and the manuscript she would carry, fragile in its leather wrapping, mattered more to the apostle than almost anything he had written. He chose Phoebe. She was, he says in the closing greetings, *our sister, who is a deaconess of the church which is at Cenchreae:* a woman of the harbor town two miles east of Corinth, where ships put in for the Aegean crossing. He gives the church at Rome two instructions. *Receive her in the Lord, as becometh saints.* And: *assist her in whatsoever business she hath need of you.*

The little we know of her is in those two verses. The church at Cenchreae had made her a *diakonos* — a servant, an officer, the same word Paul applies to himself in the same letter. He calls her, in the next breath, *prostatis,* which the Latin renders *adstitit,* a word for the legal patron whose house was open to the apostle and to many others. She paid for things, in other words. She fed people. She used a position of standing in a Greek port city to keep an apostle moving and a young church watered. When Paul needed a courier, the courier was someone the church already trusted with her name on the door.

The early Greek synaxaria remember her at September the third, paired with the other women Paul names in Romans 16 — Priscilla, Tryphaena, Persis, Junia. The Latin tradition picked up the day later. The patristic homilies on her are sparse; Chrysostom, in his homilies on Romans, gives her a brief paragraph and the title *deaconess of the women,* which is what the early church seems to have meant by it. The letter she carried became one of the four pillars of the New Testament. The hand that carried it was hers.

> *"I commend unto you Phoebe our sister, who is a deaconess of the church that is at Cenchreae."* — Paul, *Romans* 16:1

## September 4

# Rose of Viterbo

*Franciscan tertiary, child preacher, c. 1233–1252*

She was twelve years old when she began preaching in the streets of Viterbo against the emperor. Frederick II had been excommunicated for the second time and was at war with the papacy; Viterbo, a papal city in central Italy, was wavering. Rose — born to a poor family near the church of Santa Maria in Poggio, taken into the third order of Saint Francis as a child after a vision of the Virgin had cured her of a long illness — went out with a small wooden crucifix and stood in the squares and called the city back. The Bollandists, working from the canonization process Calixtus III ordered in 1457, are careful with the legendary material; what survives the editorial knife is a girl, perhaps thirteen by then, drawing crowds.

The imperial party in Viterbo had her exiled with her parents. They went up the road to Soriano nel Cimino, then to Vitorchiano, where (the *Vita* says) she converted a sorceress by stepping unhurt onto a burning pyre. She kept preaching. When Frederick died in 1250 she came home. She asked to be admitted to the Poor Clares of Saint Mary of the Roses; the abbess refused her — perhaps she had no dowry, perhaps the convent was wary of a girl who had already drawn that much attention. Rose accepted the refusal. *If you will not have me living,* she is said to have replied, *you will have me dead.* She returned to her father's small house and lived there as a recluse until she died in 1252, eighteen years old at most.

Five years after her death the same convent received her body for burial, and her uncorrupted relics have lain in their church ever since. Every September fourth the city of Viterbo carries her on the *macchina,* a five-ton illuminated tower lifted on the shoulders of a hundred men, through streets she once walked alone with a wooden cross. The girl who could not get in died and was let in. The city she preached to never stopped listening.

> *"Ego sum, quæ Christum amavi pauperem; et qui me amavit, recipiet me."* — Rose, refused at the convent gate

## September 5

# Bertinus of Sithiu

*Abbot, drainer of marshes, c. 615–c. 698*

He came north from Luxeuil with two companions, Mommolinus and Ebertramnus, sometime around the year 638. Audomarus — Omer — had been sent into the Pas-de-Calais a decade earlier as missionary bishop to the Morini, the half-pagan coastal tribes of the Frankish far north. The country he had inherited was unworked: salt marshes east of the Aa, peat bogs, willow thickets, fishing villages set on low islands. He needed monks who knew how to dig. Bertinus had grown up under Columbanus's rule and had spent ten years at Luxeuil learning Burgundian agriculture. He arrived with an axe and a Psalter.

The brothers settled first at Sithiu, a small island Omer had been given by a local lord named Adroald. Within a generation they had drained the surrounding marsh, raised dykes, planted orchards, built a church on rising ground a mile to the north — the *upper* church, *altum,* where the town of Saint-Omer would later grow — and a monastery on the lower island. Bertinus succeeded Mommolinus as abbot in 663 and stayed in office for thirty-five years. The abbey he ran became one of the great houses of Carolingian Flanders. By the ninth century it would shelter the *Annales Bertiniani,* one of the chief chronicles of the period; by the tenth it bore his name.

The Bollandists devote three closely-argued sections to untangling the manuscript history — six different *vitae* survive, anonymous and Folcardine and metrical, none of them quite reconcilable — and conclude with reluctance that the man behind the layers is recoverable only in outline. What the outline preserves is steady work in a country that did not invite it: thirty-five years in a damp dormitory, a chapter of monks holding a community together by the rule, a slow conversion of marsh into arable. Of his miracles, the *Vita* records the unremarkable kind — a sick brother healed, a difficult harvest brought in, his own body still upright at his death-Mass at the age of eighty-three. He was buried under the church floor of his own monastery. The drained land has not gone back to swamp.

> *"Abbas pius, qui non tam verbo quam exemplo docebat."* — *Vita prima,* of Bertinus

## September 6

# Magnus of Füssen

*Apostle of the Allgäu, abbot, c. 700–c. 772*

The Vita the Bollandists print under his name is, as they patiently demonstrate over six dense paragraphs, not the original. A monk named Theodorus is supposed to have written it at his bedside; what survives is the ninth-century recension by Ermenric of Ellwangen, smoothed and re-smoothed by later hands, with passages lifted whole from Walafrid Strabo's *Vita Galli.* What can be salvaged is enough: a Frankish-Alemannic monk, raised at Saint Gall under Otmar, sent south into the Allgäu at the request of Bishop Wikterp of Augsburg to evangelize a stretch of upper Bavaria where the Christian gospel had not yet taken hold. He arrived around 740 and stayed for thirty years.

The country he inherited was the Lech valley as it climbs toward the Alps — alpine meadow, dense fir, the small fortified Roman ruin at *Faucibus,* the place we now call Füssen, *the gorges,* where the river squeezes through limestone. He built a cell there, then a wooden chapel, then a stone abbey. The Vita is at its best when it tells the small story: the brewing accident in which the cask miraculously refilled itself; the bear who would not let him fell a tree until he made the sign of the cross; the dragon (or great serpent) of the Roßhaupten gorge who was driven out by the staff he had received from Columbanus's spiritual descendants at Saint Gall. The staff, called the *Magnusstab,* was preserved in the abbey for a thousand years and is still venerated in Füssen today. Local farmers used to bring it to their fields when caterpillars or hail threatened the grain.

He died, according to the Vita, on the sixth of September, an old man among his monks. The abbey at Füssen kept his rule for centuries; the village he founded grew into a market town under the Hohenstaufen castle on the cliff above; his cult spread up and down the Lech and into the Tyrol. *Magnus* — *the Great* — was a name his Latin biographers liked. The Allgäu, more honestly, called him *Mang.* The shorter name is the one his people kept.

> *"Sub fasce voluminis gemeret robustus lector"* — *the Bollandists, on the legendary additions: the careful editor sighs under their weight*

## September 7

# Cloud (Clodoald)

*Merovingian prince turned hermit-priest, c. 522–c. 560*

His grandfather was Clovis. His father Clodomir, king of Orléans, was killed in 524 fighting the Burgundians; his head was carried back on a Burgundian spear. The three sons left behind — Theodebald, Gunthar, and Clodoald — were sent to Paris under the protection of their grandmother, Clovis's widow Chrothilde, who raised them in her own household with the affection of a woman who had buried too many children already. They were five, seven, and three.

When the boys were ten, eight, and five, their uncles Childebert and Chlothar, who had inherited the Frankish kingdoms north and south of the Loire, decided that Orléans needed a king again and that none of the candidates should be small. Gregory of Tours preserves the scene that became one of the most famous in early Frankish memory. The uncles sent a messenger to the queen-grandmother in Paris with a pair of scissors in one hand and a sword in the other: choose, the messenger said, *which of these your grandsons shall feel.* Tonsure them and they live as monks; refuse the tonsure and they die as princes. Chrothilde, in the heat of grief, said: *Better dead than tonsured,* and meant it of the sword. The messenger took her at her word. Theodebald and Gunthar were murdered on the spot. Clodoald — five years old — had been spirited out a back door by a servant and was hidden in the countryside until the danger passed.

He grew up in obscurity. When he came of age he refused the kingdom that was, by inheritance, half his. He cut his own hair before any uncle could send a sword for it, took the habit of a monk, lived for years as a hermit in Provence, returned to Paris, was ordained priest by Bishop Eusebius, and founded a small monastery at the village of Nogent on the Seine three miles below the city — the place we now call Saint-Cloud. He was perhaps thirty-eight when he died. The grandson of Clovis, who had every claim to a crown, was buried in the church he had built with his own hands, having outlived his uncles and chosen, at every fork, the smaller portion.

> *"Melius est tonsorari, quam pro regno mori."* — *the saying afterwards put on Cloud's lips, against his grandmother's grief*

## September 8

# Adrian and Natalia

*Roman officer, his Christian wife, martyrs at Nicomedia, c. 304–310*

He was a young guards officer, a *notarius* in the imperial palace at Nicomedia, when the second great persecution opened in 303. His job that morning was to write down the names of the Christians as they were brought in for sentencing. The acts the Bollandists print, drawn from a twelfth-century Greek codex they judge older and better than the Latin compendia, slow the scene to a long question. Adrian had watched twenty-three of them refuse to sacrifice. He had heard them sing on the way to the rack. He laid down his pen and asked them, *What do you hope for, that is worth this?* They told him. He took their words with him to the prefect, gave his own name, and walked into the cell beside them.

His wife Natalia was thirteen years old, a Christian who had not told him. When she heard he was in prison she came down to the gates dressed in men's clothes — the law forbade Christian women to visit the condemned — paid the keeper, and threw herself at his feet. *Do not turn back,* she said. *I have prayed for this since the day my mother gave me to you.* For four weeks she came every night, talked through the bars, dressed his wounds, brought bread. When the sentence came down — the prisoners' legs to be broken on the anvil — Natalia was at the prison door before sunrise. She held her husband's leg on the iron herself, *so that*, she told the executioner, *he should not have to suffer the touch of an unbeliever.*

Adrian died first. The other twenty-three followed. Maximian, fearing the bodies would become relics, ordered them burned outside the city; a sudden rainstorm put the fire out before they were consumed. Natalia recovered her husband's severed hand, wrapped it in linen, and fled with the surviving Christians across the Bosphorus to a small house at Argyropolis on the European shore, where she lived another year, sleeping with the relic under her pillow. She was buried beside him. The hand she had carried over the water was venerated in Constantinople for nine hundred years. The shrine of Saint-Adrien at Geraardsbergen in Flanders, where his cult travelled west, still keeps the day.

> *"Hold fast, my lord; for you have not lost me, but found me."* — Natalia, at the prison gate

## September 9

# Kieran of Clonmacnoise

*Founding abbot, the carpenter's son, c. 515–549*

He was the son of a wheelwright in Connacht, born to a craftsman's house in the western country where the Shannon turns south. The Irish vitae call him *Mac an tSaer,* the artisan's boy. He grew up at the workbench, learning to plane and joint and shape wood with his hands. When he heard of Finnian of Clonard he walked east to learn from him, and was numbered among the *Twelve Apostles of Ireland* — Finnian's twelve famous students who would each found a monastery and shape the Irish church for a thousand years. Columba was one of the twelve; so was Brendan the Navigator; so was Kieran.

He went first to Inishmore in the Aran Islands, sat under Enda, learned silence. He went next to Inis Aingin on Lough Ree, founded a small house there, and after some years moved south to a bend in the Shannon where the high road from Connacht to Leinster crossed the river. He chose a meadow above the floodplain and laid out the sketch of what would become Clonmacnoise — *Cluain mhic Nóis,* the meadow of the sons of Nós. He had been there seven months. He had built the first wooden church and gathered ten brothers when the yellow plague came up the river in 549 and took him at thirty-three years old. They buried him in the church he had finished the week before.

The community he had begun for seven months kept his rule for a thousand years. Within a generation Clonmacnoise was the largest monastic city in Ireland — a town of churches, round towers, scriptoria, and the high crosses whose stone faces still rise from the ground above the Shannon. The *Annals of Clonmacnoise,* the *Book of the Dun Cow,* much of what we know of early Ireland came out of those buildings. The kings of Connacht and Tara competed for the privilege of being buried in the cemetery his ten brothers had laid out around his grave. He had lived as long as Christ, the Bollandists note, and built almost nothing. What he built lasted.

> *"The carpenter's son outlived the carpenter."* — *medieval Irish proverb*

## September 10

# Nicholas of Tolentino

*Augustinian friar, intercessor for the dead, c. 1245–1305*

He was born to old parents who had walked to the shrine of Saint Nicholas at Bari to pray for a child and named him for the saint who had answered them. The Marches of Ancona, where he grew up, were full of new mendicant friars in the 1250s — the Franciscans had been there a generation, the Augustinian Hermits had just been gathered out of older eremitical communities by the Grand Union of 1256. Nicholas joined the Augustinians at sixteen, took orders at twenty-five, and was sent in his early thirties to a small house at Tolentino, a hill town on the upper Chienti. He stayed there for the next thirty years and never left.

The Bollandists print Peter of Monte Rubbiano's life of him, written by a confrere who had known him, around 1326. The portrait is unhurried. Nicholas heard confessions for hours. He preached in the open piazza in the evenings. He took bread to the prisoners in the *carceri* under the town walls. He fasted hard enough that an old companion warned him he would die early; he kept fasting. The miracle the friars remembered most was the smallest — sick once and unable to eat, he was given a roll of bread by the Virgin in a vision, with the instruction *eat this and you will recover.* He did. Afterwards he blessed bread for the sick of the town every day, and the practice — *pane di San Nicola* — has not stopped in eight hundred years.

His other gift was for the dead. He saw them. They came to him in his cell, especially in the days after Mass, asking him to offer the sacrifice for them; he kept a register of the names. The Augustinian *Office for the Dead* still credits him with formalizing the friars' practice of constant Masses for souls in purgatory. He died on the tenth of September, 1305, at sixty years old. His body was found incorrupt — the Bollandists devote a long chapter to the inquests — and remains so. The town that took him in has not given him back.

> *"Eat this and you will recover."* — *the Virgin to Nicholas, in the vision of the bread*

## September 11

# Paphnutius of Egypt

*Bishop, confessor of Nicaea, c. 270–c. 360*

He had been a desert monk under Anthony the Great when Diocletian's persecution caught up with him, around the year 308. Maximinus Daia, ruling the eastern provinces, had ordered a new refinement of cruelty for those who would not sacrifice — they were not to be killed but disfigured. The right eye was put out with a sharpened iron; the tendons behind the left knee were severed with a hot blade so the man could never again walk straight. Then they were sent into the copper mines of Phaeno, at the edge of the Red Sea desert, to die slowly of work. Paphnutius was sent with the others. He survived. When Constantine published the Edict of Milan in 313 the survivors came back, hobbling, half-blind, and went back to the cells they had left.

He was elected bishop of an unknown see in the Upper Thebaid not long afterward. When the great council met at Nicaea in 325 he was carried there with the other Egyptian bishops — Athanasius was a deacon in his train — and Constantine, on first meeting him, recognized the empty socket and the dragging leg and bent down and kissed both. Rufinus and Socrates both preserve what happened next. The council was debating whether to require celibacy of all clergy — a proposal that had support from many of the bishops, especially from Spain. Paphnutius rose and spoke against it. *Marriage is honorable,* he said, *and the bed undefiled. Do not lay on others a yoke neither we nor our fathers were able to bear.* The Bollandists note that he had himself been celibate from his youth. The council heard him out and adjusted the canon: the church would receive into orders married men, and married priests would keep their wives.

He returned to Egypt, served as bishop into great age, helped Athanasius against the Arians, and died in his cell sometime around 360, eyepatch and limp and all. The man who had been disfigured for refusing to give up Christ spent the rest of his life keeping the church from disfiguring others.

> *"Do not lay on others the yoke their fathers were not able to bear."* — Paphnutius, at Nicaea

## September 12

# Ailbe of Emly

*Bishop, second Patrick, patron of Munster, d. c. 528*

The Irish writers call him *alter Patricius* — the second Patrick — and the title is not entirely figurative. Ailbe (Albeus, Elvius) was perhaps a generation older than Patrick of Armagh, perhaps an exact contemporary; the medieval Irish chronologies give him a long life and a death in 528. The legend that he was preaching the gospel in Munster before Patrick landed in Ulster has the shape of southern pride and possibly some truth — there were Christians in Ireland before Patrick was sent, and the southern province was likelier to have them than the northern, given the trade with Roman Britain.

The vitae the Bollandists know — Hugh Ward sent them three Latin manuscripts from the Irish Franciscans at Louvain in the 1630s, drawn from older Kilkenny and Inishbofin codices — give Ailbe a charming and probably half-fabulous origin. He was the son of a slave woman of the king of Cashel; she exposed the infant on a hillside; a she-wolf found him and nursed him among her cubs; British traders saw him crawling among the wolves, redeemed him from his master, and took him east to be baptized. He returned an old man, walked the length of Munster preaching, and finally fixed his cathedral at *Imelaca* — modern Emly, in Tipperary — beside a small lake which had nearly dried up by the Bollandists' day. The see he founded was the chief seat of Munster Christianity for a thousand years, until in 1568 the Tudor parliament suppressed it and joined it to Cashel.

What survives in the carmen of Bishop Cumin of Connor, preserved by Philip O'Sullivan and printed by the Bollandists, is the line his own people remembered: *Albeus æterna fuerit mihi laude canendus; / haud scio, num vivat largior ulla manus.* — *Ailbe will be sung in praise forever; I do not know if any hand alive is more open than his.* The boy the wolves had kept alive grew up to keep the poor alive. Cashel forgot his see; the country he served has not forgotten him.

> *"I do not know if any hand alive is more open than his."* — *Cumin of Connor, on Ailbe*

## September 13

# Maurilius of Angers

*Bishop, hidden gardener, c. 363–c. 437*

He was born at Milan to wealthy parents, was sent into Gaul as a young man to escape the unrest in northern Italy, and ended up at Tours in the household of Martin — the old soldier-bishop whose monastery at Marmoutier was, by the 380s, training a generation of Gallo-Roman clergy in the new Egyptian style of monastic life. Maurilius stayed long enough to be ordained priest. When the see of Angers fell vacant around 423 the clergy and people sent for him, and he was consecrated bishop against his wishes — the formal protest of the unwilling bishop, a *recusatio* almost ritual by then, was in his case sincere. He served the diocese for fourteen years.

His biographer Magnobodus, writing two centuries later, fills the period with stories the Bollandists handle with patient skepticism: a serpent expelled from a hilltop near Calonnes that allowed Maurilius to found a monastery there; the dead son of a noblewoman raised at the bishop's prayer; demons driven out, lepers cleansed, the usual furniture of an early Gallic *vita.* What seems to be older and may be true is the central episode: Maurilius, accused of accidentally letting a baby die unbaptized at a confirmation visit, fled his see in shame, took ship to Britain, took service under a false name as a gardener for a king there, and remained in hiding for seven years. The Angevin clergy hunted him down. They found him in his garden. They brought him home.

He resumed the office, took up the work, and died fourteen years after that on a September Sunday in his cathedral church, around the year 437. They buried him in a double crypt outside the city walls he had built for himself before he died. Four centuries later, when Bishop Nefingus translated the relics into a finer reliquary at the cathedral around 970, the body was found, the witnesses said, *ita inventa sunt incorrupta, acsi ipsa die* — *as though it had been laid there that day.* The bishop who had once gone away in shame had not been moved since.

> *"As though it had been laid there that day."* — *the witnesses at the translation of his relics, c. 970*

## September 14

# Cornelius and Cyprian

*A pope and a bishop, friends in the third-century church, d. 253 and 258*

When the persecution of Decius lifted in 251 the church at Rome, which had been without a bishop for fourteen months — Fabian was killed in January 250 and the cathedra had stood vacant under the imperial ban — finally elected Cornelius. He was a Roman of the Cornelii Centumcelli, an old name; he had been a presbyter in the city for twenty years; he was, by every account, a quiet man. The election unleashed a year of fighting. Novatian, the brilliant theologian who had carried the church through the vacancy and who held that the *lapsi* — those who had sacrificed to the gods under torture — could not be readmitted to communion at all, refused Cornelius and had himself ordained as anti-pope. The eastern and African churches had to choose. Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, the most respected voice in Latin Christianity, wrote in his support. The choice held. Cornelius was the lawful Roman bishop; Novatian was a schism.

The two men corresponded for the next two years. Cyprian's seventeen surviving letters to Cornelius are among the warmest in the patristic corpus — *carissimo fratri,* dearest brother, almost every one. They worked out the discipline together: the lapsed could be readmitted after lengthy penance, but not denied entirely. The church's unity, Cyprian argued, was not a club whose members were the perfect; it was a hospital. Cornelius died in 253 at Centumcellae, exiled there by Gallus's revival of the persecution, and was buried in the catacomb of Callixtus on the Appian Way. The Bollandists print the legendary *acta* — the soldier Cerealis whose paralytic wife Salustia Cornelius healed, the mass conversion of the guard, the twenty-one martyrs decapitated outside the Appian Gate — and they handle the legends with their usual care, but the bones in Callixtus are real.

Five years later Cyprian was beheaded at Carthage under Valerian, on the same day. The Roman calendar pairs them now as it always has: a Latin pope and a Latin bishop, the men who together held the third-century church to its unity through the worst of its disputes about its own discipline. They never met. They are remembered together.

> *"There can be no other bishop, while one bishop holds the chair."* — Cyprian, *Letter* 68 to Cornelius

## September 15

# Catherine of Genoa

*Mystic, hospital matron, theologian of purgatory, 1447–1510*

She was the youngest daughter of the Fieschi, one of the four great Genoese houses, born in a palazzo and married at sixteen to Giuliano Adorno, of the rival house, in a contract designed to end the feud. The contract held. The marriage was a disaster. Giuliano was unfaithful, wasteful, often violent; she spent her early twenties in a depression so deep her sister finally insisted she go to confession at the convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie, in March 1473. While she was kneeling at the *prie-dieu* — she had been there perhaps a minute, the *Vita* says, and had not yet spoken — she was struck in the heart by what she would describe afterwards as the immediate, overwhelming, nonverbal love of God. She walked out a different woman.

She did not enter religious life. She did not leave her husband. Giuliano went bankrupt three years later, lost the palazzo, and in his ruin had his own conversion; the two of them moved into two rooms beside the great Pammatone hospital and began to nurse the poor of Genoa. He died in 1497. She continued at the hospital for another thirteen years, eventually as its matron during the plague years of 1493 and 1501, when she ran the wards and managed the books and stayed when other women fled. Her *Treatise on Purgatory* — one of the strangest and most beautiful works of late-medieval mysticism — was dictated to her confessor in those years. *The fire that purifies the souls of the elect,* she wrote, *is the same fire that beatifies them. It is the love of God, become unbearable to a soul not yet ready to bear it. The pain is the joy not yet large enough.*

She died on a September morning in 1510, having taken nothing for forty days but the Eucharist. The Bollandists, drawing on the contemporary testimony of Augustine Justiniani, who saw her uncorrupt body twenty-five years later, treat her as one of the great cases of the late mystical tradition — Doctor *purgatorii,* in the Latin manuals. The hospital ward she ran is gone. The fire she described has not.

> *"The pain is the joy not yet large enough."* — Catherine, *Treatise on Purgatory*

## September 16

# Euphemia

*Virgin-martyr of Chalcedon, witness to a council, c. 290–304*

She was a young woman of Chalcedon, the senatorial daughter of a Christian house — Bithynia had been quietly Christian for two generations by the time the great persecution opened in 303 — when the proconsul Priscus published Diocletian's fourth edict and required everyone in the city to sacrifice. Forty-nine of the local Christians refused. They were rounded up and brought to the forum. Forty-eight were sent to Rome for trial. Priscus kept Euphemia. She was the most prominent — the family was known — and he believed that if she could be made to recant, the others would follow.

The acts the Bollandists print walk through the standard catalogue of imperial torture: the rack, the wheel, the millstones, the heated frying-pan, the fire of the furnace into which she was thrown but which (the witnesses said) parted around her like water; finally the arena, where the bears who had been starved for the show declined to touch her, and a single bear, prodded forward, gave her a small wound that killed her. She was nineteen. Her father carried the body a mile out of the city and buried her on the shore of the Bosphorus.

The shrine that grew over her grave became, a century and a half later, the most consequential basilica in the eastern church. In 451 the bishops of the world came to Chalcedon to settle the question of how the divine and human natures held together in Christ, and they met in Euphemia's basilica, with her relics under the high altar. The legend the Greeks tell — and that the Bollandists are too Latin to swear to but too honest to omit — is that the council, after weeks of debate, drew up two creeds, the orthodox and the monophysite, sealed both, and laid them on the breast of the young martyr in her tomb. They locked the church and watched it for three nights. When they opened the tomb on the fourth morning, Euphemia was holding the orthodox creed in her right hand. The other was at her feet.

Whatever happened in the basilica that morning, the council that came out of it shaped Christian doctrine for fifteen hundred years. The girl who would not sacrifice held the church together when its own bishops nearly could not.

> *"In Chalcedonian shores, the martyr Euphemia consecrates the holy strand with virgin blood."* — Paulinus of Nola, *Carmen* on Saint Felix

## September 17

# Hildegard of Bingen

*Benedictine abbess, visionary, polymath of the Rhineland, 1098–1179*

She was a small, sickly child, the tenth in a noble Rhineland family, and her parents — following the medieval custom of dedicating the tithe of one's children to God — gave her at the age of eight to the anchorhold of a young noblewoman named Jutta of Sponheim, attached to the monastery of Disibodenberg above the Nahe. She was enclosed with Jutta in a single cell. She would live in monastic enclosure for the next seventy years. When Jutta died in 1136, the small community of women that had grown up around her elected Hildegard their *magistra,* and the visions she had been having since her fifth year — and had told no one about for forty — began to insist on being written down.

Her two contemporary biographers, Godfrey and Theodoric, both monks of her own community, watched her work for the next forty-three years. She wrote *Scivias,* the great vision-cycle in which Pope Eugenius III recognized authentic prophecy and which the Council of Trier formally approved in 1147; she wrote two sequel-volumes, *Liber vitae meritorum* and *Liber divinorum operum;* she wrote a natural-history encyclopedia (*Physica*), a medical handbook (*Causae et curae*), nine settings of the Mass, seventy-seven antiphons and responsories — the *Symphonia armoniae caelestium revelationum* is one of the great twelfth-century song-cycles — a morality play with music that may be the first of its kind, almost four hundred letters, and a private language called *Lingua Ignota.* She left Disibodenberg in 1150 over the abbot's objections and founded her own house at Rupertsberg, on a hill above the Rhine outside Bingen. She made four preaching tours up the river in her sixties and seventies — an enclosed nun, preaching in cathedrals to bishops and crowds — and rebuked the emperor Frederick Barbarossa to his face. She quarreled with the Mainz cathedral chapter over a burial in her last year, was placed under interdict, and won.

She died on the seventeenth of September, 1179, at eighty-one years old. The local cult was immediate; the formal canonization arrived eight hundred and thirty-three years later, in 2012, when Benedict XVI declared her a Doctor of the Church. The girl walled into a stone room had not, it turned out, been silenced.

> *"Listen: there was once a king sitting on his throne. Around him stood great and wonderfully beautiful columns, ornamented with ivory…"* — Hildegard, opening of *Scivias*

## September 18

# Joseph of Cupertino

*Franciscan friar, ecstatic, patron of slow students, 1603–1663*

He was born in a stable behind his parents' house at Cupertino, a small town on the heel of Italy between Lecce and Nardò, on the seventeenth of June, 1603 — his father, a poor carpenter, had had to leave the house to escape his creditors and his mother, Francesca, gave birth in the outbuilding where she had taken refuge. Joseph grew up dreamy, half-deaf, prone to long staring fits the village called *bocca aperta,* the open-mouthed boy. He could barely read. The Conventual Franciscans of Grottella turned him away. The Capuchins took him as a lay brother and dismissed him after eight months as too clumsy for the kitchen. The Conventuals reconsidered, took him back as a stable hand, and discovered, slowly, that the slowness was the surface of something else.

He was ordained in 1628 — not because he passed his exams (he did not) but because the bishop, examining the candidates, opened the book at random to the only passage Joseph had managed to learn, *Beata viscera Mariae Virginis,* and let him through. From that day until his death thirty-five years later he was almost continuously in some form of ecstasy. He levitated. The Bollandists, working from the canonization processes Pastrovicchi summarizes for them, count seventy occasions in which sworn witnesses saw him rise from the floor, sometimes in his cell, often in choir, once in the open during a procession, once at the altar at the elevation, once carrying a heavy wooden cross to the top of a hill at Grottella while gripping it lightly between two fingertips. The phenomenon was so disruptive that his superiors moved him from convent to convent for thirty years to keep crowds from forming. He was forbidden to say Mass in public. He spent his last decade under a kind of monastic house arrest at Osimo, hidden from pilgrims, allowed only the company of his few brothers.

He died on a September evening in 1663, calmly, after the *Salve Regina.* The Bollandists print Pastrovicchi's careful 1753 *Vita,* and the canonization came in 1767. The slow boy who could not pass an exam became, of all things, the patron of students sitting examinations. *Bocca aperta* meant something else by then.

> *"Beata viscera Mariae Virginis."* — *the only passage Joseph could quote, on which the bishop made him a priest*

## September 19

# Theodore of Tarsus

*Greek monk, archbishop of Canterbury, organizer of the English church, c. 602–690*

He was born at Tarsus in Cilicia — Saint Paul's city — and grew up bilingual in Greek and Syriac, perhaps trilingual if one counts the Latin he picked up later. He studied at Antioch and Constantinople in the 620s, fled the Arab conquest of his homeland in the 640s, and ended up in Rome as an old man, a learned Greek monk in the Asian community on the Aventine, sixty-five years old and expecting nothing further from his life.

In 667 the church at Canterbury, two years bereft of an archbishop after the death of Wighard, asked Pope Vitalian for a candidate. Vitalian asked Hadrian, an African abbot whom the pope had wanted for the post. Hadrian declined and pointed at Theodore. The pope, after consulting the candidate's age and consenting, tonsured him in the Roman fashion (he had worn the Eastern monastic tonsure), waited four months for the hair to grow back, ordained him bishop, and put him on a ship for England.

He was sixty-eight when he stepped off at Canterbury in May 669. He would live another twenty-two years and reorganize the church of an entire kingdom. Bede, who was a child when Theodore died and grew up with men who had known him, gives him the warmest portrait in the *Ecclesiastical History.* He was, Bede writes, *the first archbishop to whom the whole English church consented to give its hand.* He toured every kingdom; he settled the date of Easter once and for all in Northumbria; he divided the over-large dioceses (this is what put him at odds with Wilfrid of York for the rest of his life); he convened the first English national council at Hertford in 672, where the bishops of seven kingdoms sat down together for the first time and signed nine canons that would govern the church for centuries.

The school he and Hadrian opened at Canterbury — Greek, Latin, computus, music, astronomy, scripture — produced a generation of English scholars whose Latin Bede still envied. He died at Canterbury at eighty-eight, on a September morning in 690. The English church he found in eight separate dioceses he left as one. It has held that shape since.

> *"The first archbishop to whom the whole English church consented to give its hand."* — Bede, *Historia Ecclesiastica*

## September 20

# John of Egypt

*Blind confessor, martyr at Caesarea, d. 310*

He was an Egyptian Christian sent into the copper mines of Phaeno around 308, in the same group that took Paphnutius — the same disfiguring sentence had been applied to him: the right eye gouged with a sharpened iron, the left leg crippled with a hot blade, then the desert mines for what was meant to be the rest of his short life. John was already blind in both eyes by the time the executioners came; the older injury seems to have been from an earlier round of torture, and the cauterizing of the second eye was, the Bollandists note from Eusebius, simply the standard sentence applied to a man on whom it could no longer change anything. He was sent to the mines all the same.

What Eusebius preserves of him in book thirteen of the *Martyrs of Palestine* is one of the strangest and most intimate portraits of an early-fourth-century saint. John could not work the rock — by then he could not see, could not stand straight — and was kept apart from the labor gangs with a few other infirm confessors, in a cell where they prayed and fasted together. Eusebius, who knew him personally, says that John had memorized the entire Bible. The whole of it. Not on stone tablets, the historian writes, *not on parchment that worms and time consume, but in the tablets of the heart, in the soul itself, white and clear, in the pure light of the mind.* When the small assembly of confessors gathered for prayer John would stand in the middle of them and recite — sometimes from Moses, sometimes from the prophets, sometimes a stretch of gospel or epistle — and Eusebius, the first time he heard him, walked forward thinking the blind man must be reading from a book. He was not. He was reciting from inside himself.

In the autumn of 310 the prefect Maximinus, frustrated that the disfigured Christians were not dying as fast as he had wished, ordered them all executed together. Thirty-nine of them, including the bishop Silvanus of Gaza and the blind John, were beheaded outside Caesarea on a single morning. Eusebius watched. *I saw,* he wrote afterwards, *what truly is a man — not what appears in the outward shape of the body, but what dwells in the soul and in the mind.*

> *"Quippe corpore licet mutilato, nihilominus majorem facultatis suæ vim ac virtutem exerebat."* — Eusebius, *de Martyribus Palæstinæ* 13: *though his body was disfigured, the more strength of soul he showed*

## September 21

# Matthew the Apostle

*Tax-collector, evangelist, fl. c. 30, d. by tradition in Ethiopia*

He was sitting at the customs-table at Capernaum on the road from Damascus when Jesus walked past with the first of the disciples and said to him, *Follow me.* The synoptic gospels all preserve the moment, and Matthew's own gospel — which the early church always took to be his — preserves it with a brevity that has the force of an unembellished memory. *And he arose and followed him. And it came to pass, as Jesus sat at meat in the house, behold many publicans and sinners came and sat down with him and his disciples.* He had been a Levi son of Alphaeus, paid by Herod's tetrarchy to collect taxes for Rome at the customs-post on the Sea of Galilee, the despised middle-man who took his cut. He left the table.

The Bollandists work patiently through the medieval *acta* — the legend of the apostle's mission to Aethiopia, his confrontation with the magicians Zaroes and Arphaxat, his raising of the king's daughter, his martyrdom under the king's brother Hirtacus who wanted the consecrated virgin Iphigenia for himself — and conclude what the early church already knew: most of the *acta* is fabulous, descended from a sixth-century pseudo-Abdias the historians have learned to discount. What survives, however, is older. Eusebius and Origen and Clement of Alexandria all knew that Matthew had preached east of Palestine after the dispersion of the apostles in 42, that he had written his gospel for Hebrew Christians and probably first in Aramaic before the Greek redaction, and that he had ended in martyrdom somewhere on the upper Nile or beyond it — *Aethiopia* in the broad ancient sense, perhaps Axum, perhaps further south, perhaps Persia. He is venerated under the high altar at Salerno, where his relics were translated in 954 from Brittany, where they had been hidden during the Saracen raids, having come west across the Mediterranean a long way from where the apostle laid them down.

What he himself wrote of the call he received outranks anything written about him afterwards. *Follow me,* the Lord said, and he arose. The Greek of his own gospel uses the same verb — *anastas* — that the gospels use, after the resurrection, for the Lord. Matthew got up.

> *"And he arose, and followed him."* — Matthew on his own call, *Matthew* 9:9

## September 22

# Maurice and the Theban Legion

*Officer and his soldiers, martyred at Agaunum in the Alps, c. 287*

The legion was Egyptian — recruited from the Christian population of the upper Thebaid where Pachomius would soon be founding monasteries — and it had been moved across the empire in the 280s to reinforce Maximian's campaigns against the Bagaudae of Gaul. By the late summer of 287 the legion was crossing the Pennine Alps toward the Rhone valley, billeted at Agaunum in the narrow gorge of the Valais where the river squeezes between cliffs. The emperor had ordered every unit of the marching army to sacrifice to the gods before the next engagement and to put any Christians among them to death. Maurice, the *primicerius* — first centurion of the legion — refused on his own behalf and on behalf of his men.

The standard hagiography preserved by Eucherius of Lyon a century and a half later, which the Bollandists print after weighing it and finding it substantially defensible, gives the speech he sent back to the emperor. *We are your soldiers,* he wrote, *but we are also Christ's. We owe you our military service; we owe him our innocence. From you we accept our pay; from him we have life. We cannot follow our emperor into a denial of our God. If we are required to do further wickedness — to shed innocent blood, to persecute the faithful — we will rather suffer the sword. We are armed and we will not resist. We will die rather than live by killing the innocent.*

The emperor decimated the legion: every tenth man pulled out of line and beheaded. The survivors held. He decimated them again. And again. The Bollandists are careful with the round number — six thousand six hundred Christians is the medieval count, and probably an exaggeration — but the core of the story they regard as ancient and authentic. A whole unit of imperial soldiers, in the hour they had been trained for, refused.

The bodies were left where they fell at the river-bend below the cliffs of Agaunum. A Christian community grew up around the burial. Theodore, bishop of Octodurum, raised a small church over them around 380; Sigismund of Burgundy expanded it in 515; the abbey of Saint-Maurice d'Agaune that grew from those buildings is the oldest continuously functioning monastery north of the Alps. The men who refused to draw their swords against their fellow Christians have not been disturbed in fifteen hundred years.

> *"We are your soldiers, but we are also Christ's."* — Maurice, in the message to Maximian

## September 23

# Adamnan of Iona

*Abbot, biographer, peacemaker, c. 624–704*

He was born in Donegal a generation after Columba died, a kinsman of the Cenél Conaill — Columba's own people — and entered Iona as a young monk under the abbot Seghine, who had himself been trained by men who remembered the founder. He was elected ninth abbot of Iona in 679, in his middle fifties, and held the office for twenty-five years. The community he inherited was the head of a great federation of Columban houses spread from western Ireland to the Hebrides to Lindisfarne — the largest monastic network in the British Isles at the time — and the most pressing piece of unfinished business the founder had left him was the question of when to keep Easter. Iona kept the old Celtic computation; Rome and most of the rest of the church kept the newer Alexandrian calendar; the difference could put the date as much as a month apart. Adamnan, after a visit to Wearmouth-Jarrow in 686 where he met Bede's master Ceolfrid, became persuaded that the Roman date was correct. He spent the rest of his life trying, and largely failing, to bring his own community along. Iona did not adopt the Roman Easter until eleven years after his death.

He wrote two books that have outlasted the controversy. *De Locis Sanctis* — a careful guidebook to the holy places of the East, written down from the eyewitness account of a Frankish bishop named Arculf who had been shipwrecked on Iona on his way back from Jerusalem — became the standard pilgrim's reference of the early medieval church and was abridged by Bede a generation later. *Vita Sancti Columbae* — three books on the founder, drawn from the community's living memory and the records kept since Columba's death in 597 — is one of the most beautiful early-medieval saints' lives.

His other great work was political. In 697, at the synod of Birr in the Irish midlands, he gathered the bishops and kings of Ireland and persuaded them to enact what is still called *Cáin Adomnáin* — the Law of the Innocents — the first written legislation in Western Europe to forbid the killing of women, children, and clergy in war. He died on Iona in his eightieth year. The biography of Columba he had written, the law he had given Ireland, the Easter he could not quite carry: three works of patience, two of which lasted.

> *"In every conflict, spare the women, the children, and those who pray."* — *Cáin Adomnáin,* 697

## September 24

# Gerard of Csanád

*Venetian monk, missionary bishop, martyr of the Magyars, c. 980–1046*

He was born Gerardo Sagredo to a noble Venetian family, entered the Benedictine abbey of San Giorgio Maggiore as a boy, was elected abbot in his early thirties, and resigned the office to set out for the Holy Land. He never got there. Stopped in Hungary on the way through, he was recognized by King Stephen — who had been gathering missionary monks for the new Christian kingdom for twenty years — and persuaded to stay. Stephen made him tutor to the prince Imre, then sent him into the southern marches of the kingdom, the wild country between the Danube and the Tisza where the Magyar tribes were still half-pagan and the priests were few. Gerard fixed his see at Csanád on the Maros river, the first bishopric of southeastern Hungary, and stayed there for fifteen years.

He was a Benedictine of the strict observance: rose at midnight to chant the office, kept perpetual silence except for preaching and confession, ate one meal a day. His preaching was famous — he learned enough Magyar to preach in it — and he wrote, while bishop, a biblical commentary called the *Deliberatio supra hymnum trium puerorum,* one of the earliest surviving works of theology composed in central Europe, in which he meditated on Daniel 3 and the witness of those who would not bow to the king's image. The Bollandists print the older eleventh-century *Vita,* careful to distinguish it from a fifteenth-century interpolation that mixes legend into the simpler narrative.

When King Stephen died in 1038 the Christian settlement of Hungary nearly came apart. The pagan party rose under a chieftain named Vata; the next two kings were assassinated within four years; the missionary bishops were marked. In September 1046 Gerard was traveling toward Buda to meet the new king when his party was attacked at the ford of the Danube below Pest. He was sixty-six. The pagan warriors stoned him, broke his cross over his head, and threw him over the cliff into the river, on what is still called Gellért Hill in Budapest — the *montis Gerardi.* The body was recovered that night and taken back to Csanád, where Stephen's grandnephew Ladislaus elevated his relics in 1083 alongside Stephen and Imre. The Magyar nation he had helped baptize keeps his memory still: *Szent Gellért,* the bishop of the bridge.

> *"Suscipiam coronam, quam mihi Dominus paravit."* — *Gerard, when warned of the ambush at the Danube ford*

## September 25

# Cleophas

*The Emmaus disciple, kinsman of the Lord, fl. 30, d. by tradition c. 36*

He is named once in Luke and once in John, and the tradition has spent two thousand years trying to fit the two mentions into one man. Luke 24 calls him *Kleopas,* one of the two disciples walking out from Jerusalem on the afternoon of the first Easter — leaving the city, the gospel says, in defeat — when a stranger fell into step with them on the road. They told him about the women's report from the empty tomb and about their own crushed hopes; *we had hoped,* they said, *that it had been he which should have redeemed Israel.* The stranger walked seven miles with them opening the scriptures, sat down at their table at Emmaus, took the bread, broke it, and at that moment they recognized him. He vanished. They got up — *eadem hora,* the Vulgate has, *the same hour* — and walked the seven miles back to Jerusalem in the dark.

John's gospel mentions a *Klopas* once, the husband of one of the Marys at the foot of the cross. The early church almost universally took the two Cleophases to be the same man, and Hegesippus (preserved in Eusebius) makes him further the brother of Joseph the husband of the Virgin — which would make him the Lord's uncle by marriage, and his son Simeon, who became the second bishop of Jerusalem, the Lord's cousin. The Bollandists, working through Bede and Ado and the Roman *Parvum* and the Greek menologies, treat the genealogy as plausible if unprovable. What they treat as more secure is the old Latin tradition the Roman martyrology preserves: that Cleophas was killed at his own house in Emmaus, perhaps in the dispersion of the Jerusalem disciples after the death of Stephen around 36, and was buried in the room where he had once given Christ supper.

He was not an apostle, not an evangelist, not a writer; the church does not even know what day he died. What it remembers is the conversation on the road. He had walked seven miles in defeat. He walked seven miles back in joy. Most discipleship happens in the time between.

> *"Did not our hearts burn within us, while he talked with us by the way, and while he opened to us the scriptures?"* — Cleophas to his companion, *Luke* 24:32

## September 26

# Cyprian and Justina

*A magician converted, a virgin who held, martyrs of Nicomedia, c. 304*

He was not the Cyprian of Carthage who fell to the sword twelve days earlier in the calendar — the Bollandists are firm about the distinction — but a different man, a pagan magician of Antioch in Syria, son of priestly families on both sides who had been initiated into the mysteries at Athens, Argos, Sparta, Eleusis, Phrygia, and finally at Memphis under the priests of Egypt. He had become, by the time he settled at Antioch, the most accomplished sorcerer in the East. He had a paying clientele. One of them, a young Greek named Aglaides, came to him in the spring of 304 because he was in love with a Christian girl in the city named Justina, daughter of a converted priest of Apollo, and Justina would not have him.

Cyprian undertook the commission. He sent the demon of fornication — first one, then a second more powerful, then the prince of all of them, the demon his arts were strongest at compelling. None of them could touch her. She prayed; she fasted; she wore the cross openly at her throat; the demons returned to Cyprian humiliated and were sent back; they returned again; they would not go a fourth time. Cyprian, in the dialogue Gregory of Nazianzen preserves in his eighteenth oration, asked the strongest of his servants what kind of being it was that they could not move. *One,* the demon answered, *who has the sign of the crucified.* Cyprian understood, in that moment, that the powers he had served were neither what he had thought them to be nor as strong as what stood against them. He went home, burned his books in the public square in front of his neighbors, and presented himself for baptism at the Antiochene church.

Justina forgave him. He was eventually ordained, then made bishop of his own city; she became deaconess and abbess of a community of consecrated women under his pastoral care. The persecution caught up with them both. They were arrested by the prefect at Damascus, transferred to Nicomedia, and beheaded together with a young man named Theoctistus who had stepped forward at the trial to confess himself a Christian on the spot. Their bodies were thrown to the dogs and recovered by Christian sailors who carried them to Rome, where they have been venerated ever since at the Lateran. The magician who had tried to deliver an innocent woman to a young man's lust was delivered, by her, to Christ.

> *"One who has the sign of the crucified."* — *the demon's answer to Cyprian, on Justina*

## September 27

# Cosmas and Damian

*Arabian twin physicians, the silverless ones, martyrs at Aegae, c. 287*

They were Arabian Christians, twin brothers — *gemini,* the *acta* call them — born to a noblewoman named Theodota who had brought them up alone after their father died, raising them in the faith and in the medical art that ran in their mother's family. They studied at Syria and Cilicia, settled at Aegae on the Cilician coast across the gulf from Cyprus, opened a small dispensary near the harbor, and began treating the poor of the town. Their distinguishing practice — the one that gave them their Greek name, *anargyroi,* the silverless — was that they would not accept payment. Not from the poor, who could not have paid. Not from the rich, who would have. The Bollandists print the long *acta* preserved in Bede and Ado: a steady traffic of the lame and the lepers and the demon-tormented, healings credited not to their skill but to their prayers, three brothers — Anthimus, Leontius, Euprepius — who joined the practice and worked alongside them.

The persecution found them in 287, perhaps 297; the dating is uncertain, but the prefect Lysias was real and the trial is preserved in the protocol shape of authentic court records. The twins were arrested with their three brothers and brought before him. Asked their names and their country, Cosmas answered for all five: *We are from Arabia. Our names are Cosmas and Damian. We are Christians. We have brothers also, Anthimus, Leontius, and Euprepius.* The order was for sacrifice. They refused. Lysias tried, in turn — the *acta* are stylized but consistent — bound and thrown into the sea, from which an angel released them; bound and cast into a fire, which would not burn them; lapidated, the stones turning back on the stone-throwers; shot at with arrows, the arrows returning likewise; finally, when the spectacle had run out and the prefect was tired, beheaded.

The twin brothers who had refused to accept silver became the patrons of the medical guilds. Felix IV built them a basilica in the Roman Forum in 526 — the *santi Cosma e Damiano* still stands, the apse mosaic among the great sixth-century works of Christian art — and the Medici family of Florence took them as patrons because of the punning likeness of *medici* to physicians. Their feast remains, in the East and the West, a day for praying over those who treat the sick.

> *"We are Christians. We have brothers also: Anthimus, Leontius, and Euprepius."* — Cosmas, before Lysias

## September 28

# Wenceslaus of Bohemia

*Duke and martyr, the good king of the carol, c. 907–935*

His grandmother Ludmila, who had been baptized along with her husband Bořivoj by Methodius the Apostle of the Slavs, raised him in the new religion against the strong opposition of his pagan mother Drahomíra. When his father died young in battle around 921, Drahomíra became regent and had Ludmila strangled in her oratory at Tetín to break the Christian party at court. Wenceslaus, fourteen, was sent into hiding. He came back at eighteen, took the throne with the support of the bishops and the German emperor Henry the Fowler, sent his mother into internal exile, and began to govern.

His biographer Christian — a monk of Břevnov writing within fifty years of the events, himself a great-grandnephew of the duke — gives him the warmest possible portrait. He kept perpetual chastity in his palace, rose before dawn for the office, walked barefoot to Mass through the snow even in winter, brought corn to widows and firewood to the cottages above the Vltava, paid the tribute to the emperor without resentment, founded the church of Saint Vitus on the hill where the cathedral now stands, gathered priests to teach his people, and — the hardest part — pardoned his enemies repeatedly, including his younger brother Boleslav, who hated him from boyhood and resented being kept from the throne.

In September 935 Boleslav invited him to the dedication of a new church at his estate at Stará Boleslav, a day's ride down the Elbe. Wenceslaus came. The two brothers ate together, drank together, parted for the night embracing, and the next morning, as Wenceslaus walked alone to the early Mass, Boleslav and three of his retainers met him at the chapel door. Wenceslaus saw the swords and said, very quietly — Christian preserves the line — *Frater, Dominus tecum sit.* *Brother, may the Lord be with you.* Boleslav struck him down on the threshold. He was twenty-eight.

The body was carried back to Prague and buried in the church of Saint Vitus he had built; the cult was immediate and immense; within a generation Boleslav himself, in old age and remorse, was begging for his brother's intercession. The English know him from the carol — *Good King Wenceslas looked out, on the Feast of Stephen* — but he was not a king and the Stephen of the carol is the day after Christmas, not his day. What the carol does have right is the open hand: bread and pine-logs to a poor man's house in deep snow, and a master who would carry the load himself when the page was failing.

> *"Frater, Dominus tecum sit."* — Wenceslaus, at the chapel door at Stará Boleslav

## September 29

# Michael, and All the Holy Angels

*Archangel and the heavenly host, feast known as Michaelmas*

The day was originally a dedication. Around the year 490 a herdsman named Gargano, looking for a stray bull on the rocky promontory that bears his name in southern Italy, found the animal kneeling at the mouth of a cave high on Monte Sant'Angelo. He shot an arrow at it; the arrow turned in the air and struck the herdsman. The local bishop, hearing the story, fasted and prayed; an angel appeared and identified himself as Michael, *guardian of this place,* and asked for a church on the cave. The shrine raised over the cave's entrance — the basilica of San Michele Arcangelo on Monte Gargano — became, with Saint James at Compostela and the tomb of the apostles in Rome, one of the three great pilgrimage sites of medieval Western Christianity. Its dedication day, the twenty-ninth of September, the Latin church kept thereafter as the feast of Michael and all the angels.

The Bollandists trace the spread of the cult: the second basilica that Pope Boniface raised at Rome on the Circus Maximus in the seventh century; the third on the Via Salaria seven miles from the city; the great Norman foundation of Mont-Saint-Michel rising out of the tide on the border of Normandy and Brittany after a vision in 708; the further chain of Michael shrines that ran the whole length of the *via Sacra Langobardorum* down the Italian peninsula and across the Channel to Cornwall and Skellig Michael off the Irish coast. The angel of the Apocalypse who fights the dragon, of Daniel who guards the people of Israel, of Jude's letter who would not bring railing accusation against the devil but said only *the Lord rebuke thee* — Michael is the angel the church set as the patron of soldiers, of the dying, of high places, of the moment when one age yields to another.

The day in the West has always belonged to the angels generally. The Eastern church keeps the *Synaxis of the Bodiless Powers* on November 8 — Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Uriel, the cherubim, the seraphim, the thrones, the dominions — but the Latin tradition has kept the autumn feast as a feast of the whole heavenly host. The crops are in by the twenty-ninth in northern Europe; the rents are due; the equinox has just turned. The angels who watch the changing of the seasons are remembered when the season changes.

> *"Quis ut Deus?"* — *Michael's name in Hebrew: Who is like God?*

## September 30

# Jerome

*Translator, polemicist, hermit of Bethlehem, c. 347–c. 420*

He was born at Stridon on the border between Dalmatia and Pannonia, a small frontier town the barbarians erased from the map within his lifetime. He was sent to Rome as a teenager to study grammar and rhetoric under the great Donatus, baptized there in his late teens by Pope Liberius, took up the Christian life slowly — he confesses in the letters that he loved Cicero too well, that the famous dream in which the Lord rebuked him as *Ciceronianus, non Christianus, more Ciceronian than Christian,* was the turning. He went east in his thirties, into the Syrian desert at Chalcis, where he lived for four years as a hermit, learning Hebrew from a converted rabbi to give his mind something to grip while it broke its more delicate addictions. The languages stayed with him. The mood, mostly, did not.

He returned to Rome in 382 as secretary to Pope Damasus, who set him the task that would consume the rest of his life: a single Latin Bible to replace the chaos of competing Old Latin versions then circulating. Damasus died in 384; Jerome left Rome under a cloud — the Roman clergy who had hoped to succeed Damasus had not appreciated his blunt assessment of their morals — and went back to the East with Paula and her household. They settled at Bethlehem in 386. Paula founded a monastery for women on the slope below the basilica of the Nativity; Jerome founded one for men on the opposite slope. He stayed for thirty-four years.

The Vulgate took him most of those years. Working from the Hebrew (against the conventional preference for the Greek Septuagint), revising the gospels against the Greek, polishing one book and then another, he produced — with Paula's literary criticism and her household's transcription — the Latin Bible that would be the only Bible the Western church knew for a thousand years, and the foundation of every translation that came after. The Bollandists take three full opening sections to defend him against his detractors. *Even the prophets and the apostles,* they note dryly, *spoke sharply when the case required.* Jerome's case very often required.

He died at his desk in his cell beside the Cave of the Nativity, an old man, perhaps in his early seventies, on a September evening around 420. The translation was finished. The temper, by then, had quieted. He was buried near Paula and Eustochium, who had gone before him.

> *"Ignoratio Scripturarum, ignoratio Christi est."* — Jerome, *Commentary on Isaiah,* prologue
