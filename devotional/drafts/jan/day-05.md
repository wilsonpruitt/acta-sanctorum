## January 5

# Edward the Confessor

*King of England, c. 1003–1066*

He was sixty when the kingdom finally came to him. His father Ethelred the Unready had died fleeing the Danes; his half-brother Alfred had been blinded and killed in the same flight; his mother Emma had spent his childhood remarrying his father's enemies. Edward had grown up in Normandy, in exile, and had said more than once that he would rather live a private life forever than recover the kingdom through the slaughter of men.

When the Danish line at last failed, the slaughter was not required. He was crowned, took to the throne with mild reluctance, and for twenty-four years ruled an England that William of Malmesbury could not stop calling — to his own faint astonishment — *quiet*. The civil wars stopped. The Danes stopped. The laws he restored were his grandfather Edgar's, recalled from sixty-eight years of disuse and reissued under his own name, with one provision freshly added: any man who lent at usury was to forfeit his goods and live as an exile. The English remembered the Laws of St. Edward longer than they remembered Edward himself.

He kept his marriage to Editha chaste, by mutual agreement; he founded the monastery at Westminster, where he would be buried and where his bones became the abbey's most-visited relic; he forgave easily; he gave widely. The chronicler delights in one small story: hunting near Windsor, a peasant once accidentally scared the deer off, and the king, roused, cried — *By God and His Mother, I will do you as much harm if I can!* — and that, says the biographer, was the worst threat Edward could imagine. *A noble spirit*, he writes, *which did not remember that it was king.*

The peace of his reign was a benediction the next century would not see. It rested, his eulogists thought, on the simple fact that the man who held the sword had never quite wanted to use it.

> *"I would rather live a private life forever than recover the kingdom through the slaughter of men."* — Edward, in exile
