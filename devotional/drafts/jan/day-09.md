## January 9

# Adrian of Canterbury

*Abbot, African scholar in England, c. 635–710*

He was African by birth — Libyan, the Bollandists guess — abbot of a small monastery near Naples and, in 667, the man Pope Vitalian first chose to send to Britain. The archbishopric of Canterbury had been vacant for three years; the new English Church needed a head. Adrian declined the office. He recommended instead a Greek monk named Theodore, then sixty-six years old, who was living in Rome. The pope agreed, on condition that Adrian travel with Theodore as his counselor.

They sailed in 668. Theodore was made archbishop; Adrian became abbot of the monastery of SS Peter and Paul at Canterbury, where Augustine of Canterbury was buried. The two foreigners — one Greek, one African, neither of whom had a word of English between them when they landed — set about the slow rebuilding of an Anglo-Saxon Church that had lost its bearings.

They opened a school. Students walked to Canterbury from every kingdom of the heptarchy. They were taught Latin, Greek, the Scriptures, computation, music, and — Bede notes with quiet pride — verse. Aldhelm of Sherborne was Adrian's pupil; through Aldhelm came Bede's generation, and through them the literacy of the entire Western Middle Ages. Bede, writing forty years later, says that some of Adrian's pupils were still alive in his own day and could converse in Greek and Latin as easily as in their mother tongue.

He outlived Theodore by twenty years and continued to teach until he could no longer rise from his bed. He had not wanted the journey; the kingdom of God had asked it of him anyway, and the kingdom had been the better for it.

> *"Never had there been such happy times in Britain since the English came to Britain."* — Bede, on the school of Theodore and Adrian
