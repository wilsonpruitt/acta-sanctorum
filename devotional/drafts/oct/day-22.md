## October 22

# Abercius of Hierapolis

*Bishop, traveller, author of his own epitaph, c. 120–c. 200*

He was bishop of Hierapolis in Phrygia in the second half of the second century — successor of Papias, who had been a disciple of John the Apostle, and writing before the persecution of Septimius Severus had begun. The acts the Bollandists print are full of the standard hagiographic apparatus — the demoniacs cured, the well that sprang up at his prayer, the trip to Rome to exorcise the daughter of Marcus Aurelius — and the editors handle the legendary material with patience. What survives the editorial knife, what no one disputes, is the epitaph he wrote for himself.

The *Inscription of Abercius* — first quoted in the late-fourth-century Greek life, then partly recovered in stone in 1883 by the Scottish archaeologist William Ramsay in the wall of a public bath at Hieropolis — is the oldest substantial Christian inscription that survives. It is twenty-two hexameter lines, in the *koine* Greek of the eastern Roman world. It was carved on the marble sarcophagus Abercius prepared for himself in his late seventies, on the eve of one of his pastoral journeys, and the slab now hangs in the Vatican Museum.

*A citizen of an elected city,* the epitaph begins, *I made this in my lifetime that there might be in plain view a place to receive my body. My name is Abercius, the disciple of a holy shepherd who feeds his flocks on the mountains and on the plains, who has great eyes that look down everywhere, who taught me the trustworthy writings.* The shepherd is Christ. The writings are the gospels. *He sent me to royal Rome to behold a queen with golden robe and golden sandals; and a people I saw there having a shining seal. I saw the plain of Syria and all the cities — Nisibis after I crossed the Euphrates. Everywhere I had brothers. Paul I had with me; faith led me everywhere; everywhere food was placed before me — the fish from the spring, mighty and pure, which the chaste virgin caught and gave to her friends to eat continually, having sweet wine and giving the cup with the bread.*

The fish from the spring is Christ. The chaste virgin is the Church. The cup with the bread is the Eucharist. The epitaph is, in a single block of second-century marble, a complete profession of Christian faith — Trinitarian, sacramental, ecclesial, eucharistic — set into the gravestone of a Phrygian bishop a hundred years before the persecutions ended and three hundred years before any of the great creeds were written. He died around the year 200, at perhaps seventy-five. The marble slab he had cut survived. What he had wanted plainly visible — *in plain view,* the epitaph says — has been visible ever since.
