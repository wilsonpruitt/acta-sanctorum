## May 14

# Carthach the Younger of Lismore

*Irish abbot, d. 637*

He was a Munster boy baptized Carthach, but known almost always by the pet-name his master, Bishop Carthach the elder, gave him in affection — Mochuda, *my Cuda* — and rendered Carthagus in the Latin form the chroniclers used. He had been a swineherd in his early youth on his father's lands, learning by ear the psalms a passing monk had taught him while he watched the pigs. He came to a monastic vocation at perhaps twenty. He was tonsured at Killeely and then went out, like many of the Irish abbots of his generation, looking for a place to build a community of his own.

He found it at Rahan in central Ireland, in the middle of the kingdom of Mide. He built a small wooden chapel and a beehive cluster of cells; men came; the place grew. Carthach governed Rahan for the better part of forty years and wrote there the Rule that still bears his name — a brief Old Irish text in verse, simpler than the Continental Rules, focused on the gentle balancing of prayer, work, and silence.

About 630, a political quarrel between rival Irish kingdoms turned against him. The new king of Mide wanted Rahan for his own bishop. He came in person with armed retainers and ordered Carthach to leave. Carthach, who would not call down the king's army on his own monks, took his community south. They walked the hundred miles from Rahan to the kingdom of the Déisi in Munster, a long, exhausting journey for a man already in his seventies. The local Déisi king Maelochtar gave them land on a sheltered ridge above the river Blackwater. They began building again.

The monastery they founded at Lismore became the great theological school of southern Ireland and lasted nine centuries. Carthach himself died on the ridge less than two years after the displacement, still unpacking, surrounded by the men he had brought south.

> *"Here I wish to end my life; from this place I will not depart, unless someone drag me away by force."* — Carthach, when the king ordered him from Rahan
