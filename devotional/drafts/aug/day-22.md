## August 22

# Sigfrid of Wearmouth

*Northumbrian abbot of Bede's house, d. 689*

The AASS preserves him through one witness above all: Bede the Venerable, who as a young monk lived under Sigfrid's brief abbacy at the twin monastery of Saint Peter at Wearmouth and Saint Paul at Jarrow, and who would later write the life of his masters. Bede knew the man personally. He calls him *sanctus Sigfridus*. Everything else comes from him.

Sigfrid had come to Wearmouth as a young man, probably from a Northumbrian noble household, drawn by the reputation of Benedict Biscop — the great traveling abbot whose five journeys to Rome had brought back the books, the chant-masters, the glass-workers, and the canonical observances that made Wearmouth the most learned monastic foundation in seventh-century England. Biscop ruled both houses with three successive coadjutor-abbots: Easterwine for four years, Sigfrid for three, then Ceolfrid, who would send the *Codex Amiatinus* to Rome.

Sigfrid took office at Wearmouth while Biscop was away on his last journey south. Bede, in the *Lives of the Abbots*, calls him *vir litteris Scripturarum sufficienter imbutus, moribus optimis ornatum, et mira abstinentiæ virtute præditum atque etiam patientiæ* — a man well-trained in the literature of the Scriptures, adorned with the best manners, and gifted with a marvelous virtue of abstinence and patience. The abstinence was the technical monastic term: he ate even less than the rule required, and not from ostentation, but from the long disposition of a man who had learned to do without.

The patience went into his last illness. He died slowly of what Bede calls *diuturnus pulmonum morbus* — a long disease of the lungs — that wasted him for nearly three years, exactly the years he was abbot. He kept the office through all of it. The brothers, who watched him at the daily Eucharist and at the night offices, learned more of monastic patience from his bearing of the disease than they had learned from the rule. He died on the twenty-second of August, 689, and was translated by Abbot Huwetberht in the next generation into the church beside Easterwine, in a single shrine. Bede himself was perhaps fifteen.

> *"Beautifully trained in the Scriptures, gifted with abstinence and patience: he taught us most when he could no longer speak."* — Bede, *Lives of the Abbots* (AASS Augusti IV)
