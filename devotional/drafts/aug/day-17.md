## August 17

# Liberatus and the Carthaginian Martyrs

*Seven monks under the Vandal Arian king Huneric, 483*

The AASS Augusti III names them in the order Ado kept: Liberatus the abbot, Bonifacius the deacon, Servus and Rusticus subdeacons, Rogatus and Septimus monks, and Maximus a boy. The witness is Victor of Vita, the African Catholic bishop who survived the Vandal persecution and wrote its history within the decade — the *Historia Persecutionis Africanae Provinciae*, which the Bollandists print as the *Passio* itself. The date is 483, the seventh year of Huneric, son of Geiseric, king of the Vandals of North Africa.

The Vandals were Arian. Geiseric had crossed from Spain into Africa in 429, had taken Carthage in 439, and had ruled the western Mediterranean from his African base for nearly fifty years. His son Huneric, who succeeded him in 477, hated the Catholics with focused intensity. Edict followed edict: the Catholic clergy were exiled in waves; the Catholic basilicas were turned over to the Arian Vandal clergy; the Catholic monasteries were watched. The territory of Capsa in southern Byzacena — modern Gafsa, in central Tunisia — held a Catholic monastic community whose name we have lost; we have only its abbot Liberatus and his six companions. They were arrested in 483 and brought up the coast under guard to Carthage.

They were given the standard last chance: rebaptism by Arian clergy, in the formula Huneric had imposed on the African church — that the Son was not consubstantial with the Father, but a lesser, made being. They refused. They were thrown in the cells of the city prison, weighted with iron chains, and kept in the dark. From the cells, Victor of Vita records, they continued to strengthen *die ac nocte* — day and night — the Catholic people of the city in their faith, calling out their teaching through the bars to whoever could hear.

Huneric ordered them executed publicly. A ship was loaded with bundles of dry wood. The seven were tied to the wood. The ship was rowed out into the bay of Carthage and the fire was lit. The flames, Victor's narrative says, were extinguished by divine command in front of the watching crowd on the shore — *statim imperio divino, videntibus cunctis, exstinctus est*. The fire was rekindled; the flames went out again. The king, furious and ashamed, sent oarsmen aboard with the long oar-paddles. The seven were beaten to death with the oars, *cerebris comminutis* — their skulls broken — and the bodies thrown into the sea. By the same hour of the same day, the bodies were washed ashore intact. The Carthaginian Catholics carried them in procession, with hymns and the clergy walking before, to a monastery beside the basilica of Saint Celerina, and buried them there together. Huneric died within months.

> *"He who lit the fire could not make it burn; he who broke the skulls could not break the witness."* — Victor of Vita, on the seven martyrs of Carthage
