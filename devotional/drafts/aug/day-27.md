## August 27

# Caesarius of Arles

*Provençal bishop, preacher, drafter of the Council of Orange, 470–542*

He was born about 470 at Chalon-sur-Saône in Burgundy, in a Gallo-Roman family of senatorial rank already deeply Christian. He was tonsured at the cathedral school at eighteen, lectured for two years, then walked south to the great monastic island of Lérins off the coast of Provence — already a generation old in his day, the seedbed of half the Provençal episcopate. He spent seven years there. The abbot Porcarius made him cellarer; he distributed the abbey's stores so freely that the brothers complained, and the abbot defended him: *He has given more than the rule allows; but only to those whom the rule has failed.* He fell ill from over-fasting and was sent to Arles for medical care.

He was made priest of Arles by Archbishop Aeonius, who was a kinsman. When Aeonius died in 502, Caesarius — perhaps reluctantly — was elected to succeed him. He was thirty-two. He governed Arles forty years, the longest archiepiscopal reign of any Gaulish bishop of the late-antique west.

The pontificate is remembered for three things. First, preaching: nearly two hundred and fifty of his sermons survive — the largest body of Latin homiletic preaching to come down from the fifth or sixth century in Gaul — in a Latin Caesarius worked hard to keep simple, pastoral, accessible to working farmers. He cut the Mass in half so the catechumens would not leave before the homily. He had the choir sing the antiphons in Latin verses simple enough for the congregation to follow. He preached without notes, every Sunday and every feast. Second, the monasteries. He founded the first women's monastery in the West to follow a written rule of its own — *Regula ad Virgines* — and put his sister Caesaria over it at Arles. The rule survives. It is strict: enclosed life, manual work, the recitation of all 150 psalms each week, complete community of goods. The house grew to two hundred nuns in his lifetime. Third, the Council of Orange of 529, which settled the long Semi-Pelagian controversy by drafting twenty-five canons that affirmed Augustine's doctrine without the predestinarian extensions: that grace precedes and enables every motion of the will toward God, but that the will, once moved, freely cooperates. The Pope confirmed the canons. The council became, for the next millennium of Latin theology, the standard mediating settlement on grace.

He died at Arles on the twenty-seventh of August, 542, the morning after his last consecration of a deacon. The Vita's closing lines preserve the death-bed instruction he gave his community: *No image of me anywhere; my poor habit is to be given to the next pauper at the gate; my books are to be kept where they have been kept.*

> *"The voice that fits the country pulpit is not the voice of the schools; preach what they can carry home in their basket."* — Caesarius, to his preachers, AASS Augusti VI
