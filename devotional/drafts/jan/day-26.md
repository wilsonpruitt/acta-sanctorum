## January 26

# Paula

*Roman matron, foundress at Bethlehem, 347–404*

She was one of the great families of Rome — Scipios, Aemilii Pauli; her bloodlines went back to the Republic — and she married into another, the Toxotii. She bore five children. She was thirty-two when her husband died. The next two years were the wreckage of an aristocratic widow's grief: her circle in Rome included the most stylish Christian matrons in the city, but none of them could console her, and she half-broke for those two years.

Jerome arrived in Rome in 382 as secretary to Pope Damasus, with a working knowledge of Hebrew and an irritating habit of speaking the truth to senators. Paula met him at the house of her relative Marcella and never looked away. When Jerome's three years in Rome ended — controversially — she sold the great estates, packed her younger daughter Eustochium, and followed him east. By 386 they were both in Bethlehem. She had founded two monasteries — one for women, one for men — with her own money. She learned Hebrew well enough to read the Psalter in it. She paid the copyists who produced the manuscripts on which Jerome built the Vulgate. She corrected him when his Latin lapsed.

She died at fifty-six. Jerome, whose grief at her grave was, by his own account, dangerous to his faith, wrote her epitaph. He also wrote a long letter to Eustochium — Epistle 108 — in which he tried to record the life he had known. The letter is one of the great pieces of late ancient prose. It exists because she gave him forty years of the Latin Bible, and he could think of nothing else to give her in return.

> *"All her glory was within."* — Jerome, *Letter 108* on Paula
