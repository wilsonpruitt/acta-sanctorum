## October 9

# Denis of Paris and Companions

*First bishop of Paris, with Rusticus and Eleutherius, c. 250–c. 286*

He had been sent into Gaul, as Gregory of Tours preserves it, with six other bishops in the time of Pope Fabian — around 250 — to plant the church in the chief cities of the Roman north: Saturninus to Toulouse, Trophimus to Arles, Martial to Limoges, Gatian to Tours, Austremoine to Clermont, and Dionysius to Lutetia, the small island-city on the Seine that the Romans had begun to call Paris. He took with him a presbyter named Rusticus and a deacon named Eleutherius. They built the first chapel of the city on the right bank and began to baptize.

The persecution that caught them came perhaps thirty years later, under Valerian or under Maximian — the Bollandists conclude that the early 280s is most likely. The three were arrested together, brought before the prefect, scourged, racked, and finally beheaded on the high northern hill above the city — *Mons Mercurii,* the Latin called it, *Montmartre,* the hill of the martyrs, after them. The cephalophore legend that grew up around the death-scene — Denis bending down to pick up his own severed head and walking with it three Roman miles north to the place where he wanted to be buried, preaching as he went — first turns up in the ninth-century life by Hilduin of Saint-Denis, and the Bollandists handle it with patient skepticism. What they accept as older and more secure is the burial site itself: a little Christian cemetery in the Frankish villa of *Catulliacum,* north of Paris, where the bodies of the three were laid quietly in a wooden coffin, and where the chapel that grew over them eventually became, four hundred years later under Dagobert and again under Suger, the great basilica of Saint-Denis, the mausoleum of the kings of France.

The older *Passio* treats the three as the historical figures they were: a missionary bishop, his presbyter, and his deacon, who walked into a Roman city of strangers, established a chapel, baptized, were arrested, and died together on the same morning on the same hill. Their fellow-feeling was preserved in the burial: the three bodies in one trench, *unico tumulo* — *in a single grave.*

The kings of France would crown themselves in their basilica for a thousand years. The chapel on the right bank of the Seine became Notre-Dame. The mission three men had begun — bishop, presbyter, deacon, walking into a city that was not asking for them — outlived them by every measure they would have known how to count.

> *"Unico tumulo."* — *the older* Passio, *on the burial of Denis, Rusticus, and Eleutherius*
