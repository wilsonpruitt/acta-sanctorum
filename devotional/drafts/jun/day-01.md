## June 1

# Justin Martyr

*Philosopher and apologist, c. 100–165*

He was born at Flavia Neapolis, the Roman colony on the site of ancient Shechem in Samaria, of pagan Greek parents. He took up philosophy as a boy — first the Stoics, then a Peripatetic who, the *Dialogue with Trypho* recalls, dismissed him as soon as it became clear he could not pay the fees; then a Pythagorean who required preliminary studies in music, astronomy, and geometry; and at last the Platonists, in whose contemplation of the incorporeal he believed he had found peace. He walked one afternoon along the shore at Caesarea, alone, trying to think. An old man met him there and asked him a question he could not answer: how the soul knew God. The old man told him to read the Hebrew prophets. He did. He found, he says, the only philosophy at once safe and profitable.

He kept his philosopher's *pallium* — the traveling cloak of the schools — for the rest of his life. He opened a Christian school of philosophy in Rome above the baths of a man named Martinus, on the road to the Forum, and taught there for years. He composed two *Apologies* addressed to the emperors Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius — careful, courteous, learned, arguing that the philosophers of the Greeks had partly known the *Logos* that had now been made flesh; that Christians were not atheists or seditionists but the truest citizens; that the rites of the Eucharist were ancient and reasonable and should be examined, not suppressed. He wrote the *Dialogue with Trypho*, the first long Christian-Jewish exchange to survive.

He was denounced about 165 by a rival philosopher named Crescens, brought before the prefect Rusticus with six of his students, and questioned. The transcript of that hearing — preserved in his *Acts* — is one of the earliest court records of any kind from the Roman world. *Rusticus said: Do you suppose that you will ascend into heaven? Justin answered: I do not suppose; I know.* They were scourged and beheaded together. He had spent forty years arguing that Christ was the *Logos* of every honest philosopher. He was the first philosopher to die for the argument.

> *"I do not suppose; I know."* — Justin, before the prefect Rusticus
