## March 12

# Gregory the Great

*Pope, doctor, 540–604*

He was the son of a senator and the great-grandson of a pope, born into the kind of Roman family that, in the sixth century, was old enough to have memories no one else had. By thirty-three he was prefect of the city — the senior civil magistrate of Rome — and in the next year of his life he resigned the office, sold the family estates, founded seven monasteries from the proceeds, kept one of them as his own, and put on a monk's habit. He had wanted, his biographer says, to be left alone with God for the rest of his life. The Church would not allow it.

He was sent as papal envoy to Constantinople. He was made deacon. He was elected, at fifty, to a papacy he tried hard to refuse. Rome at his accession was visibly dying: the Tiber had flooded, the plague was killing thousands, the Lombard armies were at the walls, the imperial bureaucracy in Ravenna no longer answered letters. Gregory took the city in hand. He organized the grain dole. He led the penitential procession across the city during the plague, and saw — or the Romans saw, watching him — an angel above the Castel Sant'Angelo sheathing a sword. He negotiated with the Lombards. He invented the title *servus servorum Dei*, servant of the servants of God, to describe what he thought a pope was.

He sent a band of monks from his own monastery on the Caelian Hill, under one named Augustine, to evangelize the English. He wrote the *Pastoral Rule*, which the medieval Church would use to teach bishops for a thousand years; he wrote the *Moralia in Job* and the *Dialogues* on the saints of Italy; he wrote eight hundred and fifty letters that survive. He died at sixty-four, in the same monk's habit he had put on at thirty-four.
