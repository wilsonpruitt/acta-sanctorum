## February 28

# Proterius of Alexandria

*Patriarch and martyr, d. 457*

He was a priest of the Alexandrian Church, the senior presbyter under Dioscorus, when the Council of Chalcedon in 451 deposed Dioscorus for refusing the Chalcedonian definition of Christ as one Person in two natures. Alexandria had been, for generations, the most theologically distinctive see in the East — Cyril's see, Athanasius's see — and the Egyptian clergy and laity were overwhelmingly committed to a single-nature Christology of the Lord. They had not wanted Chalcedon. They had not wanted Dioscorus deposed. The imperial party put Proterius in his place over the protests of the city.

He governed for six years under siege. The Monophysite faction — the majority — refused him communion, refused to recognize his authority, rioted when he attempted to install priests in the city's churches. He held the see by imperial military protection. He was, by all reports, a learned and humble man trying to do an impossible job; he insisted on the Chalcedonian definition because he believed it true, and he refused to leave the see because he believed it his.

When the emperor Marcian — who had backed him — died in January 457, the Alexandrian Monophysites took the death as the moment to act. On Maundy Thursday, during the evening Eucharist in the cathedral, a mob broke through the doors and forced their way to the sanctuary. They killed Proterius at the altar. They dragged his body through the streets of Alexandria. They burned what remained in the public square.

The Egyptian Church, after his death, fell completely out of communion with Constantinople and Rome and remained so. The split that began with the rioting against Chalcedon has not entirely healed in our day.

Proterius did not choose his end; he chose the doctrine, and the end came with the choosing. He is the rare patriarch whom both the Chalcedonian and the non-Chalcedonian sides remember — one as a martyr, the other as a memory still painful to touch.

> *"I have not held this office for the office's sake."* — Proterius, in his final Easter homily
