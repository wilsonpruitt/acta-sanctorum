## March 5

# Gerasimus of the Jordan

*Abbot, fifth century*

He was a Lycian, born on the southern coast of Asia Minor, and he came east as a young man to the Egyptian desert and then to the wilderness east of the Jordan, near Jericho, where the river opens out into the long dry valley before the Dead Sea. The hermit caves of that country were filling up by the 450s with monks who had come from every part of the empire; Gerasimus, sober and gentle, was made superior of a great *laura* — a settlement of separate cells around a common church — and governed it for the last decade of his life.

He had, like many monks of his generation, leaned briefly toward the Monophysite position; the elderly Euthymius the Great, his neighbor in the desert, talked him out of it. He spent the rest of his life — as if making up for the lapse — defending the Chalcedonian definition of Christ as one Person in two natures.

The story by which he is remembered involves a lion. He met it on the road, lame from a thorn in its paw; he drew the thorn out and bound the wound, and the lion, instead of leaving, followed him home and took up residence at the gates of the laura. The community fed it. The lion learned to carry water-jars on its back. When one day a passing trader stole the laura's donkey, the lion was blamed and exiled in disgrace; months later the donkey was found and the lion's name was cleared. When Gerasimus died, the lion came to the grave, the chronicler John Moschus says, and would not leave, and would not eat, and died there.

It is not the story they told about him in his own life. It is the story they began to tell once they had buried him. *He was the kind of man*, they were saying, *whom even animals could read.*

> *"He drew the thorn out, and the lion would not leave him."* — John Moschus, *Spiritual Meadow* 107
